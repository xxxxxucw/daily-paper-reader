# Each Insufficient in Its Own Way: Calibrating Language Models Across Varying Evidence States 

Jiayi Li ⋆ Dennis L. Shung ⋄ Bradly C. Stadie ⋆⋆Northwestern University ⋄Mayo Clinic 

Abstract 

How can a language model know whether it has enough evidence to answer? Language models tend to answer even when the evidence they have is insufficient. Deciding whether to an-swer or gather more evidence requires a cali-brated estimate of how likely an answer is to be correct with the available evidence. However, most calibration work treats the input as fixed, without asking how this estimate should change with the evidence. We introduce evidence-state calibration , which requires a model’s confi-dence before answering to match its probabil-ity of answering correctly and to change ac-cordingly whenever the evidence changes this probability. We propose SuffiCE ( Suffi ciency 

Confidence Estimation), which fine-tunes the confidence already present in a model’s next-token distribution to match how often the model answers correctly from each evidence state. Across clinical diagnosis and multi-hop ques-tion answering with four models from two fam-ilies, SuffiCE reduces the Brier score by up to 60% and increases AUROC by up to 0.16. Its confidence matches the empirical success rate and responds not only to the amount of evidence but also to which evidence is present, showing that models can learn to judge whether their current evidence is enough to answer. 

1 Introduction 

Before answering a question, one must judge whether the information at hand is enough to an-swer it. In high-stakes settings, a physician who di-agnoses before gathering enough information may miss the real disease. For example, a patient who has been tired for two months may seem iron de-ficient, but a careful clinician would order more blood tests, which may reveal celiac disease, an autoimmune cause of poor iron absorption. Language models facing insufficient informa-tion, however, often reach confident but incorrect conclusions (Kirichenko et al., 2025; Yin et al.,

2023). A common remedy for such overconfidence is to abstain below a confidence threshold (Wen et al., 2025). This works only if the model is cali-brated, so that answers given x% confidence are cor-rect about x% of the time (Guo et al., 2017; Geng et al., 2024). To achieve this, existing methods train language models to provide calibrated con-fidence estimates for generated answers (Kapoor et al., 2024; Bani-Harouni et al., 2026b). However, most calibration work holds the ev-idence fixed for each question, so it can check whether confidence is calibrated but not whether it varies correctly as a function of the evidence. In interactive settings, the available information evolves as models ask clarifying questions (Zhang and Choi, 2025) or seek additional information (Li et al., 2024). At each turn, the model must decide whether to answer or keep gathering evidence. To make this decision, a model needs a confi-dence estimated from the available evidence alone, before it produces any answer. We call the evidence available to a model at a given moment its evidence state. We then define evidence-state calibration 

1arXiv:2610.33237v1 [cs.CL] 23 Oct 2026 as requiring the confidence before answering to match the probability of answering correctly, so that whenever the evidence changes this probabil-ity, the confidence changes by the same amount (Figure 1). Testing this requires observing the same prob-lem under different evidence states. Rather than contrasting the extremes of complete and missing information (Kirichenko et al., 2025) or follow-ing a single fixed sequence of accumulating evi-dence (Zhang et al., 2026), we construct granular states for each problem, spanning minimal to com-plete evidence and including different combina-tions of information. Granularity matters because sufficient evidence states are alike, but each insuf-ficient one is insufficient in its own way. 1 Some insufficient states can still be answered from what the model already knows (Joren et al., 2025), while others lack the one piece of evidence the answer depends on. We therefore measure each state’s sufficiency by how often the model answers cor-rectly from it, not by whether the required facts are present, and teach the model to anticipate this success rate before answering. With these success rates as targets, we propose SuffiCE ( Suffi ciency Confidence Estimation), to our knowledge the first method that trains a model’s confidence before answering to be evidence-state calibrated. It reads the confidence directly from the model’s own next-token distribution. The con-fidence is the model’s probability of choosing to answer instead of asking for more information. No additional classifier or generated number is needed. This avoids an ambiguity in verbalized confidence. When a model states that it is 80% confident, it is unclear whether to trust the stated number or the probability the model assigned to generating it. We instead train the model’s own probability directly to match the success rate across our diverse evi-dence states. The result is a model that can tell, before answering, whether what it currently knows is enough to answer correctly. We evaluate on two clinical diagnosis datasets, MIMIC (Hager et al., 2024) and MedQA (Jin et al., 2021), and the multi-hop question answer-ing dataset MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022). Across three Qwen models from 4B to 27B parameters and Kimi K2.6, SuffiCE improves calibration for every model and dataset, reducing the Brier score

2 Related Work 

Deciding whether to answer or gather more evi-dence requires a calibrated judgment of whether the current evidence suffices. This connects three lines of work: interactive information seeking ex-plores how models acquire evidence, information sufficiency and unanswerability examine whether an input supports an answer, and confidence cali-bration studies whether confidence reflects actual accuracy. 

Interactive information seeking. In an interac-tive setting, a model can gather more informa-tion before answering by asking clarifying ques-tions (Zhang and Choi, 2025; Li et al., 2024), re-trieving documents (Jiang et al., 2023; Asai et al., 2024), or ordering diagnostic tests and searching for medical evidence (Schmidgall et al., 2026; Dang et al., 2026). Deciding when enough has been gathered typically relies on a confidence or uncertainty signal (Jiang et al., 2023; Zhang and Choi, 2025; Li et al., 2024), and MediQ shows that improving confidence estimation can enhance interactive diagnostic accuracy (Li et al., 2024). Our approach aims to provide such a signal, es-timating before answering how likely the current evidence is to yield a correct answer. Closest to our work, LA-CDM trains multi-turn diagnosis with a verbalized confidence that guides whether to re-quest another test, but this confidence evaluates a hypothesis the model has already generated (Bani-Harouni et al., 2026a). We instead estimate success before generating an answer and train this estimate across controlled evidence states of the same prob-lem, capturing how changes in evidence affect the likelihood of success. 

Information sufficiency and unanswerability. 

Whether an input contains enough information to answer a question has been widely studied through unanswerability (Rajpurkar et al., 2018; Yin et al., 2023). Benchmarks test whether models ab-stain when information is insufficient (Kirichenko 2et al., 2025), including settings where key evi-dence is removed from otherwise answerable ques-tions (Trivedi et al., 2022; Machcha et al., 2026). Detection methods assess sufficiency using model hidden states (Slobodkin et al., 2023; Lavi et al., 2026) or by identifying missing information (Jain and Garimella, 2026), and training methods teach models to abstain when information is insuffi-cient (Zhai et al., 2026). A shared limitation is that sufficiency is a binary label attached to the input, so a partially informative input counts as either suf-ficient or not, regardless of which model answers it. Joren et al. (2025) take a first step, showing that models often answer correctly from insufficient context. Whether evidence is enough depends on the model answering, so we define sufficiency by the model’s success, how often it answers correctly from each controlled evidence state. 

Confidence calibration. A model is calibrated when its confidence matches its accuracy (Guo et al., 2017). Training-free estimates based on ver-balized judgments (Tian et al., 2023; Xiong et al., 2024), sampled-answer consistency (Kuhn et al., 2023), or token probabilities (Kadavath et al., 2022) can be informative, but calibration varies across tasks (Vashurin et al., 2025). Trained methods es-timate answer correctness (Kapoor et al., 2024), verbalize calibrated confidence (Lin et al., 2022; Bani-Harouni et al., 2026b; Damani et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025), or predict success before answer-ing (Kadavath et al., 2022; Lugoloobi et al., 2026). However, they do not explicitly train confidence to track success across changing evidence states. Zhang et al. (2026) evaluate confidence as hints accumulate in a fixed sequence but do not train on these changing states. We instead train and eval-uate confidence across controlled evidence states, varying both the amount and type of evidence to track changes in empirical success (Appendix E). 

3 Method 

Our method, SuffiCE, has three stages. We first construct multiple evidence states for each problem (§3.2), then estimate a fixed answering policy’s success rate on each state from repeated answers (§3.3), and finally train an estimator to predict this rate from the state alone, before answering (§3.4).

3.2 Evidence States 

For each problem i, we construct mi evidence states xi,s = ( qi, I i,s ), s = 1 , . . . , m i, each pairing the question with a subset Ii,s ⊆ Ei of its evidence. We write x for a generic state when the indices are not needed. All states of a problem share the question and gold answer and differ only in the evidence shown, so differences in success between them are attributable to the evidence. The model is never told which state it is in and sees only the question and the evidence, so it must judge from their content whether the evidence is enough. Train-ing on the same problem under different evidence teaches it which evidence makes a correct answer reachable. Evidence states vary in both the amount and the composition of evidence. Since success depends on which evidence is present, not on the order it was gathered, states can combine evidence freely, including states that hold the amount fixed and vary the composition (§5.3). The states are specific to each dataset (§4).

4 Experimental Setup 

Datasets. We evaluate on two clinical diagno-sis datasets and one multi-hop question answer-ing dataset, which range from mostly answered correctly to mostly not (details in Appendix A.1). MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2022) composes multi-hop questions over Wikipedia from single-hop questions, each answered by one supporting paragraph, and the model gives a short answer. Metrics. Let ci,s = cθ(xi,s ) be the sufficiency confidence of state s of problem i and ˆpi,s its em-pirical success rate. The Brier score, the mean of (ci,s − ˆpi,s )2 over the test set, measures over-all error. AUROC , averaged over the K sampled answers, measures how well confidence ranks cor-rect answers above incorrect ones. Both pool the whole test set, so they reward knowing which prob-lems are hard. The change correlation ρ∆ =Corr( ci,t −ci,s , ˆpi,t − ˆpi,s ), computed over all pairs of states of the same problem from a state s to a state t with more evidence (Appendix C.4), instead tests whether confidence follows success as the evidence state changes, as criterion 2 requires.

5.3 Evidence-State Analysis Confidence rises with the decisive evidence, not with more evidence. SuffiCE closely tracks the empirical success rate across states, rising only when new evidence makes the question answerable, whereas the base logit rises with every paragraph added (Figure 6, more measures in Appendix C.4). For both question types, success stays near zero until the final hop e3 is shown. On chain questions, the base logit treats each earlier hop as progress and is confident enough to answer given {e1, e 2}

(0.71), although the model almost never succeeds (0.06), while SuffiCE stays low (0.16) until e3 ar-rives. On branching questions, the base logit rises with each branch, while SuffiCE stays low for ei-ther branch alone and for both, since neither helps without e3. SuffiCE is never told the question type or which paragraph fills which hop, so it judges from the content alone whether the reasoning chain is complete. 6 Conclusion 

To our knowledge, this is the first work to train a model’s confidence before answering to be cali-brated across varying evidence states. The decision to answer or gather more evidence is made before any answer exists, so a model is only as reliable as this judgment. We formalized it as evidence-state calibration , requiring confidence to be calibrated and to change as much as the chance of success whenever the evidence changes, which calibration alone does not guarantee. SuffiCE outperforms every baseline, and its confidence rises with the de-cisive evidence, not with every piece added. Such confidence is a step toward language models that can judge, in high-stakes settings, when the evi-dence at hand is enough to answer. 8Limitations 

SuffiCE shows strong calibration and evidence sen-sitivity across four answering models and three datasets, but it has three limitations. First, we pre-construct evidence states rather than gather evi-dence in live multi-turn interactions, a controlled setting that lets us attribute differences in success and confidence to the evidence itself. Second, to limit cost, we estimate each target from ten sam-pled answers per state, and more samples would give more precise targets. Third, SuffiCE has not been tested in real clinical scenarios, and its confi-dence should not replace clinical judgment.

13 A.2 Answering Policies 

The main experiments study self-calibration. Each model in Table 1 serves as both the answering pol-icy and the estimator, so it estimates its own chance of success, and every method for that model is compared against the exact same targets. 

Targets for self-calibration. For each model, the frozen model with thinking disabled samples 

K = 10 answers per evidence state, and the frac-tion graded correct is the success rate of that state. These success rates are the only targets used for that model. The base logit reads P (A | x) from this model before training, and verbalized confidence prompts this model for the probability that it could answer correctly. Platt scaling fits its two param-eters to this model’s success rates on the training set, and SuffiCE trains a LoRA adapter on a copy of this model to predict the same success rates. All four are evaluated against the success rates of this model on the test set. Methods are therefore com-pared only for the same model, since each model has its own targets. The cross-policy experiment of §5.4 is the only exception, where Qwen estimators are trained on and evaluated against the success rates of Kimi K2.6. 

Difficulty. Success rates differ across answering models, little on MIMIC but widely on MedQA and MuSiQue (Table 4), so the same evidence state has a different target for each answering policy. 

Precision of the targets. Each target is estimated from K = 10 answers, so even a perfect estimator that outputs the true success rate p∗ has a nonzero Brier score against it. This noise floor is the ex-pected binomial sampling variance p∗(1 − p∗)/K ,which we estimate without bias by ˆp(1 − ˆp)/(K−1) 

averaged over the test set. On every dataset, the Brier score of SuffiCE is over 13 times this floor (Table 6). Sampling noise therefore accounts for only a small part of any method’s error, and ten an-swers per state are enough to compare estimators.

E Comparison with Related Work 

We compare our setting with the closest prior work on confidence estimation along five properties (Ta-ble 12). • Pre-answer : the confidence is produced be-fore any answer is generated, so it can decide whether to answer or to gather more evidence. • Trained : the confidence estimator is trained, rather than read from an untrained model or elicited by prompting. • Controlled evidence subsets : the same prob-lem is observed with different combinations of evidence, not only with evidence that accu-mulates in a fixed order. • Within-problem evaluation : the evaluation tests whether changes in confidence follow changes in success across states of the same problem. • Own token probability : the confidence is the model’s next-token probability, with no added classifier head or verbalized number. P(IK) (Kadavath et al., 2022) trains an added value head to predict, from the question alone, whether the model will answer correctly. It adds source doc-uments and hints only at evaluation and does not relate the change in P(IK) to the change in accuracy. Pre-generation probes (Lugoloobi et al., 2026) train linear probes on the model’s activations before generation to predict success, with each problem shown in a single fixed prompt. Calibration tun-ing (Kapoor et al., 2024) fine-tunes the model to judge whether a proposed answer is correct, read from its token probability. Rewarding Doubt (Bani-Harouni et al., 2026b) uses reinforcement learning to train the model to state a confidence from 0 to 10 in an answer it has already generated, with each question asked without context. LA-CDM (Bani-Harouni et al., 2026a) trains a stated confidence in a diagnosis the agent has already proposed, and the evidence is limited to the tests its agent chose to request. P(Sufficient) (Zhang et al., 2026) prompts the model for the probability that the information so far entails its answer. Its clues arrive in a fixed order, and its evaluation measures per-turn calibra-tion and whether confidence rises over turns. Each prior method shares some of these proper-ties, and ours is the only one that has all five. No prior method trains the model’s own token probabil-ity before an answer exists. P(IK) (Kadavath et al., 2022) and pre-generation probes (Lugoloobi et al., 2026) estimate success before answering but read it from an added head or probe, while calibration tun-ing (Kapoor et al., 2024) and P(Sufficient) (Zhang et al., 2026) use the model’s token probability but score an answer it has already given. SuffiCE in-stead trains the model’s own probability of choos-ing to answer, so the same next-token probability that would decide whether to answer or gather more evidence is calibrated, with no extra head and no an-swer to generate first. We choose this formulation because the model’s next-token distribution already assigns a probability to answering versus seeking more evidence, so no classifier head is added and the confidence is the model’s own preference be-tween the two actions. A full-only estimator with the same formulation falls short of SuffiCE (§5.2), so the gains come from training across evidence states. Two further properties set SuffiCE apart, and no prior method has either. Training on con-trolled evidence subsets of the same problem lets the estimator learn which evidence decides success, rather than only which problems are hard (§5.2). Evaluating within each problem tests this directly, showing that confidence rises with the decisive evi-dence and not with every piece added (§5.3). 23 F Case Studies 

F.1 MuSiQue Case Studies 

We examine two branching (3hop2) questions from the MuSiQue test set in detail (Tables 13 and 14). In both, the branches e1 and e2 answer two inde-pendent sub-questions, and only the final hop e3

combines their answers into the requested date. 

Case 1: When the Republicans took control of the Senate. The branches establish that Alaska supports the Republicans ( e1) and that the Senate sets its own rules ( e2), but only e3 gives the date on which the Republicans regained the Senate. With both branches shown, no sampled answer is correct. The base logit nonetheless jumps from at most 0.02 to 0.50, treating the two branches as progress de-spite the lack of sampled success. SuffiCE stays at or below 0.02, matching this low success rate. Once e3 is shown, 20% of sampled answers are cor-rect. SuffiCE rises to 0.19, closely matching this rate, while the base logit falls to 0.11 and becomes underconfident. 

Case 2: When the Dutch reached New Zealand. 

The branches establish that The Lord of the Rings was filmed in New Zealand ( e1) and that the Dutch first brought enslaved people to New York ( e2), but only e3 gives the date on which the Dutch naviga-tor Abel Tasman reached New Zealand. Again no sampled answer is correct with both branches, yet the base logit climbs with each branch to 0.98. Suf-fiCE reaches only 0.16. With full evidence, 30% of sampled answers are correct. SuffiCE gives 0.34, again close to this rate, while the base logit stays at 0.96 and is far overconfident. 

Comparing the two full states. The two ques-tions share the same structure and both show all three supporting paragraphs, yet they differ in how often they are answered correctly. SuffiCE follows this difference, matching each success rate within 0.04, whereas the base logit misses in opposite di-rections. The confidence of SuffiCE is not fixed by the evidence being complete, so it judges not only whether the reasoning chain is complete but also how likely the answering model is to succeed once it is.
