# ABSTRACT 

Learning high-level causal variables from low-level observations is a central prob-lem for artificial intelligence (Schölkopf et al., 2021). With known node semantics but an unknown graph, visual readout errors can misalign model settings with envi-ronment intervention values. We address this mismatch by pairing image readouts with verified values attained through fixed round-robin interventions. With en-coder and decoder frozen, a dimension-wise invertible calibration map puts visual responses and target settings in shared numerical units. After freezing this map, the agent selects interventions to resolve graph directions and fits local mecha-nisms to predict consequences; inverse calibration returns counterfactual states to the decoder’s original coordinates. Across Domino, Pick-and-Place, Balance, Gears, CAUSAL3D, and CausalWorld, we evaluate graph discovery, intervention consequences, and counterfactual images. Controlled domino experiments show that interventions resolve orientation ambiguity left by equal additional observa-tions. In a matched-data domino control, calibration reduces the mean error of predicted changes in other variables within the same factual context, relative to an uncalibrated model. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Can an agent predict intervention consequences from an image? Beyond reconstructing the domino scene in Figure 1, intervention queries require a target, its new value, and resulting re-sponses (Schölkopf et al., 2021)—a distinction relevant to physical-system counterfactuals (Li et al., 2020) and robotic transfer under environmental changes (Ahmed et al., 2021). Our goal is to query pixel-derived variables in environment intervention units. Differently directed graphs can share conditional independences, forming a Markov equivalence class (MEC). Interven-tions can distinguish candidates, but unique recovery requires sufficient coverage and identification conditions (Section 3) (Hauser & Bühlmann, 2012). Label-aligned readouts can still have scale, offset, or nonlinear errors that corrupt queries despite correct graph directions. A verified intervention value is the attained target state; queries assume a valid setting without modeling actuator execution.

# 4 METHOD

Freeze the encoder/decoder, then update only dimension-wise T using verified values from fixed round-robin acquisition. Second, recover prop-agation structure. Freeze T , estimate an observational candidate skeleton, and orient edges using responses to actively targeted environment interventions. Third, predict states and images. Fit local mechanisms on a valid, sufficiently oriented graph for population interventional means and factual-image counterfactuals; inverse calibration returns predictions to decoder coordinates. # 5 EXPERIMENTS 

Our evidence follows three links: interventions resolve ambiguity left by matched additional obser-vations; same-data calibration lowers mean intervention-effect error in the controlled domino cohort; state and image queries test downstream use. System comparisons assess task performance, not indi-vidual components. Decoder-only controls separate rendering gains from calibration (protocol and statistical index: Appendix A.1). 5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Benchmarks. Tasks comprise Domino, Pick-and-Place (PnP), Balance, Gears, CAUSAL3D Spring and Seesaw (Liu et al., 2025), and CausalWorld (Ahmed et al., 2021). Domino separately in-cludes a seven-node continuous SCM and four-node contact dynamics. The latter uses binary events 

Fi for whether each domino falls and evaluates fixed/release commands through same-initial-state replay. PnP, Balance, and Gears contain six, five, and six SCM variables. Figure 4 illustrates scenes; Domino configurations and task-table scope appear in Appendices A.9 and A.3. 

Baselines. System comparisons include CausalVAE (Yang et al., 2021) and ILCM, which learns from intervention-paired images (Brehmer et al., 2022). Each uses its own representation, graph extraction, latent intervention, and decoder. ICM-VAE is a given-graph generation reference (Ko-manduri et al., 2024); no-graph controls remove dependencies through their cohort-specific operators (Appendix B.2). Appendix G.2 reports ID quality and model selection. Weak baseline results are not attributed to an absence of intervention data. 

Metrics. Graph discovery reports structural Hamming distance (SHD) ↓, counting a reversed edge once, and area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) ↑. Consequence prediction reports training-scale-normalized mean squared error (NMSE) ↓ for non-target mean states. Counterfactual images use RGB mean squared error (MSE) ↓. For counterfactual images, models receive only a factual im-age and command; ground truth (GT) replays the same exogenous context or physical initial state. Population prediction instead takes only a command. Errors in the latter two task tables are mul-tiplied by 10 3. Completion, graph recovery, and executable queries have separate denominators (Appendix A.6). 

Comparison scope. Our method uses learned T in the three task tables. Appendix A.2 distin-guishes unified comparisons, given-graph references, and physical ILCM with different permissions. These descriptive summaries measure conditional error on executed queries, not end-to-end success or superiority on a common query population. Their protocol coverage is specified in Appendix A.3; the matched-data and matched-acquisition controls, rather than these aggregate gaps, support compo-nent attribution. Continuous-task columns retain reported ID/amplitude-shift labels; other columns compare test sets A/B (Appendix A.8). Light blue marks our method, bold marks the best point esti-mate, and “–” means not applicable. # 6 CONCLUSION 

With known node semantics, verified intervention values calibrate visual readouts so that node set-tings, causal mechanisms, and decoding use consistent intervention units. Matched controls support improvements for some intervention queries, but the relative benefits of affine and nonlinear calibra-tion depend on the setting; better readouts do not automatically improve downstream predictions. Environment interventions provide direction evidence absent from additional observations. This finding is distinct from targeting efficiency and gains in image reconstruction. 10 Preprint

A.4 CAUSAL TERMINOLOGY In G = ( V, E ), edge i → j denotes direct dependence in j’s structural equation; E is distinct from encoder Eθ . A DAG is acyclic. Parents are Pa G(j) = {i : i → j ∈ E}; descendants De G(i) are nodes reachable from i, excluding i itself. An effect along 1 → 2 → 3 does not require edge 1 → 3.For disjoint sets A, B, C , sA ⊥⊥ sB | sC denotes conditional independence. Markovness links graphical d-separation to independence; faithfulness excludes additional independences from exact cancellation (Colombo & Maathuis, 2014). Zero correlation is insufficient. A skeleton omits di-rections, a PDAG permits unoriented edges, and a CPDAG represents an observational equivalence class (Hauser & Bühlmann, 2012); arbitrary finite-sample completion does not identify that class. do (sj = u) replaces a mechanism, unlike observational conditioning. Here u is a verified attained value, not unchecked ucmd ; calibration changes the visual mapping rather than the environment. Causal sufficiency, independent disturbances and absence of selection bias are assumptions, not consequences of encoding. Labels anchor node identities. Fitted residuals approximate, rather than reveal, true noise (Appendix A.5); preserving them gives factual-conditioned queries, while resam-pling gives population queries (Appendix B.4). Hidden single-frame information can still preclude individual identification. # H ASSUMPTIONS AND SCOPE OF RELATED METHODS 

H.1 RELATED-METHOD ASSUMPTIONS AND SCOPE Tables A23 and A24 expand the settings in Figure 2: identifying unknown factors, calibrating known variables, and selecting experiments are distinct tasks. The tables describe original inputs, supervi-sion, modules, and graph outputs, not equal-budget performance or proof of priority. 

Visual variables and intervention information. Causal representation learning targets causally meaningful generative factors rather than reconstruction alone (Schölkopf et al., 2021). CausalVAE uses concept supervision (Yang et al., 2021); CITRIS (Causal Identifiability from Temporal Inter-vened Sequences) uses temporal images and known targets, and iCITRIS includes instantaneous effects (Lippe et al., 2022; 2023b). BISCUIT (Causal Representation Learning from Binary Inter-actions) learns variable–interaction correspondence (Lippe et al., 2023a). The implicit latent causal model (ILCM) uses paired observations around unknown random interventions and permits subse-quent graph extraction (Brehmer et al., 2022). Identification from interventional-distribution geom-etry can require neither target indices nor actual values in the corresponding settings (Ahuja et al., 2023). These methods use different graph-learning stages; later extraction is not absence of graph capability. Our known target identities and actual values are stronger supervision, used to calibrate an existing visual correspondence while preserving decoder coordinates. 

Control, acquisition, and structure. Independently controllable factors (ICF) jointly learns pixel factors and policies using selectivity feedback, without estimating their adjacency graph (Thomas et al., 2018); Causal Curiosity learns behaviors that distinguish environment factors (Sontakke et al., 49 Preprint 2021). Action-controllable factorization (ACF) instead estimates policies to correct given transition-data distributions, not to acquire new experiments (Rodriguez-Sanchez et al., 2025). The visual causal discovery network (V-CDN) learns keypoints and structure from given videos (Li et al., 2020). For observed variables, active strategies (Hauser & Bühlmann, 2014), active intervention targeting (AIT) (Scherrer et al., 2021), causal Bayesian experimental design (CBED) (Tigas et al., 2022), and causal amortized active structure learning (CAASL) (Annadani et al., 2024) choose experiments as detailed in the tables. Our calibration acquisition is fixed round-robin; only subsequent graph discovery selects targets from current ambiguity. Values follow a fixed distribution. 

Extrapolation and task-specific structure. Rep4Ex uses action–latent invariance for intervention extrapolation (Saengkyongam et al., 2024); action-informed representations therefore have prece-dent. Causality-Seeking Agent learns context-relevant subgraphs from structured state–action tra-jectories (Zhao et al., 2025), reward-oriented causal representation learning recovers task-relevant variables/structure through adaptive exploration (Yan et al., 2025), and Active Causal Experimen-talist (ACE) studies mechanism estimation with a known graph (Cooper & Velasquez, 2026). Their specialized outputs should not be interpreted as general capability deficits. 

Our scope. We calibrate the magnitude of pixel-derived readouts with known semantics, then connect target settings, graph-conditioned consequences, and decoding. Constraint-based and interventional-equivalence-class methods supply graph-learning tools (Colombo & Maathuis, 2014; Hauser & Bühlmann, 2012); invertible coordinate-wise calibration adds no identifiability guaran-tee. Empirical gains require matched-data and matched-budget controls. Image counterfactuals and OOD outcomes have distinct query/distribution definitions; a checkmark denotes neither a theorem nor overall superiority.
