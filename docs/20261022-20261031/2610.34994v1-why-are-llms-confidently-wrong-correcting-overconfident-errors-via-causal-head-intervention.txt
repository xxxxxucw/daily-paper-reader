## Abstract 

Modern large language models (LLMs) often exhibit overconfident errors, assign-ing high confidence to incorrect predictions and thereby undermining reliability in real-world use. Existing calibration methods typically rely on post-hoc adjust-ment or retraining, operating at the output level without addressing the underly-ing causes of miscalibration. We propose CoCHI (Class-oriented Causal Head 

Intervention), a framework that improves calibration by identifying and interven-ing on internal components responsible for distinct confidence behaviours. By analysing predictions through correctness-confidence patterns, CoCHI enables targeted intervention on model internals, reducing overconfident errors while pre-serving correct predictions. Our approach provides a mechanistic perspective on calibration, linking reliability to identifiable structures within the model rather than treating it as an output-level issue. Experiments across multiple LLMs show consistent improvements in calibration metrics, including Expected Calibration Error, Negative Log-Likelihood, and Brier Score, while maintaining accuracy on five multiple-choice question answering benchmarks. These results suggest that effective calibration requires not only adjusting outputs, but also addressing the underlying causal mechanisms within the model. 

## 1 Introduction 

Modern neural models have achieved remarkable performance across a wide range of tasks. However, they frequently produce overconfident errors, assigning high confidence to incorrect predictions [1, 2, 3, 4] and thereby undermining reliability in high-stakes applications. This issue is further amplified in large language models (LLMs) [5, 6, 7], which have become a dominant paradigm in modern AI and are widely deployed in domains such as healthcare, education, and scientific reasoning, where incorrect yet confident outputs can lead to significant downstream risks [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. To address this problem, a large body of work has focused on calibration , aiming to align predicted confidence with empirical accuracy [13, 14, 15]. Existing approaches, such as temperature scaling and its variants, primarily operate at the output level by adjusting logits or confidence scores, while more recent methods introduce learned calibrators or inference-time interventions [16]. Despite their effectiveness in some settings, these approaches share a fundamental limitation: they treat miscalibration as a surface-level phenomenon and do not address the internal mechanisms that give rise to overconfident errors. As a result, they often fail to generalise and provide limited insight into why models are confidently wrong.

In this work, we take a different perspective and ask: what internal components of a model are responsible for overconfident errors, and can we intervene on them directly? Recent advances in mechanistic interpretability [17, 18, 19, 20] have made significant progress in moving beyond correlational post-hoc explanations toward causally grounded, intervention-based understanding of how LLMs implement computations internally. In particular, causal intervention methods such as causal head gating [17], causal mediation analysis [18, 21], and causal tracing [19] allow us to identify internal components that are causally responsible for specific model behaviours by systematically perturbing internal activations and observing the resulting change in the model’s output distribution. However, existing interpretability approaches largely focus on post-hoc analysis and explanation, rather than leveraging these insights to actively improve model reliability. Consequently, the connection between internal computational circuits and miscalibration remains largely unexplored. To bridge this gap, we propose CoCHI (Class-oriented Causal Head Intervention), a framework that improves calibration by directly targeting the internal causes of overconfident errors in large language models. Our key insight is that miscalibration is not merely an output-level issue, but arises from specific internal components that systematically promote incorrect yet highly confident predictions or introduce noise into otherwise correct ones. To capture this, we decompose model predictions into four calibration classes based on correctness and confidence, and perform class-conditioned causal analysis to identify attention heads associated with distinct behaviours. Based on this analysis, CoCHI constructs a structured intervention that selectively suppresses heads responsible for overconfident errors while removing non-contributing components in correct predictions. This results in a minimal, targeted modification to the model’s internal computation that improves calibration without altering model parameters, providing a mechanism-aware alternative to conventional post-hoc approaches. 

Contributions. Our main contributions are as follows: • We propose a structural causal mask for calibration, operating directly on model internals rather than relying on post-hoc output adjustment. • We partition model predictions into four fine-grained classes based on correctness and confi-dence, enabling precise identification of attention heads that causally promote overconfident errors or fail to support correct predictions. • We introduce CoCHI (Class-oriented Causal Head Intervention), an inference-time frame-work that performs class-aware causal analysis and constructs a structured mask to selectively suppress problematic attention heads. • Extensive experiments across five multiple-choice question answering benchmarks show that CoCHI consistently improves calibration metrics while preserving accuracy, outperforming traditional post-hoc calibration methods. 

## 2 Related Work 

Uncertainty Estimation and Overconfident Errors. A large body of work studies uncertainty estimation in neural models, including Bayesian approaches [22, 23, 24, 25, 26], deep ensembles [27], and post-hoc techniques such as temperature scaling [16], which rescale logits using a learned temperature parameter. In the context of LLMs, uncertainty estimation and calibration have received increasing attention [28]. Recent efforts extend post-hoc calibration to LLMs, including adaptive temperature scaling [29] and learned calibrators such as Thermometer [30], aiming to better align predicted confidence with empirical accuracy. Other approaches adjust hidden representations, for example by training a linear layer on top of the final hidden states for confidence prediction [31], or exploit prompt-based strategies and self-consistency to estimate uncertainty [32, 33, 34, 35, 15]. Despite their effectiveness in some settings, these methods primarily operate at the output or representation level, but recent work such as Inference-Time Intervention [36] shows that undesirable behaviours (e.g., hallucination) are governed by internal mechanisms. These methods primarily enhance truthful representations, while overlooking the role of mechanisms that specifically drive overconfident errors, where incorrect predictions are assigned high confidence scores. Recent work has shown that attention heads encode rich signals about model uncertainty, enabling unsupervised uncertainty estimation without additional training [37]. However, these approaches primarily focus on extracting uncertainty signals from internal representations, without modifying the model’s behaviour. 2This limitation motivates our approach, which targets the structural causal components responsible for overconfident errors and performs structured intervention to improve calibration. 

Circuit Discovery in Mechanistic Interpretability Circuit discovery aims to identify the minimal subgraph of a language model responsible for a specific behaviour. The seminal work of [38] manually identified the Indirect Object Identification (IOI) circuit in GPT-2 Small, comprising 28 attention heads across seven functional classes. To scale this process, Conmy et al. [39] proposed Automatic Circuit Discovery (ACDC), an iterative pruning method based on activation patching, which successfully rediscovered known circuits such as Greater-Than and recovered most components of the IOI circuit. Subsequent approaches, including Efficient Attribution Patching (EAP), further improved the efficiency and scalability of automated circuit discovery [40]. Beyond circuit discovery, mechanistic interpretability has increasingly incorporated tools from causal inference [41]. These approaches leverage counterfactual interventions and structured perturbations to uncover how structural heads contribute to model outputs [42, 43, 44, 45]. In particular, causal head gating identifies functional roles of attention heads by assigning graded scores for facilitation, interference, or irrelevance based on their causal effect on task performance, going beyond purely correlational analyses [17]. Recent mechanistic studies have shown that verbalised confidence in LLMs is governed by dedicated internal circuits that are largely decoupled from factual correctness [46]. These findings suggest that overconfidence arises not from miscalibration at the output layer, but from specific internal mechanisms that consistently generate confident responses regardless of their accuracy [47]. Building on this insight, we identify and suppress attention heads that contribute to high-confidence errors, leading to improved calibration and reliability.

Gate Optimisation. The gating variables are optimised while keeping model parameters frozen. Given a dataset D, we minimise the negative log-likelihood objective:

Dual Optimisation and Role Identification. To disambiguate causal importance from incidental effects, we optimise two sets of gates: • G+: optimised under a retention bias, encouraging gates towards 1, • G−: optimised under a sparsity bias, encouraging gates towards 0. The divergence between G+ and G− reveals the causal role of each head: heads that remain active under sparsity are necessary for the task, while those suppressed even under retention bias are likely harmful. 3Figure 1: The framework of CoCHI. 

Causal Head Taxonomy. Based on the optimised gates, each attention head is assigned a causal role. Facilitating heads contribute positively to performance and remain active under sparsity ( G−), indicating necessity. Interfering heads degrade performance and are suppressed even under retention bias ( G+), indicating harmful contributions. Irrelevant heads have negligible impact, with gating values varying under different regularisation regimes. This taxonomy provides a causal, rather than correlational, characterisation of model internals, validated through targeted ablation and intervention. 

## 4 Methodology 

We reframe calibration as a problem of causal intervention on model heads. Specifically, our framework identifies and suppresses causal heads responsible for overconfident errors, while removing sources of noise in otherwise correct predictions. This perspective provides a mechanistic view of miscalibration, linking reliability to identifiable structural components within the model. An overview of CoCHI is shown in Figure 1.

From a computational perspective, the overhead is negligible, as the intervention involves only element-wise gating during the forward pass. Since no parameters are updated, the method can be seamlessly applied to pretrained models in a plug-and-play manner. 

## 5 Experiments 

5.1 Implementation Details 

In practice, we apply CoCHI on a small subset of the data (typically fewer than 50 samples), with balanced sampling across calibration classes to ensure stable attribution estimates. We use a fixed confidence threshold τc = 0 .5 to define calibration classes, and a threshold τ to identify facilitating heads. The number of masked heads k is treated as a hyperparameter; we find that small values (e.g., k ∈ [6 , 8] ) provide a favourable trade-off between calibration improvement and accuracy preservation. The penalty coefficient was empirically tuned across 15 model–dataset runs, yielding a stable trade-off that limits degradation on correct_high calibration ( ∆ECE < 0.015 ) while still effectively suppressing overconfident errors on incorrect predictions. We evaluate our method on three representative open-source large language models: LLaMA-3.1-8B [7], Gemma-2-9B [48], and 

Mistral-7B-v0.1 [49]. We adopt LLaMA-3.1-8B as the primary backbone for ablation and hyper-parameter studies. All experiments are conducted in a zero-shot setting, ensuring that improvements in calibration are solely attributable to the proposed inference-time intervention. Further experimental details are provided in Appendix A. 

5.2 Evaluation Protocols Datasets. We evaluate our method on five widely used multiple-choice question answering bench-marks spanning diverse domains, including general knowledge, science, and medicine, to assess both generalisation and domain-specific robustness of calibration. Measuring Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) [50] is a large-scale multi-domain benchmark covering 57 subjects such as STEM, humanities, and social sciences, designed to evaluate general knowledge and reasoning ability. MedMCQA [51] is a large medical QA dataset derived from real-world entrance examinations, focusing on domain-specific knowledge in healthcare. OpenBookQA [52] is a science QA benchmark requiring multi-hop reasoning over a small set of core science facts. MedQA-USMLE [53] is a chal-lenging medical benchmark based on USMLE exams, evaluating clinical reasoning and professional knowledge. SciQ [54] is a science QA dataset constructed from educational materials, focusing on factual understanding and basic reasoning. All datasets are formatted as four-choice multiple-choice tasks, and we adopt a unified evaluation protocol. 

Baselines. We compare our method against several representative calibration approaches, includ-ing both post-hoc and inference-time techniques. Temperature Scaling (TS) [16] is a standard post-hoc method that learns a single scalar temperature T on a held-out validation set to rescale logits. Inference-Time Intervention (ITI) [36] modifies model activations during inference using a fixed intervention strength α, enabling reliability improvements without retraining. Thermometer (Therm.) [30] is a learned calibration method that trains a lightweight MLP-based calibrator on top of model outputs to better align predicted confidence with empirical accuracy. Adaptive Temperature Scaling (ATS) [29] extends TS by dynamically adapting the temperature based on input features, allowing more flexible calibration across samples. 

Metrics. We evaluate performance in terms of both accuracy and calibration. Prediction quality is measured by accuracy , defined as the proportion of correctly predicted answers. 5.3 Main Results 

The results in Table 1 show that CoCHI consistently improves calibration across all datasets and models while maintaining competitive accuracy. On datasets such as MMLU and MedMCQA , CoCHI achieves clear reductions in ECE together with improved NLL and Brier Score. Similar trends are observed on OpenBookQA and MedQA-USMLE , where CoCHI attains the lowest or near-lowest calibration errors without sacrificing predictive performance. In several cases, CoCHI also improves accuracy, particularly on LLaMA-3.1-8B and Mistral-7B. In contrast, existing methods show limited or unstable behaviour across datasets. Post-hoc methods such as TS and ATS provide only marginal improvements and can severely degrade calibration in some settings, while Thermometer often increases NLL, indicating unreliable probability estimates. ITI also exhibits high variance across models and datasets. The advantage of CoCHI is especially evident on Mistral-7B, where it consistently achieves lower ECE together with strong NLL and Brier Score performance. Overall, these results suggest that intervening on targeted causal attention heads 8provides more effective and stable calibration than conventional output-level adjustment methods. Additional results are provided in Appendix B.1.

Overall, CoCHI shifts the computational burden from continuous inference to a one-time structural calibration step, offering a more deployment-efficient solution for improving model reliability. ## 6 Conclusion 

In this work, we study overconfident errors in large language models and argue that miscalibration should be addressed not only at the output level, but also through the internal mechanisms that give rise to unreliable confidence estimates. We propose CoCHI, a class-oriented causal head intervention framework that decomposes predictions by correctness and confidence, identifies attention heads associated with distinct calibration behaviours, and performs targeted inference-time masking without updating model parameters. Across multiple LLMs and five multiple-choice QA benchmarks, CoCHI consistently improves calibration metrics while maintaining competitive predictive accuracy. The ablation and sensitivity analyses further show that reliable calibration depends on structured causal selection rather than indiscriminate head removal. Moreover, while CoCHI primarily targets 

wrong_high errors, it also yields improvements in correct_low cases. These findings support a mechanism-aware view of calibration, linking model reliability to identifiable internal structures rather than treating it solely as a post-hoc output adjustment problem. 

Limitations. This work focuses on multiple-choice question answering, where confidence can be naturally defined over a fixed answer space. Extending CoCHI to open-ended generation, long-form reasoning, and multi-turn interaction requires further investigation, especially when correctness and confidence are harder to define. In addition, our current intervention operates at the attention-head level, which provides an efficient and interpretable unit for masking, but may not capture all sources of miscalibration arising from MLP layers, residual streams, or cross-layer interactions.

## A Dataset Preparation and Experimental Pipeline 

This section details the data preparation and experimental pipeline to ensure reproducibility. 

A.1 Datasets 

We use five publicly available multiple-choice question answering datasets (as shown in Table 5) from HuggingFace, covering general knowledge, science, and medical domains. All datasets are formatted as four-option multiple-choice questions with a single correct answer. Table 5: Statistical summary of the datasets.

A.2 Data Cleaning and Standardisation 

All datasets are converted into a unified format containing: question ID, dataset source, question text, four answer choices (A–D), and the correct label. We remove samples that: (i) do not contain exactly four options, or (ii) have missing or invalid answer labels. 

SciQ Adjustment. In SciQ, the correct answer appears in a fixed position. To avoid positional bias, we cyclically rotate answer options to ensure a uniform distribution. 

A.3 Prompt Formatting 

All inputs follow a unified zero-shot prompt: Answer the following multiple choice question with a single letter: A, B, C, or D. Question: [question text] A. [choice 1] B. [choice 2] C. [choice 3] D. [choice 4] Answer: No demonstrations or additional context are provided. 

A.4 Training Example Selection 

From the fit pool, we sample a balanced calibration subset: 16 • up to 10 examples per class, • maximum of 40 samples per dataset. This simulates a low-resource calibration setting. 

A.5 Top-k Head Selection 

We determine the number of masked heads k via grid search over k ∈ { 1, . . . , 20 }. Selection is based on minimising Expected Calibration Error (ECE), subject to an accuracy drop constraint of at most 1% relative to the unmodified model. A single k is selected per model and applied across all datasets to capture model-level calibration properties. ## B Additional Results 

B.1 Main results 

Table 6 confirms the effectiveness of our method on SciQ, demonstrating consistent improvements in calibration across all models while maintaining strong predictive performance. On Gemma-2-9B, our method achieves the best overall calibration, reducing ECE and improving both NLL and Brier Score , while matching the highest accuracy. On LLaMA-3.1-8B, the gains are even more pronounced: our method simultaneously achieves the best performance across all four metrics, improving accuracy, lowering ECE, and reducing both NLL and Brier Score. Similar trends are observed on Mistral-7B, where our method achieves the best results across all metrics, outperforming the strongest baseline (ATS) in both calibration and accuracy. These results indicate that the proposed intervention consistently improves confidence alignment without sacrificing predictive quality. In contrast, baseline methods exhibit unstable and inconsistent behaviour. ITI achieves competitive accuracy on Gemma-2-9B but significantly worsens calibration, while on Mistral-7B it severely degrades both accuracy and calibration. Post-hoc methods such as TS and ATS provide moderate or inconsistent calibration improvements but often fail to jointly optimise NLL and Brier Score, with ATS in particular showing substantial degradation in probabilistic metrics (e.g., NLL exceeding 1.0 across models). Thermometer similarly leads to large increases in NLL, indicating unreliable probability estimates. B.2 Efficiency Analysis 

Table 7 shows that the calibration cost of our method scales predictably with model size while remaining practical. The average calibration time increases from 39 s for Mistral-7B to 58 s for LLaMA-3.1-8B and 88 s for Gemma-2-9B, reflecting the expected growth in forward-pass complexity, yet all models complete calibration within roughly 1–1.5 minutes.
