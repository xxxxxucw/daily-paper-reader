# Metric Conformal Prediction via the Conditional Expected Local Radius

> 2

School of Mathematical Sciences, Center for Statistical Science, Peking University 

Abstract 

We introduce the conditional expected local radius (CELR) and develop a new conformal prediction method for non-Euclidean responses in general metric spaces. CELR measures local conditional probability concentration, allowing prediction sets to accommodate heterogeneity and multimodality without re-quiring densities or a vector space structure. Its conditional distribution yields a nonconformity score that is comparable across covariate values and avoids costly transport comparisons between distance profiles. We further adapt a metric distributional random forest that handles multivariate covariates and nonlinear interactions, while a single forest fit supplies all score components, reducing computation by eliminating repeated local fitting. We establish uniform consistency of the estimators and asymptotic conditional validity under suitable regularity conditions, together with finite-sample marginal coverage under ex-changeability. Numerical studies demonstrate effective uncertainty quantification across different metric spaces and substantial computational savings over the conditional profile score method. 

Keywords: Conditional validity, Conformal prediction, Metric space, Random forest, Random object 

# 1 Introduction 

Conformal prediction (CP) quantifies predictive uncertainty through prediction sets with finite-sample and distribution-free coverage guarantees (Vovk et al., 2005). In 1arXiv:2610.31336v1 [stat.ML] 22 Oct 2026 regression, the aim is to construct a set ˆC1−α(X0) for a future observation ( X0, Y 0)satisfying the marginal coverage requirement

For scalar responses, CP yields prediction intervals and bands (Lei and Wasserman, 2014; Lei et al., 2018; Chernozhukov et al., 2021; Sesia and Romano, 2021; Barber et al., 2023). The nonconformity score ranks candidate responses and determines the size and shape of the prediction set. Studentized residual scores (Lei et al., 2018) and scores based on conditional quantiles (Romano et al., 2019), for example, allow prediction intervals to adapt to heteroskedasticity. Other developments concern data reuse (Vovk, 2015; Barber et al., 2021), adaptation to changing distributions (Gibbs and Candes, 2021; Gibbs and Candès, 2024), and the selection and aggregation of prediction sets (Yang and Kuchibhotla, 2025; Xu et al., 2026). Marginal coverage averages over the covariate distribution and need not provide adequate coverage at a particular covariate value. Exact conditional validity without restrictions on the joint distribution is generally unattainable (Vovk, 2012; Lei and Wasserman, 2014; Barber et al., 2021). A common alternative is asymptotic conditional validity:

Conditional density ranks yield sets that converge to highest predictive density regions and adapt to skewness, heavy tails, and multimodality (Izbicki et al., 2022). Localized conformal methods pursue related coverage goals (Guan, 2023; Hore and Barber, 2025; Gibbs et al., 2025). For a general account of conformal prediction, see Angelopoulos et al. (2024). These methods are designed primarily for Euclidean responses. Non-Euclidean responses, such as phylogenetic trees, covariance matrices, and networks, are naturally treated as random objects in metric spaces. Methods for such data include Fréchet regression, regression on manifolds (Lin et al., 2017; Petersen and Müller, 2019; Dubey and Müller, 2020; Lin and Müller, 2021; Bhattacharjee et al., 2025), and dimension reduction (Panaretos et al., 2014; Virta et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024). Constructing informative prediction sets requires additional information about the spread and shape of the conditional law. Vector operations and densities need not be available in these 2spaces, so conventional constructions based on residuals or densities are not directly applicable. The conditional law may also concentrate in several separated regions whose locations and relative masses vary with the predictors. A ball centered at a single regression estimate may therefore give a poor description of the likely responses. Zhou and Müller (2025) addressed this problem by constructing conformal prediction sets from conditional distance profiles, using the average transport cost between profiles. Their method accommodates multimodality without requiring a density or a conventional distribution function for the response. A candidate’s distance profile can also be summarized by the radii needed to accumulate probability mass around it. These local radii underlie the distance-to-a-measure (DTM) family (Chazal et al., 2011, 2016), including the L1 formulation studied by Brécheteau (2019), which primarily concern unconditional geometric or topological inference and comparisons between metric spaces equipped with probability measures. In this work, our contributions are three-fold. First, we construct a conformal score from the conditional expected local radius (CELR) that is distinct from the conditional distance profiles. Its unconditional counterpart, the expected local radius (ELR), coincides with the L1 formulation of DTM studied by Brécheteau (2019), up to the convention used at probability mass thresholds. Under the conditional law of 

Y | X = x, CELR averages the radii needed to accumulate probability mass around a candidate. This description of local concentration complements metric depths, ranks, and quantiles (Geenens et al., 2023; Dai et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2022; Dubey et al., 2024). We transform CELR by its conditional distribution before conformal calibration, making the score comparable across covariate values. The resulting prediction sets can accommodate heterogeneity and multimodality in the conditional law without requiring densities or a vector space structure. CELR also simplifies computation: it uses only the candidate’s conditional distance profile and avoids the transport comparisons between profiles required by Zhou and Müller (2025). Second, we estimate the conditional law with a metric distributional random forest (MDRF). Local linear smoothing often requires additional structure or dimension reduc-tion as the number of covariates increases (Fan and Gijbels, 1996). Our construction uses maximum mean discrepancy to select splits and draws on generalized random forests (Athey et al., 2019; Wager and Athey, 2018) and distributional random forests (Cevid et al., 2022). It differs from the local linear approach of Zhou and Müller (2025) in three respects: (1) Unified estimation. A single forest fit provides the weights for estimating con-ditional distance profiles, CELRs, and the conditional distributions of CELRs. 3Reusing these weights reduces the computation needed at each stage. Zhou and Müller (2025) estimate distance profiles, average transport costs, and their conditional distributions through separate local linear fits. (2) Adaptive weights. The forest uses response information to choose splits, so its weights can adapt to variation in the conditional law (Lin and Jeon, 2006; Qiu et al., 2024). The local linear weights in Zhou and Müller (2025) are instead based on Euclidean distances in covariate space. (3) Multivariate covariates. The forest partitions the multivariate covariate space directly and can represent nonlinear interactions and local variation. The multi-variate procedure of Zhou and Müller (2025) relies on a single index assumption, which can be restrictive when covariate effects cannot be captured by a single linear combination. Third, we establish uniform consistency of the estimated conditional distance profiles, CELRs, and score ranks, and derive asymptotic conditional validity. Pointwise consistency does not suffice because the score construction evaluate fitted quantities at arguments that depend on the data. The proof treats the forest as a growing-order U-statistic whose order increases with sample size. We derive a maximal inequality for the associated U-process and combine it with leaf shrinkage and stability bounds to control the accumulated estimation error. The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 defines ELR and studies its geometric properties. Section 3 presents the score construction, split conformal calibration, and forest estimator. Section 4 gives the uniform consistency and asymptotic conditional validity results. Sections 5 and 6 present simulations in several metric spaces and two data applications, respectively. Section 7 concludes. Proofs and additional implementation details are in the supplementary material.

# 3 ELR-based conformal prediction in metric spaces

4.1 Uniform consistency of the forest estimator 

We first prove uniform convergence of the MDRF estimator in RKHS norm. The proof extends the pointwise analysis of Cevid et al. (2022) using bounds on leaf diameters and a discretization of the covariate space. We assume the following conditions on the forest construction (Wager and Athey, 2018; Athey et al., 2019). 

A1 (Forest regularity) . The forest satisfies the following properties: (P1) (Subsampling) Each tree is built from a subsample of size sn drawn without replacement from the n training observations, where sn → ∞ and sn/n → 0. (P2) (Honesty) Each tree subsample is randomly divided into equally sized splitting and estimation sets. Tree construction may use the splitting observations and 11 estimation covariates, but not estimation responses, which are used only for leaf averages. (P3) (Random split) At each internal node, the probability of selecting the jth covariate for splitting is at least π/p for some π > 0 and every j = 1 , . . . , p .(P4) ( ϕ-regularity) Split thresholds are selected from observed covariate values, and each child node receives at least a fraction 0 < ϕ ≤ 0.2 of the estimation observations in its parent node. Trees are grown until every terminal leaf contains between κ and 2 κ − 1 estimation observations for some fixed κ ∈ N.(P5) (Symmetry) The distribution of the randomized tree output is invariant under permutations of the training observations. We also require the following condition on the marginal covariate density, as in Assumption (A3) of Zhou and Müller (2025). 

A2 (Bounded covariate density) . The covariates X ∈ X have a density that is bounded above and bounded away from zero.

# 5 Simulation studies 

We assess the finite-sample performance of the proposed ELR-based conformal method in simulations designed to evaluate conditional coverage across the covariate domain, prediction-set size, and applicability to non-Euclidean responses. In each experiment, the data are randomly divided into equally sized training and calibration samples, and split-conformal prediction sets are constructed at the nominal coverage level 1 − α = 0 .9. Throughout this section, ELR denotes the proposed procedure estimated by the MDRF, whereas CPS denotes the conditional profile score procedure of Zhou and Müller (2025) implemented with local linear smoothing. We consider seven settings in total: Settings 1–2, 4, and 6–7 are presented below, while Settings 3 and 5 are reported in Supplementary Section S.4. 6.2 U.S. energy data 

We next analyze a state-level panel of U.S. energy consumption ( https://www.eia.go v/state/seds/seds-data-complete.php?sid=US ), comprising annual observations for the 50 states and the District of Columbia from 1970 through 2023. Since U + V + W = 1, the response lies on the probability simplex. We map it to the positive orthant of the unit sphere using the square-root transformation ˜Y =(√U , √V , √W )⊤, and construct prediction sets on the sphere using the geodesic metric. This representation preserves the compositional structure of the response while placing the conformal procedure in a natural non-Euclidean space. Our objective is to quantify predictive uncertainty in a changing and heterogeneous energy system, rather than to provide point predictions alone. The 10 predictors are calendar year, population, per-capita electricity consumption, heating degree days, cooling degree days, the nominal prices of coal, petroleum, natural gas, and electricity, and a coastal indicator. These variables represent the long-run energy transition, climate-related demand, incentives for fuel substitution, and persistent geographic heterogeneity. The energy analysis in Zhou and Müller (2025) 23 uses calendar year alone; our additional covariates allow comparisons among states within the same year. Table S.4 in Supplementary Section S.5.2 provides detailed definitions of the response components and covariates. # 7 Discussion 

CELR provides a way to construct prediction sets from local concentration in a metric space. Transforming CELR by its conditional distribution function gives the score CRS, whose values are comparable across covariate values. CRS ranks candidates by local concentration rather than distance from a point predictor, so the sets can reflect heteroscedasticity and multimodality without requiring a density or vector space structure. All score components are estimated using the weights from a single metric distributional random forest. Split conformal calibration guarantees finite-sample marginal coverage under exchangeability, and we establish asymptotic conditional validity under the stated regularity conditions. Sample splitting leaves only part of the data for estimating the conditional law. Out-of-bag forest predictions could make fuller use of the sample by scoring each observation with trees fitted without it. A coverage argument would need to account for the dependence among these scores. Conditional local radii may also be useful for anomaly detection, change point testing, and decision problems under distributional ambiguity in metric spaces.
