ABSTRACT 

Faithfully understanding the internal process that occurs as models form their chain of thought is an important problem with the rise in model capabilities. How-ever, this remains challenging due to the variance in sampled responses, making it difficult to isolate the effects of interventions and compare model reasoning systematically. We propose a method for localizing and analyzing how a given in-tervention affects the model’s reasoning through behavioral counterfactuals: given a fixed response, how does introducing an intervention to the prompt change the next-token distributions? We train a module to edit the residual stream such that it minimizes the KL divergence between the output distributions for a fixed response under a source prompt and its counterfactual target prompt. We find that match-ing the output distributions recovers the counterfactual behavior more effectively than standard activation reconstruction objectives and validate that attribution of the trained module outputs is effective for identifying causally important loca-tions for counterfactual recovery. The attribution maps and visualizations of the downstream effects of model edits provide an interface for generating hypotheses about the original model’s computation, which can then be tested with activation patching. In a case study on Gemma-4-E4B-it and DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B, we investigate how a suggestion influences model reasoning even when it is not verbally acknowledged. Our analysis identifies representations at the question statement and filler tokens that mediate a substantial part of the suggestion’s in-fluence, with downstream effects such as changing the framing of the justification or even the final answer. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

With the rise in model capabilities and the risks that come with it, understanding how language models reason is a crucial problem to solve. However, recent works have shown that the generated chain-of-thought may become less monitorable and can be unreliable for understanding the decision making and behavior of these models (Chen et al., 2025; Baherwani et al., 2026; Brauer et al., 2026; OpenAI, 2026). As a result, an increasingly important question is how do we develop a better understanding of the internal computations driving model reasoning. A central challenge for interpreting model reasoning is connecting internal computation to changes in behavior across long, open-ended responses. Even for the same prompt, sampled responses can vary substantially in wording and reasoning structure, making it difficult to compare their activa-tions, identify downstream effects, and extract consistent patterns across examples. Recent work has approached this problem by organizing analysis around reasoning behaviors such as backtrack-ing and uncertainty (Venhoff et al., 2025) or by identifying influential sentences and their effects on subsequent reasoning (Bogdan et al., 2025). While these approaches provide ways to study structure within and across reasoning traces, an intervention, such as a suggestion added to the prompt, may affect the model computation in ways that are not captured by predefined reasoning behaviors or sentence-level descriptions. We therefore aim to develop a method to isolate the effect of a given in-tervention on model computation throughout the response and identify key locations through which that effect is mediated and understand their role in downstream behavior.

Towards this, we approach analyzing model reasoning through behavioral counterfactuals: how would the model’s next-token distributions change under an intervention while holding the generated response fixed? The approach is illustrated in Figure 1. For example, if we want to understand how a change in the system prompt affects a model, we can evaluate a fixed response under the two prompts, comparing their next-token distributions at each response position. Holding the response prefix fixed isolates the effect of the prompt change on these conditional distributions, allowing us to examine shifts that may not be apparent from sampled text. While the counterfactual setup isolates the change due to the intervention, understanding how in-ternal computation produces these changes remains difficult. Building on end-to-end training ob-jectives such as Braun et al. (2024), we train an edit module that modifies the residual stream at an intermediate layer across the source prompt and response to match the counterfactual next-token distributions over the fixed response. This provides dense behavioral supervision without requir-ing tokenwise alignment between source and target prompt activations, allowing the edit module to handle natural interventions such as insertions, deletions, or paraphrases. The module provides an interface through which we can attribute distributional recovery to individual per-token edits, which we call edit events, and ablate selected edits to examine their downstream effects. Analysis based on the edit events can then be used to generate hypotheses about the original model’s representations, which we then test through activation patching. We apply this framework to Gemma-4-E4B-it (Team, 2026) and DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B (DeepSeek-AI, 2025) in a setting where the user provides a suggestion that the author is in the wrong for a given complex ethical situation. Our analysis identifies that surprisingly, a sub-stantial fraction of the intervention’s influence is mediated by the representations at the question statement and filler tokens such as whitespace and punctuation rather than at tokens with semantic content more relevant to the verbalized reasoning. Analyzing the downstream effects of the question and filler token representations, we find that they can significantly change the model’s framing of situations and its final answer. Our results demonstrate that analyzing the activations using this coun-terfactual setup can reveal the effects of interventions that are not directly verbalized and otherwise could be difficult to detect.

3 EXPERIMENTS 

Setup. We use the Scruples dataset (Lourie et al., 2021) which is a collection of complex ethical situations with labels for each situation based on judgements from community members. Our pri-mary intervention based on Guan et al. (2025) provides the model with a suggestion that the author is in the wrong for each situation. We perform our evaluations on Gemma-4-E4B-it (Team, 2026) and DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B (DeepSeek-AI, 2025). For each model, we apply the edit mod-ule after the midpoint layer of each model. Training details and hyperparameters are provided in Appendix A, and additional training diagnostics and ablations are provided in Appendix B. 3.1 RECOVERING COUNTERFACTUAL BEHAVIOR 

A common training objective for interpretable interfaces such as sparse autoencoders (Huben et al., 2024) and transcoders (Dunefsky et al., 2024) is activation reconstruction using Mean Squared Error (MSE) or a normalized form of this which divides the MSE by the L2 norm of the activation and which we will denote as NMSE. We define activation recovery as 1 − NMSE NMSE 0 where NMSE 0 is the NMSE corresponding to all zero edits. However, reconstructing the activations of the counterfactual computation may not result in similar downstream behavior due to the changes in the prompt. We provide a comparison between training the edit module to perform reconstruction of the residual stream at a given layer and training the module to reduce the KL divergence at the output layer with results for Gemma-4-E4B-it and DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B in Figure 2. We find that while activation reconstruction better recreates the residual stream at the midpoint layer, it recovers less of the output distribution, with recovery dropping from 47 .8% to 20 .4% for DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B and for Gemma-4-E4B-it, it can actually result in negative recovery. This suggests that geometric reconstruction of the activations may not be sufficient to transfer the output-level behavior of the counterfactual computation and that distribution matching is more effective at behavioral transfer. Therefore, we perform downstream exploration using modules trained with the distribution matching objective. 4.1 EXPLORATORY ANALYSIS 

Works on Chain-of-Thought monitoring such as Guan et al. (2025) find that the effects of inter-ventions, such as suggestions that the author is in the wrong, on the model’s reasoning are hard to monitor for the Scruples dataset from the generated text alone. Furthermore, as seen in Ta-ble 1, the intervention has a noticeable effect on the answer distribution for both models, but ex-plicit acknowledgement of the suggestion is limited. Important Tokens. One consistent pattern we found across attribution maps was that large attribution scores were concentrated on whitespace or delimiters as well as the question statement as seen in Table 2. We collect the top 10 most frequent tokens in the top 5% by absolute attribution scores within each record across all tokens along with the frequency with which an occurrence of that token is in the top 5%. We see that the top 10 tokens are mostly filler (whitespace, punctuation, or delimiter) tokens or text tokens that appear in the question itself rather than common words that appear in the reasoning that we may expect to be important such as “Analyze” or “answer”. Furthermore, we see that these filler tokens frequently have high attribution scores with the indent token appearing most often and 29.5% of its occurrences are in the top 5%. These findings suggest that the key representations explaining the influence of the intervention may be routed through these tokens that do not contain key semantic in-formation about the verbalized reasoning. This result is consistent with prior works that have found that models can perform reasoning over filler tokens (Brauer et al., 2026) and that filler tokens can improve performance on synthetic reasoning tasks (Baherwani et al., 2026). In order to understand how the edits at the tokens are influencing the downstream generation, we ablate the top 5 signed attribution edit events and analyze the largest changes in the generation distribution resulting from the ablation. We provide an example from the dataset for Gemma-4-E4B-it in Figure 4 where we find through ablation, that adding the edit at a filler token with a strong attribution score results in a change in the output distribution towards the counterfactual. We provide further visualizations in Appendix C. 4.2 HYPOTHESIS AND VALIDATION 

Based on these observations, we hypothesize that a representation of the provided suggestion is causally active in the model’s internal representations, particularly at the question and at filler tokens in the response. Furthermore, we hypothesize that these representations shape the wording of the verbalized reasoning and in some cases, the final answer. 

Native activation patching. We test the hypothesis by intervening directly on the original model. Based on the set of common high attribution tokens, we consider two sets of tokens for activation patching: 8Preprint • Filler Tokens - For Gemma-4-E4B-it, filler tokens refer to whitespace tokens (tabs, indents, and newlines) and for DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B, filler tokens refer to newlines and punctuation tokens. • Question span - The span corresponding to the question and answer mapping: “Is the author in the wrong...nothing else.” For each patch, we replace the activation under one prompt condition with the corresponding acti-vation from the counterfactual condition and continue the forward computation normally. Because the control and intervention prompts can differ in token length, we define patching locations based on their position in the response. This allows us to test whether native activations at the locations suggested by the attribution method causally mediate the influence of the intervention. We measure the KL recovery after patching each of the sets of tokens as well as both sets which can be seen in Figure 5. We see that patching just the filler tokens recovers 11.4% for Gemma-4-E4B-it and the question span recovers 11.3% for DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B and patching both recovers 16.2% and 19.3% for Gemma-4-E4B-it and DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B respectively. Compared to a control of randomly patching the same number of tokens, we see that recovery is consistently larger by factors of approximately 2.3 to 8.1. We can also see that the filler tokens have a larger effect for Gemma-4-E4B-it while the question has a larger effect for DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B. We provide qualitative examples of how patching influences the output distribution in Figure 6 and Figure 7 with additional examples in Appendix C, where we can see evidence of the model output moving in the direction of the counterfactual. This demonstrates the utility of the edit module for identifying representations that mediate the intervention’s influence and how the counterfactual setup with fixed responses allows us to more easily see the intervention’s downstream effects.

Understanding Reasoning. Understanding reasoning models is a challenging problem that has become essential to interpretability and model oversight. One of the challenges demonstrated by various works is that reasoning traces are not faithful (Chen et al., 2025; Turpin et al., 2023; Ar-cuschin et al., 2025). Works have explored how we can monitor the model’s chain of thought despite potential unfaithfulness using another language model (Korbak et al., 2025; Guan et al., 2025) as well as how to make models more monitorable by improving chain of thought faithfulness (Hase & Potts, 2026) or maintaining low controllability (Yueh-Han et al., 2026). Other works such as Boppana et al. (2026) aim to understand the dynamics of reasoning by probing for the model’s final answer throughout reasoning. 

6 DISCUSSION 

Behavioral counterfactuals make the effect of interventions through open-ended generations directly measurable, providing insight into model internals and how they translate to downstream behavior. Through this framework, we localized key representations mediating the influence of an interven-tion and traced their downstream effects, finding that question and filler tokens rather than seman-tically meaningful tokens in the response have higher attribution and carry a substantial part of the intervention’s influence, changing the reasoning’s framing and sometimes final answer. These re-sults, along with our finding that improved activation reconstruction does not necessarily lead to improved behavior reconstruction, demonstrate the value of grounding analysis of activations in their downstream behavior. Recent results from Karvonen et al. (2026) show that activation-based interpretability tools do not provide additional insight beyond the chain-of-thought text for predict-ing counterfactual behavior. Our analysis illustrates how activation-based methods can complement verbalized reasoning by identifying internal representations that mediate an intervention’s influence and revealing fine-grained effects of the intervention on the generated text. 

Limitations and Future Work. While the current analysis focuses on a single setting and inter-vention, we believe that such a framework based on counterfactuals and connecting intermediate representations to downstream behavior can generalize to more complex settings and interventions where an understanding of the model internals is weak. Some promising directions are performing attribution from a more targeted set of tokens, intervening on intermediate reasoning steps, or ex-ploring the representations behind harmful interventions such as jailbreaks. Further analysis on the representations at filler tokens, especially with recent geometric (Wurgaft et al., 2026) approaches or verbalization methods (Gurnee et al., 2026), could reveal new insights into model representations of the user, reasoning dynamics, and the complex relationship between verbalized tokens and inter-nal representations. More generally, we hope that our work motivates exploration of how to design counterfactual comparisons that isolate the effects of more complex factors in model computation and clarify their role in model behavior. We explore using RelP (Jafari et al., 2025) for attribution from the KL difference to the edit events in Appendix B. We use the following rules for RelP propagation. • RMSNorm. For RMSNorm, we preserve the original forward computation, but treat the input-dependent normalization factor in the denominator as constant during relevance prop-agation, stopping gradients through it while maintaining the elementwise learned scaling if present. • Nonlinear activations. We divide the backward signal equally between the two branches of a multiplicative interaction to avoid double-counting relevance. We apply this rule to both gated MLPs and the per-layer input gates used by Gemma-4. • Attention. We propagate through attention using its ordinary backward computation.
