Abstract —Counterfactual explanation methods have been in-troduced for unimodal settings, with dedicated frameworks for tabular, timeseries, text, and image data, yet no post-hoc method has been designed for classifiers that learn from multimodal inputs. Existing explainability methods for multimodal classifiers apply modality-specific attribution independently to each input, which fails to yield coherent explanations and does not transfer to arbitrary pre-trained multimodal architectures. To address these shortcomings, we present CRANE (Counterfactual Retrieval via Agnostic Nearest-neighbour Explanations), the first post-hoc, model-agnostic framework for nearest-neighbour multimodal counterfactual generation that retrieves real training examples from the target class as candidate explanations, ensuring plausi-bility by construction. Across four heterogeneous datasets and three fusion strategies, we show that Multimodal Consensus Retrieval (MC-R), which intersects per-modality neighbour sets to identify instances jointly close to the query in every modality, emerges as the strongest multimodal strategy, achieving the best or joint-best outcome on three of four datasets while grounding every explanation in the joint training distribution. Unimodal strategies remain competitive when a single modality dominates the decision boundary. 

Index Terms —Explainable artificial intelligence, Counterfac-tual explanations, Multimodal learning, Post-hoc explanation 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Data science pipelines increasingly operate on heteroge-neous inputs, combining structured records, free text, time-series, and images within a single predictive model. Clinical decision support systems fuse vital signs with structured patient demographics to predict outcomes, such as sepsis mor-tality [1] and cancer metastasis [2]–[4]; content-moderation systems pair images and their captions to detect hateful memes [5]; and fraud detectors combine free-text job postings with structured posting metadata to flag deceptive listings [6]. As these multimodal architectures become standard practice, the need to explain their predictions grows. This need has driven a substantial body of work on explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) [7], [8]. Counterfactual explanations are among the most natural and actionable forms of post-hoc justification: a counterfactual x′

reconfigures a sample x so as to flip its prediction while staying close to x and consistent with the data manifold. Falling within the example-based XAI methods, they answer the intuitive, often directly actionable question “what would need to change in x for the model to decide otherwise?”. Well-performing counterfactual generators exist for individ-ual modalities: tabular [9], [10], timeseries [11], [12], text [13], and image [14]. These generators, however, assume a single, homogeneous input space. When a classifier consumes several modalities at once, applying these generators independently per modality fails to yield coherent explanations, as illustrated in Fig. 1. For a multimodal mortality classifier over static de-mographics and laboratory timeseries, a tabular counterfactual generator may return a female survivor of the same age, while a timeseries generator may return signals that reflect decreas-ing N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), a cardiac stress marker, but might not change the increasing trend in Prostate Specific Antigen (PSA), which is a clinical impossibility, as biological females do not produce PSA. This process would result in a chimeric counterfactual that would be locally plausible within each modality, yet globally incoherent and clinically meaningless when considered as a whole. Retrieval-based methods avoid this problem by construction: rather than synthesising a new sample, they return a real training example from the target class. The nearest-unlike-neighbour (NUN) literature exploits this in unimodal set-tings [11], [12], [15], but it has not been extended to the multimodal case. In addition, when using a retrieval-based approach, different choices regarding the search space itself deserve consideration. One could search within a single modality’s feature space, concatenate all modality embeddings and search jointly, or arXiv:2610.31594v1 [cs.LG] 22 Oct 2026 search in the multimodal model’s latent representation. Each choice encodes a different notion of similarity, and how their relative merits compare in terms of the standard counterfactual quality objectives (prediction-flip rate, proximity, sparsity, and plausibility) [16], and of the diversity of the generated set [10] remains to be explored. We address these open questions with CRANE (Counterfac-tual Retrieval via Agnostic Nearest-neighbour Explanations), a post-hoc, model-agnostic framework for nearest-neighbour counterfactual generation for multimodal classifiers. CRANE 1

supports arbitrary combinations of tabular, timeseries, text, and image modalities through retrieval strategies spanning from unimodal search to multimodal fusion, and extends the standard counterfactual quality objectives (i.e., outcome, proximity, sparsity, and plausibility) to heterogeneous input spaces. Among the different strategies, Multimodal Consensus Retrieval (MC-R), which retrieves training instances jointly close to the query in each modality, emerges as the strongest multimodal strategy in four datasets spanning social media, recruitment, content moderation, and clinical prediction, while unimodal retrieval remains competitive when a single modality dominates the decision boundary. II. R ELATED WORK 

Explainability methods for machine learning (ML) span a broad landscape, from feature-attribution methods [17], [18], to saliency maps [19], attention-based explanations [20], and example-based approaches, like counterfactuals, yet the vast majority assume a single, homogeneous input space. This lim-itation is sharpest for counterfactuals, where the explanation must simultaneously specify what would change across all input spaces, not merely within one. Counterfactual explanations were formalised in the context of ML by Wachter et al. [9], and have since grown into a rich research area. For example, DiCE [10] generates a 

diverse set of counterfactuals by adding a determinantal point process term to the objective, so that different explanations suggest different actionable paths, while Dandl et al. [21] proposed a multi-objective formulation that simultaneously optimises proximity, sparsity, plausibility, and actionability without collapsing them into a single weighted sum. A distinct thread within the counterfactual literature avoids optimisation altogether by framing explanation as retrieval, where a real training example from the target class, the NUN, is used directly as the explanation or as a seed for further refinement. Brughmans and Martens [15] formalise this ap-proach in NICE, which exploits the NUN to construct tabular counterfactuals that are guaranteed to exist in the observed data distribution. The retrieval strategy carries an important structural guarantee: since the returned sample was observed in the training data, it is plausible by construction, without any explicit plausibility penalty in an objective function. However, the methods reviewed above are designed exclu-sively for flat tabular or embedding-space inputs; the question

A. Problem Formulation 

We introduce a general formulation for multimodal coun-terfactual generation that is independent of any particular data type, classifier architecture, or application domain. 

Multimodal instance space. Let M ≥ 1 be the number of modalities present in a dataset S. For each modality m ∈{1, . . . , M }, let X m be the corresponding modality space ,encoding the representation format and domain constraints of that modality. The multimodal input space is 

X := X 1 × · · · × X M . (1) A multimodal instance is a tuple x = ( x1, . . . , x M ) ∈ X ,where xm ∈ X m is the component of x in modality m.In principle, X m may represent any data type for which a suitable distance function dm and plausibility estimator pm can be defined. Without loss of generality, in this work we explore four modality types commonly encountered in heterogeneous data pipelines: static feature vectors (tabular), multivariate timeseries, token sequences (text), and two-dimensional pixel arrays (image). Adding additional modalities requires only specifying appropriate dm and pm functions for each one. 

Multimodal classifier. Let Y be a finite label set. A multi-modal classifier is a function f : X → [0 , 1] |Y| , where fy (x) is the score assigned to label y ∈ Y for instance x. The predicted label is ˆy := arg max y∈Y fy (x). CRANE treats f as a black box: only the prediction function is required, and no access to model internals, gradients, or training procedure is assumed. 

Multimodal counterfactual generation. Given a factual in-stance x ∈ X with predicted label ˆy = f (x), a labelled training set D = {(xi, y i)}ni=1 , and a target label y⋆̸ = ˆ y,the goal is to find a counterfactual instance x′ ∈ X such that f (x′) = y⋆ and x′ is of high quality with respect to the multimodal equivalents to the standard counterfactual objectives, as defined in Section III-A1.

On the plausibility of retrieved counterfactuals. A key property of retrieval-based methods is that whenever k = 1 ,the returned counterfactual is a real training instance from the target class and is therefore plausible by construction: it lies on the empirical data manifold, and all its modalities were observed together in a single real-world instance, preserving cross-modal coherence. When k > 1, CRANE always returns the single nearest neighbour as its first candidate; the j-th subsequent candidate (j = 2 , . . . , k ) is the component-wise median over the j nearest neighbours, yielding a sequence of increasingly smoothed prototypes. In general, none of these median candidates corresponds to any observed train-ing instance. The plausibility objective in Eq. (7) quantifies plausibility for all candidates uniformly. 

B. CRANE Retrieval Strategies 

CRANE constructs counterfactual candidates by searching the training set D for the k instances closest to x among those labelled y⋆, and assembling a candidate from those neighbours. The key design choice is in which space the nearest-neighbour search is performed. CRANE provides a family of retrieval strategies, ranging from unimodal search to multimodal fusion. All strategies share a common interface: they take (x, y ⋆, k ) as input and return an ordered list of candidate counterfactuals. All treat the classifier f as a black box. IV. E XPERIMENTS 

A. Datasets 

We evaluate CRANE on four publicly available datasets spanning diverse application domains, modality combinations, and class structures. A summary is given in Table I. 

Sepsis (in-hospital mortality). The Sepsis dataset is extracted from MIMIC-III [22] following the procedure described in [1], which identifies ICU admissions with a confirmed sepsis di-agnosis. Each admission is represented as 24 hourly timesteps over 100 features, partitioned into a timeseries modality of 53 time-varying clinical variables (vital signs and laboratory val-ues) and a tabular modality of 47 static features (demographic and admission data extracted from the first timestep). The classification task is binary in-hospital mortality prediction. The dataset is strongly imbalanced, with a mortality rate of approximately 7.4%. Evaluation follows 5-fold stratified cross-validation; results are pooled across folds and reported as F1 score as the primary metric, and AUC. 

Long COVID Tweets (emotion classification). This dataset 2

consists of Italian tweets discussing long COVID, annotated with four emotion labels drawn from the FEEL-IT taxon-omy [23]: fear, anger, sadness, and joy. Each instance com-prises a raw tweet text field and a tabular modality encoding engagement metadata (likes, retweets, replies, quote counts) and structural tweet features (hashtag and URL counts). The class distribution is imbalanced, with fear (41.6%) and anger (32.9%) dominating and joy (8.8%) forming the minority class. 

Hateful Memes (hate speech detection). The Hateful Memes dataset [24] is a binary classification benchmark for detecting hate speech in internet memes. Each instance consists of a text caption and a 224 ×224 RGB image. The class distribution is moderately imbalanced, with 63.7% non-hateful and 36.3% hateful instances. 

Real or Fake Jobs (fraud detection). This dataset [25] contains job postings labelled as real or fraudulent. Each instance combines a tabular modality (structured fields such as salary range, employment type, and required experience) with three independent text fields: job description, company

B. Experimental Setup 1) Classifier Families and Fusion Strategies: For each dataset, we train classifiers under three fusion strategies: early fusion, intermediate fusion, and late fusion, as defined in Section III-B. For each fusion strategy, we compare two clas-sifier families: a deep family based on multi-layer perceptrons (MLP) with task-trained branches, and a non-deep family using tree-based ensembles (Random Forest and Gradient Boosting) or, for timeseries branches, the ROCKET transform with Logistic Regression [26]. This design yields a range of classifier types that stress-tests CRANE’s model-agnostic claim: the IF-NN retrieval strategy requires a deep neural classifier with accessible penultimate activations and is there-fore evaluated only on deep models, while all other CRANE strategies are evaluated uniformly across both families. Text is encoded using pre-trained transformer models: XLM-RoBERTa for Long COVID Tweets, DistilBERT for Real or Fake Jobs, and BERT or MiniLM for Hateful Memes. Images in Hateful Memes are encoded using ResNet-50, EfficientNet-B0, or ViT-B/16. For early-fusion classifiers, all encoders are frozen (no fine-tuning); for intermediate-fusion classifiers, the transformer backbone is fine-tuned end-to-end. Class imbalance is addressed via inverse-frequency weighting for all classifiers. For training we use a 15% hold-out valida-tion split for early stopping and hyperparameter tuning. 

2) CRANE Configuration: Each CRANE retrieval strategy is configured with k = 20 , returning 20 candidates per query. For MC-R, the per-modality search pool is set to 

ksearch = 200 neighbours per modality prior to intersection, ensuring that the consensus set is non-empty for all queries in our datasets. For each dataset we run a systematic ablation over all valid combinations of encoder and distance metric per modality (as defined in Section III-B), yielding 6, 22, 16, and 84 combinations for Sepsis, Long COVID Tweets, Real or Fake Jobs, and Hateful Memes, respectively. The results are reported as averages across all ablation combinations. 

3) CRANE Strategies per Dataset: Not all retrieval strate-gies are applicable to every dataset and classifier combination. IF-NN is evaluated only on deep classifiers, as described in Section IV-B; for datasets where a modality is absent (e.g., no image modality in Sepsis, Tweets, or Jobs), the corresponding unimodal strategy is not evaluated. C. Evaluation Protocol 

We evaluate each retrieval strategy using the four quality objectives introduced in Section III-A1. All metrics are re-ported on a lower-is-better scale, where 0.0 denotes a perfect score on that objective. All four metrics are averaged across the five cross-validation folds using pooled test queries. For each dataset and strategy, we report the mean and standard deviation across all ablation combinations, providing a mea-sure of sensitivity to encoder and distance metric choice. Counterfactual generation is performed on all test instances that the explained classifier predicts to belong to an undesired class; no subsampling is applied. For binary datasets, the target label y⋆ is the single opposite class. For Long COVID Tweets, where four emotion classes are present, we focus on the sadness →joy direction, as these two classes represent the most semantically opposed poles in the dataset; the remaining class pairs (fear and anger) do not have a clearly defined opposite and are excluded from the counterfactual evaluation. Unimodal retrieval. Unimodal generators achieve strong proximity and plausibility because they retrieve complete, coherent training instances: cross-modal coherence is pre-served by construction. On Sepsis, TimeSeriesNN achieves near-perfect outcome (0.021) because the temporal clinical trajectory dominates the classifier’s decision boundary, con-sistent with prior findings on this dataset [1]; the retrieved in-stance’s static features jointly reinforce the flip. On imbalanced datasets (Sepsis, Jobs), performance is partly aided by the large majority-class retrieval pool; practitioners should expect degradation when the target class is the minority. Conversely, on Hateful Memes, TextNN (0.070) substantially outperforms ImageNN (0.182), suggesting that the text modality dominates the classifier’s decision boundary for meme classification, consistent with prior findings on this benchmark [24]. 

Latent-space retrieval. IF-NN consistently fails to produce valid counterfactuals across all datasets and fusion strategies, even at k = 50 . The penultimate layer of a deep classifier is optimised for discriminative separation rather than within-class clustering, so neighbours in that space are not reliably from the target class. This limitation is specific to current training objectives; architectures that explicitly optimise for clustered latent representations may overcome it. 

Classifier quality. Counterfactual quality is partly a func-tion of classifier quality: a well-separating classifier assigns distinct predictions to distinct classes, so retrieved target-class instances are more likely to flip the prediction. This is consistent with the results: on Jobs, where all classifiers achieve F1 above 0.88, every generator except IF-NN reaches near-zero outcome, whereas on Hateful Memes, which has the weakest classifiers in the benchmark (F1 0.669-0.713), outcome is substantially higher across all generators 

MC-R as the recommended multimodal strategy. MC-R is the strongest multimodal generator overall, achieving the best or joint-best outcome on three of four datasets and perfect outcome on Real or Fake Jobs (0.000). On Jobs, MC-R also achieves the lowest sparsity in the entire benchmark (0.137), indicating that the intersection mechanism identifies target-class instances that differ from the query in very few features, a desirable property for actionable explanations in fraud de-tection. Its intersection mechanism identifies instances jointly close to the query across all modalities, grounding counterfac-tuals in the joint training distribution in a way that MPS, which synthesises per-modality prototypes independently, does not guarantee. Its outcome saturates at k = 5 , making it efficient in practice. The choice of k also governs a trade-off between coherence and diversity [10]: at k = 1 , the proposed candidate is a real, coherent training instance; increasing k yields a larger and more diverse candidate set, with the first candidate always retaining full cross-modal coherence. VII. C ONCLUSION 

We presented CRANE, a post-hoc, model-agnostic frame-work for nearest-neighbour counterfactual generation for mul-timodal classifiers, supporting arbitrary modality combina-tions. Its extensibility to new modalities makes it naturally applicable to domains, such as clinical decision support, where normative frameworks require ML tools to be explain-able [27]. Across four datasets and three fusion strategies, MC-R emerges as the strongest multimodal strategy, combining the strongest outcome scores in the benchmark, joint-distribution grounding, and computational efficiency. Future work includes comparative evaluation against the baselines described in Section II, human evaluation of counterfactual quality with domain experts, extension to additional clinical datasets, and exploration of generative counterfactual strategies within the CRANE evaluation protocol.
