CONVEX MIXED-INTEGER PROGRAMMING FOR INVARIANT PREDICTIONS FROM MULTI-ENVIRONMENT DATA 

XIAOZHU ZHANG 1, RAHUL MAZUMDER 2, AND ARMEEN TAEB 1

Abstract. Heterogeneous data provide an opportunity to identify causal predictors by revealing which predictive relationships remain invariant. Under a linear model, we introduce a least-squares coefficient-and-risk (LSCR) invariance principle, requiring both ordinary least squares coefficients and prediction risks to remain constant across environments. Our model neither specifies an in-tervention mechanism nor assumes a structural causal model for the entire system. We develop an invariance score that quantifies departures from LSCR invariance and use its optimization to construct two complementary procedures for causal predictor identification. First, we propose an estimator that jointly minimizes prediction risk and invariance penalty, and establish non-asymptotic guarantees for estimation and recovery of the causal parents under suitable identifiability conditions. Second, without requiring full identifiability, we develop a testing procedure that minimizes the in-variance score over all predictor sets excluding a given variable, enabling valid causal discoveries with asymptotic error control via a multiplier bootstrap. Both procedures admit convex mixed-integer optimization formulations that avoid exhaustive subset enumeration while providing certificates of optimality. Simulations and real data experiments demonstrate substantial statistical and compu-tational improvements over existing methods. 

Keywords : discrete optimization, invariance learning, local causal discovery, partial identification 1. Introduction 

Identifying the causal predictors of a target response is an important problem in causal inference. However, data from a single observational distribution generally cannot distinguish causal predictors from noncausal variables that are associated with the response. Data collected across varying settings and conditions, broadly called environments , offer an additional source of information for causal discovery. Although interventions or distributional changes may alter the distributions of covariates and their associations with the response, the causal mechanism governing the response remains invariant across environments, provided it is not directly intervened upon. This idea underlies the invariant causal prediction framework of Peters et al. (2016). Let S⋆ denote the causal parent set of a target response Y . If the causal mechanism for Y is unchanged, then the conditional distribution of Y given XS⋆ remains invariant across environments. This establishes a connection between invariance and causality: the true causal parent set must yield an invariant conditional distribution, and any predictor set exhibiting such invariance is a natural candidate for the causal parent set. Motivated by this connection, Peters et al. (2016) identify invariant predictor sets across environments and take their intersection to recover a subset of S⋆.Since the work by Peters et al. (2016), exploiting invariance across environments has become an important strategy for causal discovery. One line of research estimates invariant predictive models through optimization, typically by incorporating a tractable property of invariance into the estimation objective. Under suitable identifiability conditions, these methods can recover the true causal model. Arjovsky et al. (2019) provide an

early example with invariant risk minimization (IRM), which seeks a data representation such that the same prediction rule minimizes the risk in every environment. However, IRM does not generally guarantee recovery of the causal parent set, even in linear settings, and its practical implementation relies on highly heuristic nonconvex optimization that can be sensitive to initialization and tuning parameters (Kamath et al., 2021, Rojas-Carulla et al., 2018). Other approaches focus on estimating causal regression coefficients. Rothenh¨ ausler et al. (2019) propose Causal Dantzig, which estimates causal coefficients in linear structural models using invariant moment con-ditions under additive interventions. More recently, Wang et al. (2024) developed NegDRO, a nonconvex minimax approach that exploits invariance of prediction risks to identify causal coefficients. Although both Causal Dantzig and NegDRO can accommodate hidden confounding, they rely on specific additive interven-tion mechanisms and assume a linear structural causal model for the entire system, which may limit their applicability. In contrast, EILLS, introduced by Fan et al. (2024), and CoCo, proposed by Yin et al. (2024), do not require a specific intervention mechanism. Both approaches exploit the first order optimality conditions of least squares regression across environments to identify causal coefficients. EILLS incorporates an invariance penalty into the regression objective, together with indicators for selected features, whereas CoCo relies on continuous nonconvex optimization. Another method is Bayesian invariant prediction (BIP), proposed by Wu et al. (2025), which uses latent binary indicators to select variables whose conditional relationship with the response remains invariant across environments. Among these methods, only EILLS and BIP explicitly select subsets of predictors when identifying an invariant model, while sparse formulations of Causal Dantzig perform variable selection through ℓ1 regulariza-tion. However, the exact implementations of EILLS and BIP require exhaustive enumeration over candidate predictor subsets, making them computationally prohibitive even in moderate dimensions. Although their approximate implementations alleviate this computational burden, they lack any optimality guarantees. In contrast, ℓ1-regularized Causal Dantzig admits a computationally tractable convex formulation, but its reliance on a convex relaxation of variable selection may lead to shrinkage bias and inaccurate support recovery. The methods discussed above, including IRM, Causal Dantzig, NegDRO, EILLS, CoCo, and BIP, generally require sufficient intervention heterogeneity to guarantee recovery of the causal parent set. Without such heterogeneity, the variables discovered by these methods may include noncausal predictors, and recovery of even a subset of the true causal parent set is not guaranteed. An alternative line of work seeks to identify a subset of the causal parent set while controlling type-I error, without requiring full identifiability. As discussed earlier, Peters et al. (2016) introduce invariant causal prediction (ICP), which tests invariance over all candidate predictor subsets and takes the intersection of the accepted sets. However, exhaustive enumeration of predictor subsets becomes computationally prohibitive as the dimension increases. To address this limitation, Martinet et al. (2022) formulate causal predictor discovery as a multiple testing problem instead of exhaustive subset enumeration. These tests use Wasserstein variance minimization (WVM) to assess whether the conditional distribution of the response can remain invariant across environments when a given variable is excluded. However, WVM remains computationally expensive. Moreover, both ICP and WVM can have limited statistical power in identifying causal predictors, particularly at moderate sample sizes. 1.1. Gaps and our contributions. The preceding discussion highlights two related but distinct tasks in invariant causal prediction: (T1) When S⋆ is identifiable, how can we accurately recover the entire causal parent set? (T2) When S⋆ is not fully identifiable, how can we identify a subset of S⋆ while controlling false causal discoveries? As shown in Table 1, existing approaches either do not explicitly perform variable selection or generally focus on only one of these two goals. To address both tasks, we introduce an invariance score that quantifies the degree of invariance of a predictor set S, with smaller scores indicating greater invariance. For Task (T1), we incorporate this score as a regularization term in the risk minimization objective to encourage the selection of an invariant predictor set. We establish identifiability conditions for S⋆ and show that the resulting estimator 3

recovers S⋆ under suitable regularity conditions. For Task (T2), we adopt the multiple testing framework of Martinet et al. (2022). Specifically, for each variable k, we search for the minimum invariance score among all predictor sets that exclude k. If even this minimum score is sufficiently large, it provides evidence that no invariant predictor set can exclude k. Consequently, k must belong to the intersection of all invariant predictor sets and hence to the true causal parent set S⋆. We further develop a multiplier bootstrap procedure to approximate the distribution of the minimum score and establish asymptotic type-I error control for false causal discoveries.

To avoid exhaustive subset enu-meration, we develop convex mixed-integer programming (MIP) formulations for the invariance score and its subsequent optimization problems. Subset selection is naturally represented through binary variables, and the resulting formulations have convex continuous relaxations, enabling efficient optimization and certificates of global optimality. Unlike existing approaches ICP, EILLS and BIP, our MIP framework allows the use of branch-and-bound algorithms that (i) efficiently prune candidate subsets to reduce the computational burden and (ii) return solutions with guaranteed optimality gaps below a prespecified tolerance. We further develop a scalable coordinate descent heuristic that efficiently finds solutions of high quality. When incorporated into the MIP framework, these solutions can be equipped with certificates of optimality gaps. Our invariance score is constructed based on the least-squares coefficient and risk (LSCR) invariance princi-ple for linear models. This principle requires both the population ordinary least squares (OLS) coefficients and the corresponding OLS risks to remain invariant across environments. Unlike Causal Dantzig and NegDRO, neither the linear structural causal model we consider nor the LSCR principle imposes specific restrictions on the intervention mechanisms, provided that the causal mechanism for the response remains unchanged. Moreover, tests based on the LSCR principle demonstrate greater statistical power than ICP and WVM in our experiments. This improvement can be attributed, in part, to the stronger invariance restrictions imposed by LSCR compared with tests on the regression residuals used in ICP, which can fail to detect changes in regression coefficients. In contrast, WVM involves a highly nonconvex optimization problem, making it sus-ceptible to suboptimal solutions that can compromise its empirical performance. Our MIP framework can also be extended to settings with latent confounding by adopting a different invariance score. Finally, we demonstrate the performance of our methods using both synthetic data and real data collected from a tightly controlled physical system. First, our estimator for Task (T1) achieves substantially higher accuracy in recovering causal parents than competing methods as the dimension increases. Second, our proce-dure for Task (T2) consistently achieves higher statistical power than competing methods while maintaining type-I error control. This section reviews three additional lines of related research not discussed earlier and highlights their connections to and distinctions from our proposed framework. 

Distributional robustness and domain generalization. Beyond causal predictor selection, invariance has also been widely used to improve prediction under distributional shifts. One line of work focuses on identifying invariant predictor subsets that generalize to unseen environments. Rojas-Carulla et al. (2018) study this problem in causal transfer learning, while Pfister et al. (2021) combine regression models based on invariant predictor subsets to improve predictive performance across environments. Another line of work focuses on distributional robustness. Meinshausen and B¨ uhlmann (2015) introduce maximin effects for robust regression with heterogeneous data, while anchor regression (Rothenh¨ ausler et al., 2021) and DRIG (Shen et al., 2026) aim to achieve robust prediction under distributional shifts caused by interventions. For prob-abilistic prediction, Henzi et al. (2025) propose invariant probabilistic prediction (IPP), which uses proper scoring rules to encourage invariance of prediction risks across environments. While these approaches primar-ily aim to improve prediction under distributional shifts, our work focuses on identifying causal predictors through invariance across environments. 

Beyond linear invariant prediction. Another line of work extends invariant causal prediction beyond linear models. Heinze-Deml et al. (2018) develop nonlinear and nonparametric versions of ICP using statistical tests for invariance across environments. Kook et al. (2025) extend ICP to general response types, including categorical, count, and censored outcomes, through transformation models. More recently, Gu et al. (2025) propose focused adversarial invariant regularization, which uses neural networks and adversarial optimization to identify invariant predictors in nonparametric regression models. These approaches extend invariant causal learning to more flexible model classes, whereas our work focuses on linear models to develop mixed-integer convex formulations with statistical and optimization guarantees. Nonetheless, we describe how our MIP formulations can be extended to handle nonlinear additive models in Section 8. 

Mixed-integer optimization in statistical learning. Recent advances in mixed-integer programming have made combinatorial optimization increasingly practical for statistical learning. Bertsimas et al. (2016) develop a mixed-integer optimization approach to best subset selection, using initial high-quality solutions to accelerate branch-and-bound algorithms. Bertsimas and Van Parys (2020) further introduce a binary convex reformulation and cutting-plane algorithms for scalable ℓ0-regularized regression. Hazimeh and Mazumder (2020) develop efficient coordinate descent and local combinatorial search algorithms for best subset selection. Similar ideas have also been applied to other statistical problems, including multi-task learning (Behdin et al., 2026) and Bayesian network structure learning (Manzour et al., 2021). These studies demonstrate that exploiting problem structures can substantially improve the computational efficiency of discrete optimization. Convex mixed-integer programming has also been used to learn entire causal graphs (K¨ u¸ c¨ ukyavuz et al., 2023, Manzour et al., 2021, Xu et al., 2025, Zhang et al., 2025), although this is a substantially more challenging problem that typically requires modeling assumptions on the entire system of variables. 1.3. 2. Setup: causality and invariant predictions from heterogeneous data 

2.1. Linear structural causal model. We suppose we have access to observations of variables under differ-ent environments, such as experimental conditions in which some of the variables may have been manipulated, that is, received interventions. To represent this setting, we consider covariates X ∈ Rp, and response vari-able Y ∈ R. The interventions on these variables take values in the set E; each e ∈ E represents a different environment that generates the random vector ( Xe, Y e). We posit that for every e ∈ E , the covariates Xe

and the response Y e are related via the following linear structural causal model (SCM) for some distribution 

F:

Y e = β⋆⊤Xe + εe, supp( β⋆) = S⋆, εe ⊥ XeS⋆ , εe ∼ F , E[εe] = 0 .(1) Here, β⋆ ∈ Rp is a vector encoding causal relationships between the covariates and the response variable, namely β⋆j̸ = 0 if covariate Xej is a causal parent of Y e in the graph among ( Xe, Y e). The subset S⋆ ⊆{1, 2, . . . , p } represents the covariates that are causal parents of Y e, and the coefficients β⋆S⋆ represent the strength of these causal effects. The SCM (1) thus posits that the structure and strength of the causal parents of Y do not change across e ∈ E . The noise variable εe ∈ R is assumed to be independent of the causal parents 

XeS⋆ . This independence condition corresponds to a causal sufficiency assumption, namely that there is no unobserved confounding simultaneously affecting the covariates and the response variable. In Appendix D, we discuss extensions to the setting with latent confounding. Importantly, εe can have dependencies with other coordinates of Xe outside the index set S⋆. Such dependencies are expected when some of the covariates are descendants of Y in the causal graph. The condition that the distribution of εe remains the same across all e ∈ E means that the response variable does not receive a direct intervention, although its marginal distribution could still be changed due to interventions on its causal ancestors. Finally, the zero-mean condition on εe can be assumed without loss of generality, as a nonzero mean can be absorbed into the term β⋆⊤Xe by augmenting the covariates with a constant intercept variable. The parameter β⋆ and the distribution F are not assumed to be known. For every e ∈ E , we assume that we have ne independent and identically distributed observations of (Xe, Y e) from the SCM (1). We further assume that the samples are independent across environments. 2.2. LSCR-invariant prediction models. The SCM (1) exhibits important invariances that we highlight in this section. We compare the LSCR invariance principle with other notions of invariance used in related work. First, the LSCR principle is essentially the same as that used in ICP (Peters et al., 2016), but the two methods employ different testing procedures. For each candidate predictor set S, ICP fits a pooled linear regression and tests whether the residual means and variances are invariant across environments using t-tests and F -tests with Bonferroni correction. However, differences in environment specific regression coefficients may not manifest as differences in the means or variances of the pooled residuals. Consequently, for a subset S, ICP can fail to detect violations of invariance in the conditional distribution Y e|XeS even at the population level, as illustrated in the following example.

3.3. Exact and scalable heuristic solvers. We discuss two approaches for solving the convex MIPs (8) and (9). 

An exact solver. One option is to use the mixed-integer optimization solver Gurobi , which can certify global optimality up to prescribed numerical tolerances. The solver uses a branch-and-bound framework. For both formulations, relaxing the binary constraints gj ∈ { 0, 1} to gj ∈ [0 , 1] yields a convex quadratic program whose optimal value provides a lower bound on the corresponding mixed-integer problem. These lower bounds are used to prune the search tree: if the bound at a node is no smaller than the objective value of the best feasible solution found so far, then that node and all of its descendants can be discarded. Consequently, the solver can certify optimality without enumerating all 2 p possible support configurations. Lower and upper bounds also provide a certificate of solution quality. The best feasible solution, referred to as the incumbent , provides an upper bound on the optimal objective value, whereas the convex relaxations provide a global lower bound. Their difference, referred to as the optimality gap , quantifies the suboptimality of the incumbent solution. In particular, the incumbent is globally optimal when this gap is zero, up to numerical tolerances. Several practical strategies can improve the performance of this exact approach. A high-quality warm start can provide a strong initial upper bound and thereby accelerate pruning; such a warm start can be obtained using the coordinate descent procedure described next. One may also configure the solver to prioritize improvements in the global lower bound, for example, by setting MIPFocus=2 in Gurobi . Finally, to reduce computation time, an early-stopping tolerance τ early ≥ 0 may be imposed on the optimality gap. If the algorithm terminates with a gap no greater than τ early , the returned solution is guaranteed to be within τ early

Further details on the model, optimization formulation, and numerical experiments are provided in Appendix D. 9. Discussion 

We propose a unified MIP framework for invariant causal variable selection and inference across heteroge-neous environments. Based on the LSCR invariance principle, our framework addresses both causal parent recovery under identifiability and causal discovery with type-I error control when full identification is not possible. Our methods enjoy both statistical and optimization guarantees, and experiments on synthetic and real data demonstrate their effectiveness. Our work opens several directions for future research. First, although our framework can be extended to nonlinear additive models through basis expansions, approximation errors may prevent the LSCR invariance principle from holding exactly. Developing more general methods for nonlinear causal effects while preserving computational tractability and optimization guarantees would be valuable. Second, our identification results 26 

depend on the heterogeneity of the available environments. Investigating how to design informative inter-ventions or select environments to improve causal identification is an important direction. Finally, although our MIP formulations avoid explicit subset enumeration, solving larger problems to optimality can still be computationally challenging. Developing specialized branch-and-bound algorithms that further exploit the invariance score structure may substantially improve computational efficiency.

Our coordinate descent algorithm updates the support one feature at a time. B.2. Gaining more power. Although the two components of the invariance score I(S), namely the discrep-ancies in the OLS coefficients and residual variances, are sufficient to characterize LSCR invariance, incorpo-rating additional measures of discrepancy across environments can further improve the power of the resulting test. We additionally consider the following two conditions for a model β: (i) E[Y e − Xeβ] = E[Y f − Xf β] for all e, f ∈ E ; and (ii) E[Xej (Y e − Xeβ)] = E[Xfj (Y f − Xf β)] for all j ∈ supp( β) and e, f ∈ E . Both conditions are automatically satisfied by any LSCR-invariant model and therefore do not alter the collection SLSCR (E)characterized by the LSCR principle. Nevertheless, incorporating their empirical discrepancies into the in-variance score can provide additional information for distinguishing non-invariant models, thereby improving the power to detect plausible causal predictors. 34

Appendix G. Details on experiments 

We first describe the synthetic data generation and intervention mechanisms we used. We generate data from linear Gaussian structural equation models over p covariates X = ( X1, . . . , X p) and a response Y . We first sample a common topological ordering of the p + 1 variables and place Y at a prespecified position in this ordering. The causal parent set S⋆ of Y is sampled from the variables preceding Y , with |S⋆| drawn between 

smin and smax . For j ∈ S⋆, the invariant coefficients are sampled as 

β⋆j = ξj Uj , ξj ∈ {− 1, 1}, Uj ∼ Unif (l⋆, u ⋆),

independently. Thus, in every environment, 

Y = X

> j∈S⋆

β⋆j Xj + εY , εY ∼ N (0 , σ 2 

> Y

),

For the remaining structural equations, coefficients are sampled independently as a random sign multiplied by a uniform magnitude on [ l, u ]. The parameter pact controls the sparsity of the covariate DAG: for each pair of variables consistent with the topological ordering, an edge is included with probability pact . Conditional on the resulting structural coefficients, observations are generated recursively according to the common topological ordering. Under the soft-intervention mechanism , the DAG structure is sampled once and shared across all environments. We first generate a baseline coefficient matrix A(0) . The observational environment uses this baseline mechanism. For each subsequent environment e > 0, every active edge into a covariate Xj

is independently selected with probability presample ; selected coefficients are then redrawn from the same signed uniform distribution, while the edge support remains unchanged. The noise standard deviations of the covariates are sampled once from Unif (σX, min , σ X, max ) and are also shared across environments. Hence, environments differ through perturbations of existing structural coefficients while preserving both the DAG support and the noise distributions. Under the all-intervention mechanism , the topological ordering and the response mechanism are still shared across environments, but the mechanisms generating covariates are resampled independently in every environment. Specifically, for each environment and for every admissible pair of covariates, an edge is included independently with probability pact , and its coefficient is sampled afresh from the signed uniform distribution. In addition, under the all-intervention mechanism, the covariate noise standard deviations are independently resampled in each environment from σ(e)

∼ Unif (σX, min , σ X, max ), whereas σY remains fixed. 53
