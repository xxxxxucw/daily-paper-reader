## Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) can exploit shortcuts, i.e., correlations present in training data that may not hold at test time. This can lead to poor predictions when correlations shift. Most existing solutions for removing shortcuts operate at training time, requiring labeled training data, model weight access, and significant computational resources. Here, we study the problem of test-time shortcut removal. We propose a family of counterfactual test-time augmentation (CTTA) methods: given an example, each method uses an auxiliary model to generate counterfactual edits in which the shortcut value is changed, and then takes a weighted average of the LLM’s predictions across these edits. We prove that the optimal shortcut-invariant predictor is a member of this CTTA family, and we characterize its weights. The weights depend on the training distribution, and therefore are, in general, not identifiable from the trained model and unlabeled test data. However, we present assumptions under which the weights can be obtained using the token probabilities of the trained LLM, and propose tests to detect violations of these assumptions. We use these tests to develop Select-CTTA, an aggregate method that selects among CTTA methods. Empirically, we find that Select-CTTA outperforms test-time baselines and approaches the performance of models fine-tuned on shortcut-balanced data. 

## 1 Introduction 

Despite impressive performance on a range of tasks, large language models (LLMs) remain brittle. They have been shown to abandon correct answers when users push back (Sharma et al., 2024), change answers when multiple-choice options are reordered (Pezeshkpour & Hruschka, 2024), and rely on demographic stereotypes when making decisions about people (Tamkin et al., 2023). Each of these failures is related to a common issue: LLMs are prone to relying on shortcuts , i.e., correlations in training data that may not hold at inference time. As a result, LLMs use heuristics, such as prefer option A and agree with the user , that reduce training loss but lead to errors during deployment (Zheng et al., 2024; Sharma et al., 2024). Reliance on shortcuts is a major impediment to LLM safety and reliability. Most existing methods for removing shortcuts in language models are designed to be applied at training time. However, training an LLM requires many resources, including labeled training data, access to model weights, and substantial compute. Users often lack these resources. Hence, here we study a different approach: removing shortcuts from LLMs at test-time without access to model weights or labeled data. 

We propose a family of test-time shortcut removal methods that we call counterfactual test-time augmentation (CTTA). Given a test example, each CTTA method uses an auxiliary model to create counterfactual edits in which the shortcut value is changed. We then take a weighted average of the

LLM’s predictions across the edits. As we show in this work, if the chosen weights do not depend on the shortcut value observed in the example, the resulting predictor is shortcut-invariant. Among the set of predictors that are shortcut invariant, we want the one with the lowest error, i.e., the 

optimal shortcut-invariant predictor . We prove that under a common model of shortcut learning, the optimal shortcut-invariant predictor is a member of the CTTA family. The optimal weights depend on the training distribution and therefore, in general, cannot be identified without access to the training data. However, we present a set of assumptions under which the weights can be obtained using the observable token probabilities of the trained LLM. We call the resulting method Model-Weighted CTTA. Since the assumptions needed to recover the optimal weights are not always met in practice, we also study simple fixed weighting schemes, such as uniform weighting, that do not require a weight estimation step. We prove fixed weighting schemes have a desirable property: they achieve the same AUROC as the optimal shortcut-invariant predictor, although they are miscalibrated. To select among CTTA methods, we propose Select-CTTA, a procedure that performs diagnostic tests to detect violations of the Model-Weighted CTTA assumptions. When the checks pass, Select-CTTA selects Model-Weighted CTTA; otherwise, it selects uniform weights. We evaluate the CTTA methods on two text classification benchmarks and six LLMs, spanning multi-ple model families and sizes. Select-CTTA consistently achieves the best or near-best performance among CTTA methods and outperforms test-time calibration baselines. In many cases, it approaches the performance of models trained on gold-standard balanced data, while requiring substantially fewer resources. Our contributions are: (1) We propose a family of Counterfactual Test Time Augmentation (CTTA) methods as a solution for test-time shortcut removal in LLMs. (2) We theoretically analyze the optimality of our proposed methods, providing an understanding of when they match (and fail to match) the performance of the optimal shortcut-invariant predictor. (3) We introduce Select-CTTA, a procedure that adaptively selects between model-based and uniform CTTA weights using label-free diagnostic tests. (4) We demonstrate that Select-CTTA outperforms test-time baselines and often approaches the performance of fine-tuning on shortcut-balanced data at substantially lower cost.

## 3 Methods 

To address the problem of test-time shortcut removal, we propose a family of methods that we call Counterfactual Test-Time Augmentation (CTTA). We first define this family and show that the optimal shortcut-invariant predictor belongs to it (Section 3.1). We then introduce Model-Weighted CTTA and establish conditions under which it recovers the optimal predictor (Section 3.2). Next, we analyze Fixed-Weight CTTA and show that it achieves the same AUROC as the optimal predictor, despite generally being miscalibrated (Section 3.3). 3.3 Fixed-Weight CTTA 

Since the assumptions of Model-Weighted CTTA might not hold in practice, we also consider Fixed-Weight CTTA methods, which do not need a weight estimation step. We define fixed weights as those that do not depend on the input example x, i.e., ws(x) = ws for all x ∈ X . Two natural choices are 

Uniform CTTA , which assigns equal weight to each shortcut value, ws = 1 /|S| , and Neutral CTTA ,which places all weight on a designated neutral value s0, i.e., ws0 = 1 and ws = 0 for s̸ = s0. 3.4 Select-CTTA 

Model-Weighted CTTA can recover the optimal shortcut-invariant predictor, but only under assump-tions that may fail in practice. We therefore introduce Select-CTTA, a procedure for choosing between Model-Weighted and fixed-weight CTTA at test time. It uses a batch of unlabeled test data to perform three diagnostic tests. The first examines the strength of the shortcut, while the second two provide complementary diagnostics for violations of the Model-Weighted CTTA assumptions. If all tests pass, it uses Model-Weighted CTTA. Otherwise, it uses Uniform CTTA. We prefer Uniform to Neutral CTTA because identifying a truly neutral shortcut value may be infeasible in practice. We provide details on how to implement the tests in Appendix B. 

Test 1: Shortcut Strength. If the shortcut has a weak association with the label, then λ(s) is small, and fixed weight predictors are near-optimal (see Equation 3). In this regime, estimating the optimal weights offers little potential benefit and can introduce additional error, so we choose Uniform CTTA. Ideally, to assess shortcut strength, we’d examine λ(s). However, the values λ(s) are not directly estimatable because f ∗ is unknown. Instead, we estimate shortcut strength using the model’s predictions on counterfactual edits. By Equation 2, for any s, s ′ ∈ S ,

logit Mc(τ (x, s )) − logit Mc(τ (x, s ′)) = λ(s) − λ(s′).

Test 2: Weight Collapse. We check whether the Model-Weighted CTTA weights have collapsed, meaning that they systematically assign most of the weight to the same shortcut value across examples. Though collapse does not necessarily indicate an assumption violation, assumption violations can lead to collapse. A violation of (A1) can cause collapse if the discrepancy between the LLM’s likelihood and the true training probability varies systematically with the shortcut value. A violation of (A2) can cause collapse if the editor systematically produces more probable edits for one shortcut value. For example, edits to one value may involve only a small, natural substitution, while edits to another may require longer or less fluent rewrites. ## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Tasks 

We examine two different binary classification tasks. Data processing details are in Appendix C. 6Math Grading (Sycophancy). LLMs have been shown to exhibit sycophantic behavior when providing feedback on math solutions (Sharma et al., 2024). In this task, the input X is a problem and a proposed solution, and the label Y ∈ { 0, 1} indicates whether the solution is correct. We use the PRM800K dataset (Lightman et al., 2023), which contains problems from MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021) with model-generated solutions and human-provided correctness labels. As the shortcut 

S, we insert one of three opinion statements at the end of the input: I think this solution is correct , Ithink this solution is incorrect , and I’m unsure whether this solution is correct .

Occupation Classification (Gender). We use the Bias in Bios dataset (De-Arteaga et al., 2019), a text classification benchmark introduced to study gender bias in occupation prediction. We use biographies labeled as attorney or paralegal. Each input X is a biography, the label Y ∈ { 0, 1} is occupation, and the shortcut S ∈ { man , woman } is the gender of the biography subject. 

4.2 Experimental Settings Training Distributions. In our first experiments, we fine-tune models on data with a controlled shortcut strength given by p ∈ [0 .5, 1] . For a designated shortcut value s, we set Ptrain (S = s |

Y = 0) = p and Ptrain (S = s | Y = 1) = 1 − p. For each label setting, we split the remaining probability mass evenly among the remaining shortcut values. Our main experiments use p = 0 .95 

and Ptrain (Y = 1) = 0 .5. Appendix E.2 reports results across a range of shortcut strengths. 

Test Distributions. For each training distribution, we reverse the shortcut-label association at test time: 

Ptest (S = s | Y = 1) = Ptrain (S = s | Y = 0) and Ptest (S = s | Y = 0) = Ptrain (S = s | Y = 1) .We use Ptest (Y = 1) = 0 .5. For the math task, we use 2,600 examples, and for the occupation task, we use 582 examples. Appendix E.1 reports results under milder distribution shifts. 

Models. We use six instruction-tuned LLMs from three open-weight model families: Gemma 3 (4B and 12B; Gemma Team et al., 2025), Mistral (7B and Nemo 12B; Jiang et al., 2023), and Qwen 2.5 (1.5B and 7B; Yang et al., 2024). 

Model Settings. We evaluate each LLM under three training conditions: all-tokens finetuning , where we fine-tune the model to predict both the input tokens and classification label; completion-only finetuning , where we fine-tune only on the classification label; and zero-shot , where we do not fine-tune at all. 

Baselines. We compare the CTTA methods to each other and to existing test-time methods. For the math task, we consider two Neutral CTTA variants: unsure , which uses the unsure opinion statement, and removal , which omits an opinion statement. For occupation, Neutral CTTA uses a language model to remove gender references. In our zero-shot experiments, we compare to Out-of-Context (OOC) prompting (Cotta & Maddison, 2024), a shortcut-robustness method designed for the zero-shot setting. OOC is similar to Uniform CTTA, but it generates edits with the predictor LLM itself, and its editing prompt instructs the model to preserve a stratification variable, which in the anti-causal setting of Figure 1 is the LLM’s predicted label. We also compare to Batch Calibration (BC) (Zhou et al., 2024) and Contextual Calibration (CC) (Zhao et al., 2021), two test-time calibration methods. They are not designed specifically for shortcut removal, so we additionally introduce shortcut-conditioned variants of each, which perform calibration separately for each shortcut value. In our finetuning experiments, we also report the performance of models fine-tuned on balanced data. We consider this a reference rather than a baseline, since it assumes access to labeled training data and model weights. 

Counterfactual Editor. For the math task, the shortcut edits are straightforward and can be applied in a rule-based manner. For the occupation task, we perform gender edits using Claude Opus (details in Appendix D.6). 

Hyperparameters. In Select-CTTA, we set δstr = 0 .5, δcol = 0 .8, and δalign = 0 .5. During model inference, we use a temperature of 1 in all experiments. 

Metrics. We evaluate how well predictions rank examples using AUROC. We measure calibration using Expected Calibration Error (ECE), with results for additional metrics in Appendix E.3.

Model-Weighted CTTA relies on the assumption that the classification mode and the likelihood mode of the LLM reflect the same training distribution, which is likely when input and label tokens are optimized jointly. Conversely, this assumption is likely to fail when only the label token is optimized. In line with this, we observe that Uniform outperforms Model-Weighted CTTA in the completion-only setting. The strong AUROC of Uniform and Neutral CTTA is also consistent with our theoretical result that fixed-weight CTTA obtains the optimal ranking. These differences across training settings motivate selecting adaptively between Model-Weighted and Uniform CTTA. Select-CTTA does this effectively: it consistently matches or closely tracks the stronger of the two methods, and achieves the best overall performance. Select-CTTA is also competitive with models trained on balanced data, despite requiring neither labeled training data nor model weight access at test time. In some settings, CTTA exceeds the balanced training reference; we discuss this in Appendix E.4.

## 5 Related Work 

Reliance on shortcuts is a well-documented failure mode of deep learning models (Beery et al., 2018; Geirhos et al., 2020), including language models (McCoy et al., 2019; Du et al., 2023). Most existing shortcut removal methods are applied at training time. They include balancing and re-weighting the training data (Sagawa et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2021; Idrissi et al., 2022); training with regularization penalties that encourage invariance to the shortcut (Arjovsky et al., 2019; Veitch et al., 2021; Makar et al., 2022); and counterfactual data augmentation (CDA), which augments the training set with edited examples in which the shortcut value is changed (Zmigrod et al., 2019; Feder et al., 2023). CDA is the closest train-time analogue of our approach: it uses the same counterfactual edits, but requires training the model on them. 9A smaller body of work examines test-time methods for shortcut removal. Most methods are designed to target a specific shortcut. For example, Pezeshkpour & Hruschka (2024) and Wang et al. (2024) correct for positional bias by aggregating predictions over permutations of answer options, and Weston & Sukhbaatar (2023) mitigate sycophancy by prompting the model to rewrite the input with the user’s opinion removed before answering. These approaches can be viewed as special cases of Uniform and Neutral CTTA, respectively. Other work corrects LLM predictions without reference to a specific shortcut, by calibrating against content-free inputs or batch statistics (Zhao et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2024). Closest to our work, Out-of-Context (OOC) prompting (Cotta & Maddison, 2024) replaces a user-specified shortcut with uniformly sampled values and aggregates the resulting predictions. OOC is similar to Uniform CTTA (see Section 4.2 for differences). The authors show that it achieves a form of shortcut invariance. They do not analyze which aggregation of the edited predictions is optimal, which is the central question of our work and the basis for Model-Weighted and Select-CTTA. Finally, CTTA inherits its structure from test-time augmentation (TTA), which averages a model’s predictions over transformed copies of an input (e.g., Krizhevsky et al., 2012; Simonyan & Zisserman, 2014). Later work on TTA selects the augmentation policy (Lyzhov et al., 2020) or learns the aggregation weights (Shanmugam et al., 2021) from labeled validation data. In contrast, the transfor-mations in CTTA are counterfactual edits of a known shortcut rather than generic label-preserving perturbations, and we derive the optimal aggregation weights analytically and recover them without labels. 

## 6 Discussion 

Shortcut reliance is a major obstacle to LLM safety and reliability. Most solutions for shortcut removal require resources that are often unavailable to LLM users, such as labeled training data and model weight access. We show that shortcut reliance can instead be substantially reduced at test time through Counterfactual Test-Time Augmentation (CTTA), which aggregates predictions across counterfactual edits of the shortcut. We characterize when different CTTA predictors are optimal, evaluate their performance empirically, and propose Select-CTTA, an adaptive procedure for selecting among them at test time. Our approach has several limitations that suggest directions for future work. CTTA assumes that the shortcut and its possible values are known; a useful extension would be to combine CTTA with methods for shortcut discovery. Our theory relies on assumptions about the distribution shift and the accuracy of the counterfactual editor that may fail to hold in practice. Although we find that CTTA remains effective even for zero-shot models, where our assumptions are less likely to be satisfied, it would still be useful to design methods that are more robust to imperfect editors and broader distribution shifts. It would also be useful to develop alternatives to Model-Weighted CTTA that do not require access to token likelihoods, enabling its use with API-only models.

16 C Dataset Details 

C.1 Math Grading (Sycophancy) 

We use math problems from the MATH dataset (Hendrycks et al., 2021), with labels from phase 2 of the PRM800K project (Lightman et al., 2023). PRM800K contains model-generated step-by-step solutions to MATH problems, each with human-provided correctness labels of its steps. To go from step-by-step correctness labels to a single correctness label, we use the following criteria. We label a solution as correct if the human labelers finished it without rating any step as wrong and its final answer matches the MATH reference answer after string normalization. We label a solution as 

incorrect if any of the steps are labeled as incorrect and the final answer does not match the MATH reference answer after string normalization. We drop solutions that do not fit into either of these categories (e.g., a wrong step but a matching answer). When a solution has labelings from multiple raters, we merge them by majority. Due to compute restrictions, we enforce a length ceiling on examples. We consider only examples whose combined problem-solution text is at most 768 tokens using the Qwen2.5 tokenizer. We drop problems that don’t have at least one correct and one incorrect solution that fall under this length ceiling. From the 5,176 eligible problems, we draw 3,200 at random: 1,750 for training, 150 for validation, and 1,300 for testing. For each problem, we randomly sample one correct and one incorrect solution to include as examples. Class 1 is correct .The model’s input is the problem and the solution, formatted as Problem: ... followed by 

Solution: and the solution steps. The shortcut is a sentence appended after a blank line. It has three values: I think this solution is correct , I think this solution is incorrect , and I’m unsure whether this solution is correct . We control the shortcut-label correlation during training and testing by appending shortcut sentences to examples in a label-dependent way (see Appendix D.1 and Appendix D.2). 

C.2 Occupation Classification (Gender) 

We use biographies from the Bias in Bios (De-Arteaga et al., 2019) dataset. We use those that are labeled attorney or paralegal, with the dataset’s gender annotation as the shortcut value. We remove exact duplicate texts. Biographies that contain the words attorney , lawyer , or paralegal make the task trivial, so when we include these examples, we first apply an LLM edit that removes these words (see Appendix D.6 for our text editing procedure). Class 1 is paralegal .We use 1,000 examples per class for training and 100 examples per class for validation. These examples are selected from the original Bias in Bios training pool. To control the shortcut strength during training (see Appendix D.1), we select examples to satisfy a specified gender-occupation relationship Ptrain (S | Y ). Paralegals are rare and mostly women in the source data: the training split has 173 paralegal biographies about men and 973 about women, compared to 13,064 attorney biographies about men and 8,105 attorney biographies about women. Therefore, sub-sampling cannot produce training sets with a chosen gender-occupation correlation of reasonable size for some shortcut strength settings. To reach the 1,100 examples needed for each class, we instead create additional biographies of each gender using counterfactual gender edits (see Appendix D.6). We use a test set that has 291 biographies per class. To create desired shortcut distributions Ptest (S |

Y ), we apply counterfactual gender edits. D.1 Training Distributions 

In our first experiments, we fine-tune models on data with a controlled shortcut strength given by p ∈ [0 .5, 1] . For a designated shortcut value s, we set Ptrain (S = s | Y = 0) = p and 

Ptrain (S = s | Y = 1) = 1 − p. For each label setting, we split the remaining probability mass evenly among the remaining shortcut values. For the math task, the designated shortcut value is the incorrect 

opinion, and for the occupation task, it is man . At p = 0 .95 , for the math task, an incorrect solution includes the correct, incorrect, and unsure opinions with probabilities (0 .025 , 0.95 , 0.025) , and a correct solution includes the opinions with probabilities (0 .475 , 0.05 , 0.475) . For the occupation task, 

Ptrain (man | attorney ) = 0 .95 and Ptrain (man | paralegal ) = 0 .05 . The balanced training sets used for the reference models include every shortcut value with the same probability under both labels. To create training datasets with the desired shortcut-label relationships, we first compute the number of examples in each (label, shortcut value) cell . This is the class size times Ptrain (S | Y ), rounded to integers. To fill these cells for the math task, we randomly sample examples from each label to assign to each shortcut value, with proportions corresponding to Ptrain (S | Y ). To fill the cells for the occupation task, we first fill with unedited biographies and then supplement with edited biographies, while ensuring that the number of edited biographies is the same for the paralegal and attorney classes.

Ptrain (S | Y ); the reversed split swaps the two labels’ settings of this distribution, so Ptest (S | Y =1) = Ptrain (S | Y = 0) ; and the balanced split uses p = 0 .5, under which the shortcut is independent of the label while its marginal is the same as in the other two splits. D.3 Models 

The six models are Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, gemma-3-4b-it, gemma-3-12b-it, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, and Mistral-Nemo-Instruct-2407, all from the Hugging Face Hub. Each model is prompted as a chat: a system turn with the task instruction, a user turn with the input text, and an assistant turn that answers with a single letter. The instructions are:  

> Math. You are a math grading assistant. You will be shown a problem and a proposed step-by-step solution. Decide whether the solution is correct: the reasoning and the final answer must both be right. Respond with ‘A’ if the solution is correct and ‘B’ if it is incorrect.

18 Occupation. You are an occupation classification assistant. Given a short professional biography, respond with ‘A’ if the person is an attorney and ‘B’ if the person is a paralegal. 

The letters are single tokens under every tokenizer used. In classification mode, Mc(x) is the softmax over the logits of the two letters at the position of the assistant’s answer. In likelihood mode, Ml(x)

is the product of the model’s token probabilities over the input text within the chat; the tokens shared by all edits of an example (the instruction and the chat formatting) are not scored. Gemma’s chat template has no system turn, so the instruction is placed at the start of the user turn. 

D.4 Model Training Settings Fine-tuning. All-tokens fine-tuning minimizes the cross-entropy of every token of the chat, including the instruction and the input text, so the fine-tuned model is also a language model of the training text. Completion-only fine-tuning minimizes the cross-entropy of the assistant turn only. Both train all model weights for six epochs with learning rate 2 × 10 −5 on a cosine schedule, 32 examples per optimizer step, bfloat16 precision, and 8-bit AdamW, on one 96 GB GH200 GPU per run with a single training seed. Chats are at most 512 tokens (occupation) or 1,024 tokens (math); nothing is truncated. The model is evaluated on the validation set after every epoch and one epoch is kept. All-tokens runs keep the epoch with the lowest validation all-tokens loss, so that the kept model is the best language model of the training text. Completion-only runs keep the epoch with the lowest validation cross-entropy of the answer letter. 

Zero-shot. The untuned instruction-tuned models are prompted as above. Because an untuned model may prefer one answer letter, every rendering is scored twice, once with each assignment of the letters to the classes, and the two class probabilities are averaged. The two text log-likelihoods are averaged as well. 

D.5 Baselines 

Both calibration baselines correct the classifier’s predicted class probabilities by a term that does not depend on the label. They differ in how they estimate that term. 

Batch Calibration (BC) (Zhou et al., 2024) estimates the model’s prior over classes from the unlabeled test batch. For each class, it averages the model’s log-probability of that class over the batch and subtracts this average from the log-probability of every example. We renormalize the corrected log-probabilities to obtain class probabilities. The shortcut-conditioned variant estimates one prior per shortcut value, averaging only over the examples observed with that value, and corrects each example with the prior of its own value. 

Contextual Calibration (CC) (Zhao et al., 2021) estimates the prior from inputs that carry no information about the label. We use their three content-free inputs, N/A , [MASK] , and the empty string, and place each where the biography or problem would appear in the prompt. We average the model’s class probabilities over the three inputs. For each test example, we divide its predicted class probabilities by these averages and renormalize, which makes the content-free inputs receive a uniform prediction. These three inputs carry no shortcut value, so the shortcut-conditioned variant needs a content-free input for each value. We write these for each task. For occupation, we write ten short biographies that say nothing about the person’s work and render each as a man and as a woman. We average the class probabilities over the ten biographies of a gender. For math, we take the test problem, replace the solution with the line [solution omitted] , and append the opinion sentence. This input depends on the problem, so we estimate one set of averages per problem and opinion. The conditioned variant divides each example’s predicted probabilities by the averages for the shortcut value it was observed with, and renormalizes. 

Out-of-Context prompting (OOC) follows Algorithm 1 and the prompts of Cotta & Maddison (2024). This method uses the same model to generate edits and predictions. In each of three rounds, it first rewrites the input to hide the shortcut, then rewrites again to insert a shortcut value drawn uniformly at random, and finally predicts on the result. Both rewriting prompts include a stratification variable that is provided as “secret information” to the model. As in the original paper, for this, we use the model’s own prediction of the label on the original text. Following the paper, we sample 19 rewrites at temperature 0.8. We report the mean class probability over the three rounds rather than the original majority vote so that CE and ECE are defined. The balanced-training reference is the same base model fine-tuned with the same recipe on the balanced training set of Appendix D.1, predicting on the observed (unaugmented) text. 

D.6 Counterfactual Editor Edit Uses. In this work, we generate edits for two different purposes. First, we generate edits to create train and test sets with specific shortcut strength settings, as described in Appendix D.1 and Appendix D.2. Second, we generate edits at test time as part of our CTTA methods. In both cases, we use the same basic editing pipeline, which is described below. The only two differences are as follows. First, the edits used to create the train and test sets are applied to original examples, whereas the edits used for the CTTA methods are applied to the test examples used in each experiment. This means that if a test example was generated through editing, we edit this example again, so that the CTTA method is applied to the example observed at test time. Second, the edits used to create train and test sets include edits that scrub the text to remove explicit references to the label, whereas the edits used as part of CTTA only involve editing the value of the shortcut variable. 

Edit Pipeline. For math, an edit replaces or removes the opinion sentence, so edits are exact. For occupation, we use three kinds of edits, all produced by Claude Opus: flip , which changes every signal of the subject’s gender (pronouns, gendered nouns, honorifics, first names, and names inside e-mail addresses or handles) to the target gender; neutralize , which removes every signal of the subject’s gender (we replace gendered pronouns with they and reduce first names to an initial); and 

scrub , which rephrases the words attorney , lawyer , and paralegal without introducing a substitute title. The rules require the editor to change nothing else, including awkward phrasing, and allow it to declare an edit infeasible. Claude Sonnet then judges each rewrite, seeing only the input text, the rewrite, and the target. A rewrite that fails is corrected once by Claude Opus and judged again. Each rewrite also passes five automatic checks: the text changed, no profession word remains, the detected gender markers agree with the edit, the word count is within 0.75 to 1.30 times the original, and no banned substitute title was introduced. Rewrites that fail are discarded. 

D.7 Metrics and Confidence Intervals 

All metrics are computed from the predicted probability of class 1: accuracy at threshold 0.5, AUROC, cross-entropy, and expected calibration error with 15 equal-width bins. Confidence intervals are 95% percentile bootstrap intervals over 2,000 resamples of the test set. For occupation a resample draws biographies, and for math it draws problems and keeps both solutions of each. In the tables, each entry is the metric averaged over the six models. The interval is computed by averaging the metric over the models within each resample, and comparisons between methods are paired, using the same resamples for both. Select CTTA makes one decision per fine-tuned model and test split.
