# Conformal ordering: Distribution-free predictive inference for orders and ranks 

## Yonghoon Lee and Zhimei Ren Department of Statistics and Data Science, the Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania October 30, 2026 

Abstract 

We study distribution-free predictive inference for the ordering and ranking of multiple unobserved outcomes. Although numerous methods, such as conformal prediction, have been developed for prediction and predictive inference of unobserved outcomes, extending them to provide inference for the ordering of multiple outcomes with an interpretable guarantee is not straightforward. To this end, we develop a framework and methodology for uncertainty quantification in predictive ordering. Our approach begins by reformulating the problem of determining an ordering among multiple objects as a graph inference problem. Our method adopts a multiple-testing-inspired approach and constructs an uncertainty graph , where each edge represents a confidently inferred pairwise ordering. We develop a distribution-free procedure for constructing a consistent uncertainty graph while controlling either the family-wise error rate or the false discovery rate. Moreover, we show that the resulting graphs naturally yield risk-controlling prediction intervals for the ranks of the outcomes. Empirical results demonstrate that the proposed procedure achieves the target guarantees tightly while maintaining reasonable power and prediction-interval widths. 

# 1 Introduction 

Consider a predictive inference problem in which we observe samples ( X1, Y 1), . . . , (Xn, Y n) ∈ X × R and are subsequently given new feature inputs Xn+1 , . . . , X n+m. The objective is to conduct inference and make decisions regarding the unobserved outcomes Yn+1 , . . . , Y n+m. In such settings, the target of inference may vary depending on the application and the goal of the analysis. For example, in clinical trials, one may wish to identify the patients who are most likely to benefit from a treatment, while in hiring or admissions, one may wish to rank applicants according to their predicted future performance and select the most promising candidates. In this work, we study the problem of making distribution-free inference for the ordering of the unobserved outcomes. Distribution-free predictive inference has mostly been studied in the form of prediction sets, as exemplified by conformal prediction [Vovk et al., 2005]. Conformal prediction provides prediction sets with valid finite-sample coverage guarantees under the sole assumption of exchangeability of the data points. However, when it comes to downstream tasks such as ordering or ranking multiple unobserved outcomes, it becomes unclear what these guarantees imply about the quality of the resulting decisions. For example, suppose we construct conformal prediction sets for each of the unobserved outcomes Yn+1 , . . . , Y n+m and then apply a decision rule based on these prediction sets to conclude that certain individuals are likely to have the highest outcome values. In such cases, it is not immediately clear how marginal coverage guarantees can be translated into an evaluation of, or uncertainty quantification for, the resulting ordering. In this work, we develop a procedure that addresses the problem of predictive inference for the ordering and ranks of unobserved outcomes, providing a guarantee that directly quantifies the quality of the ordering. 1arXiv:2610.40340v1 [stat.ME] 29 Oct 2026 Motivated by Conformal Selection [Jin and Cand` es, 2023], we introduce a multiple-testing-type formulation of the predictive ordering problem and a procedure with false discovery rate control, whose interpretation in this context is discussed later. We also show that this procedure leads to risk-controlling simultaneous prediction sets for the ranks. Regarding the development of an inference procedure that quantifies the uncertainty in the estimated ordering, the main question that needs to be addressed is: 

What is a useful form of inference for predictive ordering, and how can we achieve it? 

In this work, we introduce an inferential target for predictive ordering that admits a concise interpretation, and propose a procedure that achieves the target guarantee in a distribution-free manner. Throughout the paper, we assume that the data points ( X1, Y 1), . . . , (Xn, Y n), (Xn+1 , Y n+1 ), . . . , (Xn+m, Y n+m) are exchange-able, but impose no assumptions on their underlying distribution. 

Notations. We use R to denote the real space and R+ to denote the set of nonnegative real numbers. For a positive integer n, [ n] denotes the set {1, 2, · · · , n } and ( ai)i∈[n] denotes the vector ( a1, a 2, · · · , a n). We slightly abuse notation and write ( ai)i∈[n] to also denote the set {a1, a 2, · · · , a n} when the meaning is clear. We denote the maximum of the real numbers a and b by a ∨ b, and the minimum of the real numbers a and 

b by a ∧ b. Let Sm denote the set of all permutations of [ m]. 

## 1.1 Main contributions 

We study predictive inference for the ordering of multiple unobserved outcomes. Our contributions are as follows. 

A framework for predictive inference for the order. We introduce a novel framework for predictive inference on the order of test outcomes. Specifically, we propose an uncertainty quantification framework in the form of a consistent vertex-ordered uncertainty graph . We then formulate the problem as a “multiple testing problem with a random set of random hypotheses”—which introduces additional challenges beyond those in standard multiple testing problems—and develop methods for this inference task. 

Uncertainty graph with error control. We introduce conformal ordering , a set of methods for con-structing a consistent uncertainty graph with the family-wise error rate (FWER) or the false discovery rate (FDR) control. For FWER control, we develop two methods: a Bonferroni-type procedure and a method in-spired by Tukey’s honest significant difference test [Tukey, 1949]. We also provide a complementary hardness result, showing the necessity of having sufficiently many calibration samples to achieve non-trivial power. For FDR control, our method applies the e-BH procedure [Wang and Ramdas, 2022], together with the development of an efficient conformal e-value for predictive pairwise comparisons. 

Simultaneous prediction intervals for the ranks with risk control. We show that a consistent uncertainty graph for ordering with FWER or FDR control induces simultaneous prediction intervals for the ranks of the outcomes, with control of respective risk measures. 

Empirical evaluation. We evaluate the performance of the proposed methods on both simulated and real data. 1 Empirical results show that the methods achieve their target guarantees—FDR control and risk control—tightly, while maintaining sufficient power and reasonable prediction interval widths.

21.2 Related work 

Conformal prediction was introduced and studied in the foundational works of Vovk et al. [2005] and Pa-padopoulos [2008], and has since gained considerable attention in the recent literature on distribution-free inference. For overviews of results in this field, see Vovk et al. [2005], Angelopoulos and Bates [2021], and Angelopoulos et al. [2024]. Several works have extended the idea of conformal prediction and studied inference methods beyond prediction sets. A representative example closely related to this work is conformal selection, introduced by Jin and Cand` es [2023], in which the authors propose a new framework for predictive inference based on a multiple testing approach. Their work extends ideas from outlier detection Bates et al. [2023], and these ideas have subsequently been extended in Jin and Cand` es [2025], Bashari et al. [2024], Marandon et al. [2024], Gui et al. [2024], Liang et al. [2024], Gui et al. [2024], and Lee and Ren [2025]. See also Lee et al. [2024] for an alternative selection-type inference. Similarly to conformal selection, our work is closely related to multiple testing. We introduce a method with false discovery rate control and apply the e-BH procedure [Wang and Ramdas, 2022] to achieve the target guarantee. The e-BH procedure is an extension of the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure [Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995] that operates on e-values instead of p-values. E-values have gained significant interest in the recent literature, and an overview of this area is provided in Ramdas and Wang [2025]. See, e.g., Ignatiadis et al. [2024b], Fischer and Ramdas [2024], Ignatiadis et al. [2024a], Ren and Barber [2024], Lee and Ren [2024], Xu et al. [2025], Goeman et al. [2025] for recent developments on testing with e-values. Several works have studied uncertainty quantification methods for classical ranking problems with fixed parameters. Neuhof and Benjamini [2024] studies uncertainty quantification for the ordering of feature im-portance, and Al Mohamad et al. [2022] discuss methods for inference on the ranks of means from normal samples. Hung and Fithian [2019] developed a valid procedure for rank verification in exponential-family models, and Goldwasser et al. [2025] extended this framework to Gaussian means with unequal variances. Sood [2025] further extended rank verification to multivariate Gaussian data with arbitrary covariance struc-tures and to verification of the top-K set. In the context of predictive inference concerning the ranking of multiple random variables, Fermanian et al. [2026] considers the problem of constructing prediction intervals for the rank of a test point among the merged calibration and test data—a problem that differs from that considered in this work—, and proposes methods based on concentration inequalities. Neuhof and Benjamini [2026] studies prediction interval for the performance rank of models on a new task, assuming access to performance measurements on previous tasks that are exchangeable with the new task, a setting that differs from ours.

## 2.1 Graphical representation of the ordering relation 

We begin by formulating the statistical inference problem through a graphical representation of the ordering relation. 

Proposition 1. There is a one-to-one correspondence between the set of all orderings of m elements and the set of all collections of pairwise orderings of these m elements that satisfy transitivity. 

This result follows from a fundamental result in algorithm theory: any directed acyclic graph admits a topological ordering [Cormen et al., 2022]—noting that any set of pairwise orderings with transitivity naturally represents a directed acyclic graph. For example, in the case of m = 4, the ordering (3 , 1, 2, 4) uniquely determines the set of pairwise orderings {(3 , 1) , (3 , 2) , (3 , 4) , (1 , 2) , (1 , 4) , (2 , 4) }, which corresponds to the relations Yn+3 ≤ Yn+1 , Yn+3 ≤ Yn+2 , . . . , Yn+2 ≤ Yn+4 . These pairwise orderings satisfy transitivity; for instance, if Yn+3 ≤ Yn+1 and Yn+1 ≤ Yn+4 hold, then Yn+3 ≤ Yn+4 must also hold. Conversely, any set of pairwise orderings of m elements that satisfies transitivity corresponds to a full ordering of m elements. Based on this one-to-one correspondence, the task of predicting the total ordering can be reformulated as that of predicting the pairwise orderings. We therefore focus on quantifying the uncertainty in these pairwise comparisons. To be concrete, we aim to construct a procedure that outputs (ϕbIk1 ,bIk2

Ordering and ranking themselves are essentially about pairwise comparisons—e.g., an element of rank 3 among five objects means that two of the others are larger and the remaining two are smaller. ## 2.4 Interpreting uncertainty graphs through ordered clusters 

We also consider a cluster-based representation as an alternative way to interpret the ordering information provided by the uncertainty graph. A consistent uncertainty graph for ordering naturally induces clusters of adjacent outcomes whose relative ordering is uncertain, suggesting that outcomes within the same cluster tend to have more similar values than those in different clusters. Specifically, the ordered vertices in the uncertainty graph can be grouped into an ordered sequence of clusters, such that the ordering between clusters is inferred to be certain based on the constructed uncertainty graph, as no dotted edge connects vertices from different clusters. A visual illustration is provided in Figure 2. The first graph illustrates the case of simple within-cluster structures, where each cluster forms a complete subgraph of dotted edges. The overall ordering structure, together with its associated uncertainty, can then be represented by four ordered clusters C1 < C 2 < C 3 < C 4.In this case, the clusters provide a clear and intuitive grouping of outcomes with similar values. However, clusters need not correspond to complete subgraphs of dotted edges. The second graph in Figure 2 illustrates such a case: we have three ordered clusters C1 < C 2 < C 3, where C1 and C3 also contain pairs of vertices whose relative ordering are inferred certain. Thus, some certainty information within clusters may be lost when summarizing the uncertainty graph through ordered clusters. 6Nevertheless, this cluster-based representation provides an intuitive summary of the overall ordering structure, highlighting the ordering relations between clusters that are inferred to be certain. In practice, it can help identify which groups can be reliably distinguished and where uncertainty in the ordering is concentrated. An uncertainty graph with FWER control at level α guarantees that the ordering relations between all clusters are simultaneously correct with probability at least 1 − α, since every pair of vertices from different clusters is connected by a solid edge. In contrast, an uncertainty graph with FDR control does not directly imply a similarly interpretable statistical guarantee for the ordering between clusters—the resulting representation serves more as an intuitive summary of the overall ordering structure. # 3 Conformal ordering with FWER control 

In this section, we introduce methods for constructing uncertainty graphs with FWER control, as well as the induced prediction intervals for the ranks with simultaneous coverage control. 

## 3.1 Uncertainty graph with FWER control 

To start, we construct a predictor bμ : X → R using a separate training dataset (e.g., constructed via data splitting). As in (1), we then obtain a point estimator bI for the ordering of the unobserved outcomes by sorting the predicted values bμ(Xn+j ) in ascending order. Next, we observe that the pairwise certification problem in (2) is equivalent to testing the following 

m(m − 1) /2 hypotheses: 

Hj1,j 2 : ( Yn+j1 − Yn+j2 ) · (bμ(Xn+j1 ) − bμ(Xn+j2 )) ≤ 0, 1 ≤ j1 < j 2 ≤ m. (8) In words, rejecting Hj1,j 2 certifies that bμ correctly orders Yn+j1 and Yn+j2 . We now aim to develop procedures for simultaneously testing this family, whose indices are fixed but whose true values depend on the random, unobserved outcomes. Since the predicted gap |bμ(Xn+j1 ) − bμ(Xn+j2 )| assesses the reliability of the predicted ordering, it is natural to reject Hj1,j 2 when the gap is sufficiently large. How should we calibrate the rejection threshold? We discuss two methods: the Bonferroni procedure based on conformal p-values and a partitioning-based method.

4.1.1 Improving power with U-eBH procedure 

In standard multiple testing with e-values, several methods have been developed to improve the power of the e-BH procedure. A representative example is the U-eBH procedure [Xu and Ramdas, 2023], which applies the e-BH procedure after dividing the e-values by an independent uniform random variable U ∼ Unif([0 , 1]). Below, we show that the U-eBH procedure also applies to our setting with random hypotheses. 13 Corollary 2. The U-eBH procedure applied to (˜ ej1,j 2 )1≤j1<j 2≤m from (17) at level α guarantees FDR ≤ α.

Note that Corollary 2 does not follow directly from the proof of the standard U-eBH procedure, as our problem is not a standard multiple testing problem. The validity of the U-eBH procedure in the predictive selection task has been established in Lee and Ren [2025], and Corollary 2 follows from similar arguments. It is worth noting that, in our method, the U-eBH procedure has a limited effect on the composition of the rejection set due to the particular structure of our e-values in (13), which have an indicator in the numerator. Specifically, whether an e-value is positive or zero is determined by whether the corresponding test statistic exceeds its threshold, and dividing the e-values by a uniform random variable U does not change which e-values are positive. Instead, it scales all e-values upward by a common random factor, thereby reducing the likelihood that the e-BH procedure makes no rejections. In Section A.1, we empirically verify that the output of the procedure is indeed stable, i.e., robust to the particular value of U drawn. As an additional remark, in standard testing problems with fixed hypotheses, there are other methods for improving the power, such as those based on the closure principle [Xu et al., 2025, Goeman et al., 2025]. However, these approaches do not directly apply to our predictive inference problem with random hypotheses.

In this work, we developed a distribution-free methodology for predictive inference on the ordering of multiple unobserved outcomes. We proposed a multiple-testing-inspired approach that yields a consistent uncertainty graph with FDR control and showed that it also yields simultaneous prediction intervals for the ranks with risk control. Empirical results demonstrate that the proposed methods controls the error rates at the target levels while attaining reasonable power and interval widths. Many open questions remain. Conformal prediction and conformal selection have been studied and extended in various settings beyond exchangeable data and standard marginal guarantees. Similar extensions may be explored for inference on ordering, offering opportunities to generalize the methods developed in this work. We leave these directions for future work.
