# Identifying and disentangling direct and spillover effects using multiple pre-treatment periods 

Martin Huber MARTIN .HUBER @UNIFR .CH 

University of Fribourg, Department of Economics Bd. de P´ erolles 90, 1700 Fribourg, Switzerland 

Abstract 

Under interference, the treatment of some units affects the outcomes of others, so that the effect on a treated unit consists of a direct and a spillover component. A non-treated comparison group then generally identifies neither component, but the difference between the total effect on the treated and the spillover effect on the comparison units. We show that both components are identified separately when multiple pre-treatment periods are available, without an uncontaminated compar-ison group. Under no anticipation, pre-treatment outcomes are free of both components for every unit. Extrapolating the pre-treatment trend within a group, as in interrupted time series approaches, therefore identifies the counterfactual outcome in the absence of any treatment. Applied to the treated it gives the total effect on them, applied to the non-treated at a given level of exposure the spillover effect on them. Disentangling the two components in addition requires effect homogene-ity across treated and non-treated units with the same exposure and covariates, as well as common support in exposure. We derive doubly robust and Neyman-orthogonal expressions for the effects of interest, which permits controlling for covariates by machine learning, and establish asymptotic normality. A simulation study confirms the root-n behaviour of the estimators and the reliabil-ity of the standard errors. We apply the framework to the compulsory licensing of enemy-owned patents in the United States in 1919. Licensing raised patenting in the licensed subclasses and in technologically close ones alike, so that a comparison-group design understates the total effect. 

Keywords: interference, spillover effects, exposure mapping, interrupted time series, double ma-chine learning, testing arXiv:2610.37049v1 [econ.EM] 27 Oct 2026 1. Introduction 

Most studies in causal analysis and treatment evaluation explicitly or implicitly rule out interference or spillover effects, as implied by the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA), see Cox (1958) and Rubin (1980), which is frequently implausible in empirical applications. When treating some units affects the outcomes of others, the effect of the treatment on a treated unit consists of the direct effect of its own treatment and the spillover effect of the treatment of others, which together form the total effect on the treated. Moreover, a non-treated comparison group may itself be contaminated by spillovers, in which case the estimand of a causal inference method is in general the difference between the total effect on the treated and the spillover effect on the comparison units, see Mealli and Viviens (2026). Neither component is identified separately, and the sign of the estimand is uninformative about the sign of either. The remedies proposed so far restrict the interference structure and require the existence, and the correct identification, of units that are clean controls in the sense of being unaffected by any spillover. In this paper, we propose an identification approach that does not require clean controls when outcomes are observed in multiple pre-treatment periods. Under no anticipation of treatment, the pre-treatment outcomes of every unit, treated or not, are realizations of the potential outcome in the absence of any treatment, such that interference contaminates the post-treatment outcomes of the comparison group but not its pre-treatment outcomes. Extrapolating the pre-treatment trend within a group, as in interrupted time series (ITS) approaches, therefore delivers the counterfactual outcome in the absence of any treatment, provided that the extrapolation is valid. Applied to the treated, this identifies the total effect on the treated; applied to the non-treated at a given level of exposure to the treatment of others, the spillover effect on them. Exposure is summarized by an ex-posure mapping in the sense of Aronow and Samii (2017), a function of the treatments of all other units measuring how intensely a unit is reached by them, such as the number of treated units in its neighbourhood. With two pre-treatment periods and a linear trend the counterfactual conditional mean is a second difference of the cell’s own pre-treatment conditional means, and further periods permit richer trend specifications. Neither a comparison group nor a common support condition across groups is required. Celli et al. (2026) establish this identification result without covariates. Our first contribution is the conditional version, which permits trends to differ across observed char-acteristics and the covariate composition of a group to change across periods, and thereby extends the argument from panels to repeated cross sections. Separating the total effect into its direct and spillover components is more demanding, as the difference between the two is the spillover treated units would experience in the absence of their own treatment, which is never observed. Our second contribution is to make precise what it takes to recover it. First, an effect homogeneity condition is required, namely that treated and non-treated units with the same exposure and covariates experience the same average spillover, which is re-strictive whenever spillovers differ along unobserved characteristics in which the two groups differ. Second, exposure must be observed for both groups and have common support across them, which fails when treatment status mechanically determines exposure. Where either is implausible, the analysis is confined to the total effect on the treated and the spillover effect on the non-treated, which we regard as the main parameters. Our third contribution is a testable restriction. When some units are classified as non-exposed, their spillover is zero by construction, so the corresponding estimate must not differ significantly from zero. A rejection indicates either that the identifying assumptions fail or that units classified 2as non-exposed are in fact reached by the treatment. The test thus assesses the design jointly with the exposure mapping. It requires the mapping to claim that some units are non-exposed, but not that this claim be correct. We state it conditional on covariates, because spillovers of opposite sign in different regions of the covariate space cancel upon averaging. A second restriction arises if conditional parallel trends are imposed in addition: the counterfactual is then overidentified, and the two routes agree if and only if the conditional pre-treatment trends coincide across groups, which is what placebo tests in difference-in-differences analysis assess. Finally, we derive doubly robust and Neyman-orthogonal expressions for the effects of interest, permitting estimation of the conditional means and propensity scores by machine learning, and es-tablish root-n consistency and asymptotic normality. A simulation study shows the estimators to be close to unbiased, to converge at the root-n rate and to have reliable standard errors when the assumptions hold, and the overidentification test to have power against violations of conditional parallel trends. In an application to the compulsory licensing of enemy-owned patents in the United States in 1919, we find that licensing raised patenting both in the licensed subclasses and in techno-logically close ones, so that the comparison-group design of Moser and Voena (2012) understates the total effect. Mealli and Viviens (2026) show that comparison-group methods identify a difference between the total effect on the treated and the spillover on the non-treated under unrestricted interference, and that within-group before-and-after comparisons separate the two components under a constant-mean restriction, which we replace by a trend model. Identification of direct and spillover effects under a known exposure mapping is studied in difference-in-differences and related panel designs by Clarke (2017), Butts (2021), Huber and Steinmayr (2021), Xu (2023), Hettinger et al. (2024) and Sun and Xiao (2025), and in cross-sectional designs under randomization or selection on observables by Hudgens and Halloran (2008), Tchetgen Tchetgen and VanderWeele (2012), Forastiere et al. (2021) and Leung and Loupos (2025), all requiring comparison units the treatment does not reach. Counterfactual forecasting without a comparison group goes back to the intervention analysis of Box and Tiao (1975) and is developed by Brodersen et al. (2015), Menchetti et al. (2022), Cerqua et al. (2022) and Botosaru et al. (2023), and applied to interference by Celli et al. (2026), the paper closest to ours. They show that forecast-based counterfactuals identify total effects on the treated and exposure-specific spillovers on the non-treated without uncontaminated comparison units, and characterize what survives when exposure is latent or universal. Their results are unconditional and come without estimation theory or testing, which is what we add, along with the decomposition of the total effect and its conditions. Grossi et al. (2023) pursue a related synthetic control strategy. The statistics we use arise as specification contrasts without interference (Huber and Oeß, 2025), and the covariate-specific test builds on Apfel et al. (2024) and Armendariz and Huber (2026). The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Sections 2 to 6 present the effects of interest, the assumptions, identification, doubly robust estimation and the testable restrictions, Sections 7 and 8 the simulation study and the empirical application, and Section 9 concludes. 

2. Causal effects of interest 

We consider n units observed in K + 1 ≥ 2 pre-treatment periods t ∈ {− K, . . . , −1, 0} and one post-treatment period t = 1 , and denote by Yt the outcome and by D ∈ { 0, 1} a unit’s own treatment, which is realized between t = 0 and t = 1 . Let D−i be the treatment vector of all units other than i. Rather than restricting the interference structure, we summarize it by an exposure 3mapping in the sense of Aronow and Samii (2017), that is, a function 

Gi = g(D−i, F i) ∈ G , (1) which maps the treatments of all other units into a low-dimensional measure of how intensely unit

Here Fi collects the characteristics determining which units can reach i,such as spatial coordinates or network positions, and G is a finite set containing the element 0,which denotes the absence of exposure. Leading examples are the share of treated households in a village or an indicator for having a treated neighbour in a network. Potential outcomes in the sense of Neyman (1923) and Rubin (1974) are indexed by own treatment and exposure, Yt(d, g ),which replaces the no-interference part of SUTVA by the weaker restriction that G is a sufficient summary of D−i for unit i’s outcome. See Halloran and Struchiner (1991) and Manski (2013) for the general formulation of interference. The observed post-treatment outcome is Y1 = Y1(D, G ).We also observe covariates X, which may be time-varying, in which case X collects the values across the periods considered. Extrapolating the counterfactual outcome is a forecasting problem, in which post-treatment covariate values such as seasonality or local economic conditions are potential predictors. The requirement is that X be unaffected by D and G, which is innocuous for pre-treatment values but needs scrutiny for post-treatment ones, as discussed without interference by Caetano et al. (2022). Interference enlarges the set of affected covariates, since a post-treatment covariate may respond to the treatment of others as well as to a unit’s own. Covariates plausibly unaffected by any treatment may therefore enter at post-treatment values, the others at pre-treatment values only. Since interference generally rules out comparing a unit’s outcome to a world in which only that unit is non-treated, the effects are defined relative to the benchmark in which no unit is treated. We define all parameters conditional on the covariates and obtain the aggregates by averaging over the respective subpopulation observed in the post-treatment period; we suppress T = 1 in the definitions and make it explicit in the identification results. 3. Identifying assumptions 

Assumption 1 (No anticipation and consistency) Yt = Yt(0 , 0) for t ≤ 0 and Y1 = Y1(D, G ).

Assumption 1 rules out that outcomes prior to the treatment respond to the treatment of any unit, such that pre-treatment outcomes are realizations of Yt(0 , 0) irrespective of a unit’s eventual treatment and exposure. Under interference the condition is stronger than its counterpart without spillovers, since it also excludes anticipation of the treatment of others, but it concerns the timing of the response alone and places no restriction on the interference structure. It restricts outcomes, so units may learn of the treatment in advance as long as their pre-treatment outcomes do not respond to it.

Both fail when the relevant quantity differs along unobserved characteristics in which the compared cells differ, which is the case when treatment and exposure are selected on such characteristics. Conditioning on a rich set of covariates is the only remedy, which motivates the adjustment of Section 5. Their second parts require common support in exposure, discussed in Section 4 and frequently the binding constraint. 

Assumption 5 (Conditional parallel trends) For all g and X in the support among the treated, 

E[Y1(0 , 0) − Y0(0 , 0) | X, D = 1] = E[Y1(0 , 0) − Y0(0 , 0) | X, D = 0 , G = g] ,

and X has common support across the cells {D = 1 } and {D = 0 , G = g}.

Assumption 5 is a conditional parallel trends assumption, see e.g. Abadie (2005) and Lechner (2011), stated for the potential outcome in the absence of any treatment. We impose it only as an optional restriction, which overidentifies the spillover effect and generates a testable implication.

9. Conclusion 

Under interference, comparison-group methods identify only the difference between the total effect on the treated and the spillover effect on the non-treated. We have shown that extrapolating the counterfactual conditional mean within a group identifies both effects separately, in repeated cross sections as in panels and without uncontaminated comparison units. The argument rests on no antic-ipation and on a valid extrapolation of the pre-treatment trend of the cell considered. Decomposing the total effect into a direct and an interference component is more demanding. It requires effect 15 homogeneity conditional on observables across treated and non-treated units at the same exposure level, as well as common support in exposure, which is why we regard the total effect on the treated and the spillover on the non-treated as the main parameters of the framework. The framework delivers two testable restrictions. The spillover must be zero for the units the ex-posure mapping classifies as non-exposed, which assesses the identifying assumptions jointly with the measurement of exposure. If conditional parallel trends are imposed in addition, the conditional spillover is overidentified, and the two routes agree if and only if the extrapolated pre-treatment trends coincide across groups. We derived doubly robust and Neyman-orthogonal expressions for all parameters, which permit estimating the conditional means and the propensity scores by ma-chine learning, and established root-n consistency and asymptotic normality. The simulation study confirms this behaviour and shows the overidentification test to have power against violations of conditional parallel trends. In the application to the compulsory licensing of enemy-owned patents in the United States, li-censing raised domestic patenting in a licensed subclass by 0.80 patents per year and in a technolog-ically close one by 0.35 . Against all non-treated subclasses the treated gain 0.69 , which understates the total effect by the average spillover in that comparison group. Two limitations remain. The de-composition rests on an effect homogeneity condition that the data cannot test, and the extrapolation over a horizon of thirteen to twenty-one years is only as good as the linear trend it assumes, which the placebo test checks at shorter horizons alone. 16 References
