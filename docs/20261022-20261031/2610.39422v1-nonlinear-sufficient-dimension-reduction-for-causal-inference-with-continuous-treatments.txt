# Nonlinear Sufficient Dimension Reduction for Causal Inference with Continuous Treatments 

## Chak Fung Choi 1, Yin Tang 2, and Bing Li 11Department of Statistics, The Pennsylvania State University 

2Dr. Bing Zhang Department of Statistics, University of Kentucky 

Abstract 

Causal inference with continuous treatments requires estimating nuisance func-tions to adjust for the potentially high-dimensional confounder, but nonparametric estimation of these functions is severely hampered by the curse of dimensionality. To address this challenge, we propose a nonlinear sufficient dimension reduction frame-work to construct low-dimensional sufficient confounders to preserve the unbiasedness of the treatment effect. We estimate the causal effect by combining the generalized Sliced Inverse Regression with the inverse probability weighting, outcome regression, and augmented inverse probability weighting, respectively. We further establish the convergence rates of the first two estimators and derive the asymptotic distributions of the third. Our theoretical results show that, compared to estimators based on the full covariates, dimension reduction yields faster convergence rates and weaker rate conditions without inflating the asymptotic variance. Simulation studies demonstrate that the proposed estimators improve finite-sample performance over their counter-parts without dimension reduction, particularly when the original covariate dimension is high. These results demonstrate that sufficient dimension reduction provides a flexible approach to mitigating the curse of dimensionality in causal inference with continuous treatments. 

Keywords: Causal inference; Continuous treatment; sufficient dimension reduction; Gener-alized sliced inverse regression; Semiparametric efficiency 

# 1 Introduction 

Causal inference with continuous treatments arises in many applications involving drug dosage (Schomaker et al. 2024), political advertising exposure (Schindl et al. 2026), envi-ronmental exposure (Shin et al. 2025), and program intensity (Colangelo & Lee 2025). In this setting, the causal effect is naturally described by the dose-response curve, which is the primary target for causal estimation. The dose-response curve can be estimated using vari-ous strategies, such as inverse probability weighting, outcome regression, augmented inverse probability weighting, and related doubly robust procedures (Kennedy et al. 2017, Colangelo 1arXiv:2610.39422v1 [stat.ME] 29 Oct 2026 & Lee 2025). All of them involving estimation of nuisance functions, such as the generalized propensity score and the conditional mean outcome, which depend on both the treatment and covariates. Moreover, to ensure all confounding effect are removed, a common practice is to include as many suspicious confounders as possible, resulting in a high-dimensional covariate. This hampers the estimation of the nuisance parameters and consequently also the dose-response curve. The purpose of this paper is to introduce a flexible framework of nonlinear sufficient dimension reduction to overcome the curse of dimensionality caused by the high-dimensional covariate. Specifically, nuisance functions can be estimated using flexible nonparametric and data-adaptive machine-learning methods, including kernel regression (Wand & Jones 1994, Fan & Gijbels 1996), spline smoothing (Green & Silverman 1993), random forests (Breiman 2001), neural networks (Farrell et al. 2021, Hornik et al. 1989), as well as ensemble methods such as Super Learner (Van der Laan et al. 2007). Estimators of the parameter of interest (dose-response curve) such as double/debiased machine learning (Chernozhukov et al. 2018) and targeted maximum likelihood estimation (van der Laan & Rubin 2006) can incorporate these flexible nuisance parameter estimators while supporting valid statistical inference under suitable rate conditions, such as rate double-robustness conditions (Liu et al. 2024, Smucler et al. 2019). However, consistency and semiparametric efficiency typically require nuisance function estimators to converge sufficiently fast, a requirement that can be difficult to satisfy with a high-dimensional confounder. This motivates the use of sufficient dimension reduction to facilitate nuisance function estimation. Recently, Luo et al. (2017), Luo & Zhu (2020) applied sufficient dimension reduction (SDR) to reduce the dimension of the confounder to improve accuracy of the estimation of the causal effect; see also Huang & Chan (2017, 2018), Ma et al. (2019). These methods focused primarily on linear SDR, and can achieve substantial improvement in the accuracy of causal estimation. A wide variety of linear SDR methods developed in the past two decades or so and can be applied to the causal estimation setting (Li 1991, Cook & Weisberg 1991, Li 1992, Li & Wang 2007, Xia et al. 2002). See Li (2018), Ma & Zhu (2013) for reviews of this area. However, in many causal estimation problems, the low-dimensional sufficient predictors are inherently nonlinear, and no reduction is possible when attention is restricted to linear SDR methods. In this paper, we develop a nonlinear SDR method and incorporate it with several most commonly used causal inference methods by replacing the high-dimensional confounder with our nonlinear low-dimensional sufficient representation of the confounder. Other approaches to reducing confounder complexity include covariate selection, balancing-score construction and representation learning. Covariate selection aims to identify a sub-set of observed covariates that is sufficient for confounding adjustment; see, for example, Brookhart et al. (2006), De Luna et al. (2011), Shortreed & Ertefaie (2017) and Belloni et al. (2014). Balancing-score methods summarize the covariate information relevant to treatment assignment through a lower-dimensional score, with examples such as the propensity score for binary treatments (Rosenbaum & Rubin 1983), the generalized propensity score for continuous treatments (Imbens 2000), and the propensity function framework for general treatment regimes (Imai & Van Dyk 2004). Representation learning uses flexible models such as deep neural networks to learn low-dimensional features that improve covariate bal-ance or counterfactual prediction; see, for example, Johansson et al. (2016), Sch¨ olkopf et al. 2(2021). In this paper we make four main contributions. First, we introduce three types of suf-ficient confounders—the sufficient confounder, the balancing sufficient confounder, and the prognostic-balancing sufficient confounder—which provide the platform for nonlinear suffi-cient dimension reduction in causal estimation. Second, we use generalized sliced inverse re-gression (GSIR) to estimate the balancing sufficient confounder and combine it with inverse probability weighting (IPW) to construct a dimension-reduced causal estimator (GSIR-IPW). Third, we use GSIR to estimate the prognostic-balancing sufficient confounder, then incorporate it into outcome regression (OR) and augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW) to construct the corresponding dimension-reduced causal estimators (GSIR-OR and GSIR-AIPW). Finally, we establish the convergence rates and asymptotic distribu-tions of the proposed estimators, showing that they achieve faster rates or attain the same asymptotic variance under weaker rate conditions, compared to their dimension-unreduced counterparts. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we layout the background for causal inference under continuous treatments and introduce the three sufficient confounders. In Section 3 introduces the GSIR-IPW and GSIR-OR estimators. In Section 4, we establish their convergence rates and compare these rates with their dimension-unreduced counter-parts. Sections 5 and 6 motivate the GSIR-AIPW estimator from a semiparametric efficiency perspective and develop the corresponding asymptotic theory. Section 7 reports simulation results assessing the finite-sample performance of the proposed estimators, and Section 8 provides an empirical application. Section 9 gives some concluding remarks and discussions. Due to space limit, most proofs of the theoretical results are placed in the Supplementary Materials. 

# 2 Dose-response Curve and Sufficient Confounders 

Let (Ω , F, P ) be a probability space. We consider an observational study with a continuous treatment and a vector of covariates. The treatment T is a random variable taking values in a measurable space (Ω T , FT ), where Ω T ⊂ R is a compact set. The covariate X is a random vector taking values in (Ω X , FX ), where Ω X ⊂ Rp. Following the potential out-comes framework (Rubin 1974, Imbens 2000), suppose there exists a collection of potential outcomes {Y (t) : t ∈ ΩT }, where each Y (t) : Ω → R is a random variable representing the outcome that would be observed if a subject were assigned treatment level t. Since t

may not be the treatment level an individual receives, we do not observe Y (t) in practice. Instead, we observe the outcome at the random treatment T . Under the commonly used stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA), this observed outcome can be written as 

Y (T ). In summary, the observed data consist of n independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations ( Yi, T i, X i) for i = 1 , 2, . . . , n , where Yi = Yi(Ti). Our primary estimand is the average dose-response function, defined as 

μ(t) = E{Y (t)}, for t ∈ ΩT ,

which characterizes how the mean potential outcome varies across treatment levels. 32.1 Identification of Dose-Response Curve 

We now introduce the assumptions required to identify the dose-response curve from the observed data. In general, for any given t ∈ ΩT , E{Y (T )|T = t} does not identify E{Y (t)},because the potential outcomes {Y (t) : t ∈ ΩT } is in general not independent of T , as the set of covariates may affect both treatment assignment and the potential outcomes. Such covariates form a confounder and must be appropriately adjusted for (VanderWeele & Shpitser 2013). To ensure that the parameter of interest is identifiable from the observed data, we impose the following assumptions. 

Assumption 1 (Consistency) . For every t ∈ ΩT , T = t implies Y (T ) = Y (t).

Note that this assumption is already built in the notation Y (t). To see the true meaning of this assumption clearly, let Y = Y (T ), then the above assumption should be interpreted as “when T = t, Y = Y (t).” See Hernan & Robins (2025). 

Assumption 2 (Positivity) . fT |X (t|x) > c for some constant c > 0 for all (t, x ) ∈ (Ω T , ΩX ),where fT |X (t|x) is the conditional density of T given X = x.

Assumption 3 (Ignorability) . Y (t) T |X for all t ∈ ΩT , where denotes independence. 

Assumption 1 ensures that the observed outcome is exactly the potential outcome cor-responding to the subject’s own treatment level. Assumption 2 states that every subject has some chance of receiving every treatment level regardless of the covariates. It prevents the data from containing regions where some treatment levels can never be assigned under some covariates. Assumption 3, the commonly used ignorability condition (Imbens & Rubin 2015, Chapter 3), requires that, conditional on the covariates X, each potential outcome is independent of the treatment. In other words, adjusting for X removes confounding in the treatment-outcome relationship. There are three commonly used approaches that can identify the dose-response curve

High-dimensional covariates create substantial challenges for estimating nuisance func-tions. Many existing estimators for continuous treatments require estimation of the general-ized propensity score or related treatment-density quantities, the outcome regression func-tion, or both as functions of the full-covariate vector. For example, Galvao & Wang (2015) proposed a semiparametric two-step estimator, while Kennedy et al. (2017) and Colangelo & Lee (2025) developed doubly robust estimators. Because these nuisance functions are estimated in the original covariate space, their estimation can become difficult when X is high-dimensional: parametric models may be misspecified, whereas flexible nonparametric estimators may suffer from the curse of dimensionality. These challenges motivate reduc-ing the covariate dimension before estimating the nuisance functions while preserving the information required for causal identification. 

## 2.2 Sufficient Confounder 

Without further assumptions, adjustment for the full-covariate vector X is needed, because ignorability (Assumption 3) is assumed to hold only conditional on X. Therefore, the nuisance functions, such as the outcome regression function and the generalized propensity score, must be estimated based on the full-covariate vector X, which is the main source of difficulty when X is high-dimensional. We therefore consider the possibility that a low-dimensional structure in X suffices to control for confounding. That is, we assume that there exists a function z : Rp → Rd such that 

Y (t) T |z(X) for all t ∈ ΩT . (2.6) This formulation is more general than variable selection or linear SDR methods, since z(X)may consist of a subset of covariates, or of linear or nonlinear transformation of them. z(X) may have a much lower dimension than X, it retains all the information needed to control for confounding. We therefore refer to any transformation satisfying (2.6) as a

First, the defining condition (2.6) involves the potential outcomes Y (t), whereas only Y (T ) is observed for each subject. Second, a sufficient confounder is required only to satisfy (2.6), but the full-covariate generalized propensity score fT |X (t|x) and the outcome regression function m(t, x ) are not necessarily preserved. The first preserves the conditional treatment density and is motivated by IPW; the second addition-ally preserves the outcome regression and is motivated by OR and AIPW. We introduce these constructions in Sections 2.3 and 2.4, respectively.

# 3 GSIR-Based IPW and OR Estimation 

## 3.1 Nonlinear SDR and GSIR 

Estimating a sufficient confounder z(X) satisfying (2.12) or (2.14) can be formulated as a nonlinear sufficient dimension reduction (SDR) problem. For a generic response U , nonlinear SDR seeks a low-dimensional transformation z : Rp → Rd, with d < p , such that 

U X|z(X). (3.1) The transformed predictor z(X) is called a sufficient predictor for U as it preserves all information in X relevant to U . Taking U = T and U = {Y (T ), T } in (3.1) gives (2.12) and (2.14), respectively, so the corresponding sufficient predictors are exactly the balancing and prognostic-balancing sufficient confounders. Existing nonlinear SDR methods include kernel-based approaches, such as GSIR and generalized sliced average variance estimation (GSAVE) (Lee et al. 2013, Li & Song 2017), and neural-network-based approaches (Liang et al. 2022, Chen et al. 2024, Tang & Li 2025, Xu et al. 2025). Among these choices, we adopt GSIR (Lee et al. 2013, Li et al. 2025) because its established convergence guarantees (Li & Song 2017, Choi et al. 2026) allow us to account for the estimation error in bz when analyzing the proposed dose-response estimators. In practice, the estimated sufficient confounder bz(X) is obtained by solving a regularized sample eigenvalue problem, following (Li 2018, Section 13.5). See Appendix B.1 for additional details of nonlinear SDR and GSIR.

# 9 Discussion 

Several directions warrant further investigation. First, it would be also valuable to construct prognostic sufficient confounder, defined by (2.15), without requiring the balancing property. By Theorem 6.3, such a z(X) satisfies A(t) = C(t) = 0, giving 

VZ (t) − VX (t) = −B(t) ≤ 0,

with strict inequality whenever 

P σ2(t, X ) > 0, f T |X (t|X)̸ = fT |Z (t|Z) > 0.

Thus, removing treatment-assignment information that is irrelevant to the outcome can reduce the leading AIPW variance. This is closely related to the efficiency gains from 24 excluding overadjustment variables in nonparametric causal effect estimation (Rotnitzky & Smucler 2020). However, directly targeting (2.15) is not easy, because it involves the potential outcomes 

Y (t), which are only partially observed, so it falls outside classical SDR. An interesting observation is that, under Assumptions 1–3, (2.15) is equivalent to 

Y (T ) X|{ T, z (X)},

as shown in Proposition G.1 in the Supplement. This suggests a partial SDR approach (Chiaromonte et al. 2002). However, existing partial SDR methods primarily consider linear reductions (Wen & Cook 2007, Feng et al. 2013, Li et al. 2023), while nonlinear partial SDR with a continuous conditioning variable remains largely unexplored; see (Jones 2023, Section 4.5.6) for related work in the case of categorical conditioning variables. We leave this extension for future research. Second, although the present work focuses on generalized sliced inverse regression (GSIR), the proposed framework is applicable to a broad class of nonlinear SDR methods, including generalized sliced average variance estimation (GSAVE), other kernel-based SDR methods, and more recent deep learning approaches. Developing unified theoretical guarantees for causal inference based on a wider range of SDR estimators remains an important direction for future research. Third, while our theoretical results focus on a single treatments, extending the framework to longitudinal treatments or dynamic treatment regimes would substantially broaden its applicability. Another practical consideration is the selection of the structural dimension. Throughout the theoretical development, the structural dimensions of both nonlinear and linear SDR are assumed to be known. In practice, however, these dimensions must be estimated from the data. Fortunately, the structural dimension is often quite small in many applications, particularly for nonlinear SDR, where one or two sufficient predictors frequently capture the relevant information. A simple diagnostic is to examine scatter plots of the treatment or out-come against the estimated sufficient predictors under different candidate dimensions. Once the true structural dimension has been reached, additional estimated sufficient predictors would be expected to exhibit little systematic relationship with either the treatment or the outcome. More formal procedures are also available. For nonlinear SDR, the cross-validated BIC criteria proposed by Li et al. (2011) and Li & Song (2017) provide consistent estimators of the structural dimension. For linear SDR, several dimension selection procedures have been developed and are summarized in (Li 2018, Chapters 9–10), while dimension selection for MAVE is implemented in the software package described by Xia & Hang (2025). Incor-porating uncertainty from structural dimension selection into the asymptotic analysis is an interesting topic for future research. Overall, the proposed GSIR-based IPW, OR, and AIPW estimators show that sufficient dimension reduction offers a principled approach to mitigating the curse of dimensionality in causal inference with continuous treatments. GSIR finds a low-dimensional sufficient confounder that preserves the ability to identify the dose-response curve. By estimating the nuisance functions based on this sufficient confounder rather than on X, the proposed frame-work makes flexible nonparametric nuisance parameter estimation more easily compatible with existing response-dose estimators. When the dimension of the sufficient confounder 25 is small so that GSIR converges sufficiently fast, this leads to faster convergence rates and weaker rate requirements. We hope that this work motivates further integration of modern sufficient dimension reduction techniques with semiparametric causal inference methods.
