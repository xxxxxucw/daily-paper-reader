Abstract | Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly support long-horizon tasks by leveraging external memory and past experience. However, existing agents mainly retrieve records or summaries, lacking the ability to form and refine causal understanding of the world. Inspired by human causal cognition, we introduce CauProphet, an agent that develops and updates causal knowledge within its Causal Cognition. For each task, CauProphet combines relevant knowledge in Causal Cognition with current evidence to construct a Causal Belief, a structured belief capturing causal factors and the effects of possible actions. It uses this belief for decision-making and continuously updates both the belief and Causal Cognition as new observations emerge, enabling causal knowledge that persists beyond individual experiences. Evaluations demonstrate that CauProphet progressively acquires causal knowledge and transfers it to future decision-making. In financial research, CauProphet leverages prior discoveries to improve downstream analyses and outperforms all baselines in mean rank correlation with next-day returns, achieving a 25.4% improvement over a baseline agent. It also improves prediction accuracy from 21.3% to 28.6% in dynamic real-world settings. Further experiments validate its ability to perform reliable causal analysis and action selection across scientific and everyday tasks, demonstrating the potential of causal experience learning for next-generation LLM agents. ## 1. Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have enabled a new class of agents capable of perceiving environments, acting through tools, and pursuing goals [Wang et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2023; Schick et al., 2023]. Within this space, a particularly influential category has emerged in the form of general-purpose autonomous assistants, including Claude Code [Anthropic, 2025a], Codex [OpenAI, 2025], Her-mes [Nous Research, 2026], and OpenClaw [OpenClaw, 2025], which interact with users through multi-turn dialogue to address requests involving data analysis, writing, coding, and research. Much of what makes these assistants capable lies not in the base model alone but in the surrounding system—tool interfaces, context management, and safety constraints—that governs the model’s behavior through repeated cycles of reasoning, tool use, and observation [Yao et al., 2023; Lopopolo, 2026; Ning et al., 2026]. Causality investigates how entities influence one another beyond superficial correlations. It provides the conceptual and mathematical foundation for asking why observed patterns arise, what would happen under an intervention, and what would have occurred under an alternative condition [Pearl, 2009; Peters et al., 2017]. Causal analysis involves identifying the factors that influence an outcome, proposing explanations of how they do so, and examining the evidence and assumptions behind these explanations [Spirtes et al., 2001; Hernán and Robins, 2020; Sharma and Kiciman, 2020; Blöbaum et al., 2024]. Users may need such analysis for their tasks even when they lack expertise in causality. When choosing actions to achieve user goals, LLM agents need to distinguish observed associations from the changes their actions would cause [Pearl, 2009; Kiciman et al., 2024]. Without this distinction, agents may provide plausible but unreliable explanations of why something happens. Recent work on LLMs and causality follows two main directions: constructing causal knowledge and using it to guide agent behavior. The first automates causal analysis or develops methods for identifying causal relationships and estimating effects [Roy et al., 2025; Peng et al., 2026a,b; Wang et al., 2025; Verma et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026]; applying its findings to later tasks also requires retaining what was learned and when it applies. The second uses causal knowledge to guide decisions and program improvement [Chen et al., 2025; Nguyen et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026a], with reasoning organized around specific environments or programs. These approaches leave open how agents can accumulate causal understanding across experiences as human beings do. Children learn causal relationships through observation and action and use this knowledge to predict outcomes in new situations [Gopnik et al., 2004]. More broadly, human beings draw on an understanding of how events influence one another [Sloman and Lagnado, 2015] to guide decisions without recalling every experience that shaped it. Inspired by human causal cognition, we introduce CauProphet (Figure 1), an LLM agent that accu-mulates and refines causal knowledge through experience. Within CauProphet, Causal Cognition organizes what the agent learns around recurring factors, how they influence outcomes, and the conditions under which these relationships hold. For a new task, the agent checks which retained explanations fit the current circumstances and combines them with current evidence to form a Causal Belief. This structured belief describes what influences the outcome and how possible actions could change it, guiding the agent’s decisions. The same cognition can therefore inform different beliefs as the task and its evidence change. As observations arrive, the agent revises its current belief and updates Causal Cognition with selected findings that may inform later decisions. Through this cycle, causal understanding remains useful even when the original experiences are no longer available. Our experiments demonstrate that CauProphet progressively develops causal knowledge from experience and applies it to improve decisions in subsequent tasks. We first test whether knowledge retained from earlier tasks improves subsequent financial research, with each task beginning in a

fresh context. CauProphet outperforms all baselines in mean correlation between predicted and actual rankings of next-day stock returns, with a 25.4% relative gain over an agent with text memory. To test how causal understanding supports judgments across domains as new evidence arrives, we also evaluate predictions made while real-world events unfold. Prediction accuracy increases from 21.3% for a baseline agent to 28.6% for CauProphet. Further tests validate the causal analysis and action selection needed for these complex tasks, with scientific and everyday applications broadening the evaluation. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: • We introduce CauProphet, an LLM agent that accumulates causal knowledge from experience to develop its Causal Cognition. For each task, it combines relevant knowledge from Causal Cognition with current evidence to form Causal Belief, which guides decisions. New observations refine both the belief and the knowledge retained for future tasks. • We show that causal understanding learned from experience improves performance on complex tasks across domains. Accumulated knowledge guides what to investigate next and how to interpret new evidence, allowing earlier discoveries to improve decisions as circumstances change. 

## 2. Related Work 

LLM Agent Systems. LLM agents combine reasoning, memory, planning, and tool use [Wang et al., 2024]. ReAct [Yao et al., 2023] and Toolformer [Schick et al., 2023] connect language models with external actions. System design shapes these capabilities [Lopopolo, 2026; Ning et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026], and evaluations distinguish its contribution from that of the base model [Kapoor et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026]. MemGPT [Packer et al., 2023], retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [Lewis et al., 2020], and Markdown notes in Claude Code [Anthropic, 2025b] support information retention and access beyond the current context. These mechanisms leave open how agents can, like humans, develop causal understanding from experience and use it to guide new decisions without recalling every event that shaped it. 

Constructing Causal Knowledge. Kiciman et al. [2024] examine LLM knowledge for causal dis-covery and reasoning, emphasizing checks against evidence. Causal-LLM [Roy et al., 2025], Cau-Scientist [Peng et al., 2026a], and CauTion [Peng et al., 2026b] develop LLM-assisted methods for causal graph discovery. Causal-Copilot [Wang et al., 2025] and the Causal AI Scientist [Verma et al., 2025] automate causal analysis workflows, while Causal Agent [Han et al., 2026] combines causal tools with iterative reasoning and graph memory. InferenceEvolve [Wang et al., 2026] develops and evaluates causal effect estimators. Their emphasis is on producing and assessing causal structures, estimates, or analysis methods. Reusing findings beyond the original analysis also requires retaining what relationship was learned and under which conditions it holds. CauProphet focuses on how these findings become causal understanding that can guide subsequent tasks. 

Using and Revising Causal Knowledge. Causal knowledge also guides agent behavior. Causal-aware LLMs [Chen et al., 2025] learn and update environmental causal models for action selection, while CausalPlan [Nguyen et al., 2025] learns causal relationships for planning and coordination. CausalEvolve [Chen et al., 2026a] uses a “causal scratchpad” to retain findings that guide program revisions, while CausalFlow [Bonagiri et al., 2026] uses counterfactual analysis to repair execution failures. These approaches organize causal reasoning around environment dynamics, program

## 3. Methods 

3.1. Overview 

CauProphet learns causal relationships from experience and leverages them to guide new decisions (Figure 2). The agent accumulates these relationships and their conditions to build Causal Cognition, reflecting how human beings develop lasting understanding from experience. To apply this accumu-lated understanding to a new task, the agent combines relevant knowledge from Causal Cognition with current evidence to form a structured Causal Belief. This belief describes what influences the outcome and how possible actions could change it, guiding the agent’s decisions. We view each task as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP), where the agent relies on a belief about an environment it cannot fully observe [Kaelbling et al., 1998]. To investigate competing explanations, the agent uses Causal Scout to compare alternatives and suggest observations that could distinguish them. As observations arrive, CauProphet revises the belief and updates Causal Cognition, retaining causal knowledge that remains useful beyond the individual experiences from which it was learned. 

3.2. Causal Understanding across Experiences Knowledge retained across tasks. Causal Cognition organizes retained knowledge around expla-nations of how recurring factors influence one another, together with their conditions, time scales, evidence, and unresolved questions. Its graph connects these factors through proposed causal rela-tionships. Written explanations describe mechanisms and their evidence in natural language and can be revised without first constructing a graph. In both forms, the explanation can be checked against a new task and revised as evidence changes, even after the original interaction is no longer available. 

Applying knowledge to a task. When prior knowledge could help, the agent searches Causal Cognition using the user’s goal and current circumstances. It follows graph connections to find relevant factors and possible paths of influence, and retrieves written explanations of related mechanisms. The model adapts this knowledge to the task: it maps recurring factors to current variables, checks conditions and time scales against current evidence, and examines observations that challenge the explanation. Adopted relationships form the working graph in Causal Belief; the agent also records current facts, assumptions, and open questions. For retrieved written explanations, the model records how they apply to the current situation in the belief. Thus, the same retained knowledge can inform different beliefs as task conditions and evidence change. The resulting belief guides actions, and causal analysis tools can use its graph to estimate effects. 

Accumulating evidence. A retained graph relationship may receive support in one task and con-flicting evidence in another. The model records these judgments as claims, assigning confidence from the available evidence. 3.3. Revising Causal Understanding with Evidence Alternative explanations. When several explanations fit the current belief, the model proposes alternatives for Causal Scout to compare. Individually plausible relationships may become incompatible when combined. Causal Scout uses Answer Set Programming (ASP) [Gebser et al., 2019] to enumerate combinations. ASP specifies choices and constraints; each solution, called an answer set , describes a candidate graph satisfying them. Rules enforce required and forbidden relationships, exclude incompatible hypotheses, prevent directed cycles, and respect known temporal order. 

6CauProphet: Developing Causal Cognition 

Candidates are also checked against available statistical test results. Causal Scout ranks alternatives by confidence and simplicity and returns supporting evidence, counterevidence, and observations that could distinguish them. The model uses these suggestions to decide what to investigate and which relationships to adopt. 

Revising the current belief. As new evidence arrives, the model compares it with its explanation and expected outcomes, keeping observations distinct from predictions and checking their conditions and time periods. It can add or remove relationships, change assumptions, or revise the conditions under which an explanation applies. These revisions inform subsequent action selection and investigation. With suitable data, causal analysis tools help discover relationships, select factors to account for when estimating effects, estimate those effects, and check their reliability. Results return to Causal Belief, allowing the model to revisit its explanation or choose further analyses. 

Retaining revised knowledge. The agent selects findings that may help later tasks and expresses them in terms of recurring factors, preserving the conditions and evidence that make the explanation applicable. For graph knowledge, Lift maps selected task variables back to factors in Causal Cognition and records the relationship, its mechanism, conditions, and supporting or conflicting evidence. Findings can remain hypotheses. Claims about an existing relationship under the same conditions and time scale update its evidence and score through Equation 2. Findings under different conditions or time scales are retained separately. These changes affect which explanations are retrieved for later tasks. Written explanations are revised directly to reflect changed mechanisms, conditions, or uncertainty. Earlier versions and reasons for change are retained; retrieval selects the latest version available at the task’s time. In a nutshell, CauProphet applies retained understanding to a task, revises it against current evidence, and preserves selected changes for later decisions. The agent chooses when to read, retain, and revise this knowledge. Selected system and tool instructions are provided in Appendix D.

Unless otherwise specified, we use DeepSeek-V4-Pro [DeepSeek-AI, 2026] in all experiments because it is open-source, supports reproducibility, and offers strong overall performance at a low cost. Our baselines are ReAct [Yao et al., 2023] and OpenCode [OpenCode, 2025], an open-source harness with over 200,000 GitHub stars. 4.3. Foundational Causal Abilities 

Causal reasoning in the complex tasks above depends on basic abilities to identify causal relationships, estimate how actions affect outcomes, and choose interventions. We therefore test these foundations directly using fixed datasets with known causal mechanisms and an interactive environment. To test causal analysis against known answers, we use data from three structural causal models (SCMs), whose equations define the causal relationships and intervention effects. They include two nonlinear systems and one linear system, each with 800 observations. CauProphet has the lowest mean effect relative error in all three and gets the effect direction correct in all nine evaluations (Figure 6). ReAct finds a valid set of variables to account for when estimating effects in the eleven-node system but reverses every effect direction. Accurate causal analysis requires both recovering relationships and estimating how interventions change the outcome. We then move from causal structure discovery and effect estimation on fixed datasets to choosing interventions in an interactive environment. We use CausalGame [Chen et al., 2026b], a simulated drone adventure, to test whether causal understanding guides actions when agents have only partial information about their surroundings. CauProphet has higher mean survival and score than ReAct and OpenCode in all three scenarios, winning every run in both trap scenarios (Figure 7). The complete system also exceeds both ablations: joint removal of Causal Belief, Causal Cognition, and Causal Scout with causal computation retained, and removal of all dedicated causal capabilities. The results connect causal analysis to the consequences of actions; computation tools alone do not recover the complete system’s performance.

## 5. Conclusion 

We introduced CauProphet to study how agents can build causal understanding from experience, much as human beings do. By connecting Causal Cognition with Causal Belief, the agent applies retained causal relationships and their conditions to new situations, combining them with current evidence to guide decisions. Findings from these tasks can contribute to the causal knowledge available for later use. Experiments show that earlier findings guide subsequent research and that CauProphet makes more accurate predictions while events unfold. Further evaluations examine the basic causal abilities underlying these tasks and applications to scientific analysis and everyday activities. These results support our approach to developing agents whose causal understanding extends beyond individual experiences and informs new decisions. 

## Limitations 

When evidence is insufficient to test causal relationships, CauProphet relies more heavily on the base model’s prior knowledge. Richer observations and feedback from actions could help assess the reliability of the knowledge it accumulates. Our experiments cover a limited number of stages; longer studies in changing real-world settings could examine how causal understanding continues to grow and adapt over time.

## A. Case Study: Learning and Revising a Causal Explanation 

This case follows how an everyday conversation changes the agent’s understanding of an observed pattern. The user asks whether an increase in reported cases after expanded testing means that an outbreak is worsening. The interaction moves from an explanation of this particular observation to a retained relationship about how testing conditions influence what is measured. 

A.1. Forming an Explanation of the Current Situation 

The user asks for a short answer, without requesting a causal graph or analysis tools. CauProphet creates a Causal Belief with four variables and three edges. Its explanation separates the underlying infections from the process through which testing makes cases observable: reported cases can change when either infections or detection changes. The task-local belief gives the agent an explicit account of the factors behind the observation (Figure 9A). 

A.2. Revising the Explanation after Clarification 

The user then asks what happens if testing mainly expands among people who already feel ill. This introduces a condition about who is tested. CauProphet incorporates testing indication and test positivity into the same belief, expanding it to six variables and seven edges. The revised explanation considers how the composition of the tested group influences positivity, alongside the contribution of underlying infections (Figures 9B and 10C). The new information therefore changes the relationships used to interpret the observation. 

A.3. Retaining a Relationship and Its Conditions 

During the cognition update, CauProphet maps testing intensity to an existing surveillance-intensity concept and retains the selected testing-indication–positivity relationship with its source episode (Figure 10D). The retained explanation describes how symptom-targeted testing can change the observed positivity rate and records that interpreting its trend depends on the testing strategy remaining roughly constant. The resulting knowledge is expressed through recurring factors, a relationship between them, and a condition on its use. The source episode records where the explanation came from; the relationship and its condition provide the content that can be considered in another task. This makes the paper’s human-inspired motivation concrete: an experience changes the agent’s explanation, and the revised understanding remains available in Causal Cognition beyond the current conversation.

## B. Method and Implementation Details 

B.1. Representing Causal Knowledge 

An experience can suggest that one factor influences another under particular conditions. Causal Cognition retains the recurring factors and proposed relationship, together with a mechanism, time scale, and supporting or conflicting evidence. For graph knowledge, a source link connects the relationship to the experience that motivated it. In the testing case in Appendix A, the retained explanation concerns how symptom-targeted testing affects observed positivity. The Causal Belief also includes the variables and observations needed to interpret the current reports. Written explanations provide another way to retain a mechanism and its conditions, including when the agent has not constructed a graph. 

B.2. Applying Knowledge to a New Task 

When retained knowledge could help, the agent searches for relevant concepts, graph connections, or written explanations. It maps recurring factors to variables in the current situation and examines whether the retained conditions and time scale still fit. For example, using the testing explanation requires checking who is being tested and whether the selection strategy has changed. Relationships the model adopts enter the working graph in Causal Belief. When using a written explanation, the model records the selected version and its own account of how it applies to the present task. These judgments guide the next reply or investigation. 

B.3. Revising Knowledge with Evidence 

New observations may support an explanation, contradict it, or reveal a missing condition. The agent revises the belief and selects updates to retained knowledge. For the low, medium, and high confidence levels, the evidence weights 𝑤 𝑖 in Equation 2 are 0 .5, 1, and 2, respectively. Hypotheses at these levels assign prior log-odds of 0 .25, 0 .75, and 1 .25; the largest is used, with ℎ𝑟 = 0.25 when no hypothesis is recorded. Claims are aggregated for a fixed directed relation, set of conditions, and time scale. Each source episode contributes at most one claim to that relation; revising the claim replaces its previous verdict and confidence. Findings under different conditions or time scales are retained separately. Written explanations are revised directly, preserving the reason for the change and earlier versions. Retrieval selects the latest version available at the task’s time. 

B.4. Comparing Alternative Explanations 

A current belief can admit several explanations of the same observation. The model proposes hypotheses for Causal Scout to compare. Each candidate combines selected hypotheses with the known relationships. Causal Scout checks the resulting graph against supplied constraints on relationships, temporal order, and available statistical evidence, then ranks alternatives by confidence and simplicity. Its response associates each alternative with supporting evidence, counterevidence, and observations that could distinguish it. The model uses this comparison to choose which explanation to investigate or adopt in the current belief.

Revising an explanation’s applicability. The volume study starts from the hypothesis that low current volume relative to a historical baseline may precede higher next-day returns. Later searches compare longer and shorter baselines and combine 120-day and 20-day relative-volume terms. Following feedback from 2019H1, the next search retains this combined form but compares alternative recent baselines, selecting 10 days for 2019H2. The retained understanding thus changes both its assessment of persistence and the formula variants considered in later tasks. 

Using earlier comparisons to guide new ones. In the fixed-data study, the third-round BETA execution retrieves a lesson comparing volume weights applied to a relative price decline with weights applied to a raw price ratio. It re-evaluates a previous candidate, then compares reversal horizons and volatility windows before submitting a revised formula. Subsequent rounds revisit the volatility window and weighting choice. These traces illustrate how retained explanations and measured comparisons enter later searches and are supplemented by new outcomes.

## D. Prompts and Tool Instructions 

The excerpts cover the task loop illustrated in Appendix A: forming an explanation, revising it after clarification, and retaining a useful relationship and its conditions. They also cover prior-knowledge retrieval and optional investigation. Implementation wording is preserved with normalized formatting. 

D.1. Establishing a Working Explanation 

The interactive application’s system prompt connects the user’s objective to variables and evidence, establishing a working explanation that subsequent observations can revise. 

> Role and working understanding

Role and reasoning principles. 

You are CauProphet, a Causal-Native AI Agent for automated scientific discovery and optimization. Your core role is to serve as an invisible causal reasoning backend: users describe a problem, and you autonomously orchestrate causal discovery, inference, and intervention analysis to deliver actionable, scientifically grounded insights. Think causally. Follow the causal workflow for substantive requests. Distinguish correlation from intervention; central claims about "X affects Y" need a structural argument. Keep observed facts distinct from causal hypotheses. 

Initialize and revise the belief. 

Do not wait for the user to ask for a graph. Causal Belief is task-local working state. Decide whether to create, switch, or reuse a Belief based on the current task and reasoning needs. Call create_causal_belief (or create_scratchpad) immediately if no task-matching Belief is active; open the matching Belief when resuming a task. Causal Cognition is long-term memory across tasks and conversations for useful experience and reusable mechanism knowledge. Before the first substantive answer, you MUST call write_causal_belief (or write_scratchpad) to record the objective, named variables with measurement meanings, currently justified or explicitly hypothesized edges, and uncertainty in notes. For every final directed edge, record a concise rationale and method showing whether it came from current evidence, a measurement definition, or a mechanism hypothesis. Upsert, rewrite, remove, or clear belief entries as the hypothesis changes; it is a scratchpad, not a commitment.

D.2. Using and Revising Retained Knowledge 

The model follows relevant graph connections or retrieves written explanations, checking their conditions before applying them to the current belief. Useful findings can then revise retained explanations or add selected relations, grounded in recurring concepts. These instructions govern both earlier knowledge use and later updates. 

> Retrieving, applying, and retaining knowledge

Retrieve and check. 

Start with search_causal_cognition using keywords chosen freely from the task’s domain, tools, API behavior, action dependencies, measurements, mechanisms, current variables, or a suspected missing cause. Use exact concept_ids returned by the index or a previous frontier. Every retrieved relation or path is candidate-only. Before changing the task-local Causal Belief, check its measurement meaning, context, lag, mechanism, and current-task evidence. 

Apply a retained explanation. 

Search also returns current written mechanism notes. Check their evidence, scope, conditions, and time scale before applying them. Use instantiate_cognition_note to record the selected version and your natural-language application in the active Belief; applying a note does not change the persistent explanation. 

Retain and revise useful knowledge. write_cognition_note: Save or revise useful observations, reusable mechanisms, strategies, or unresolved hypotheses in natural language. Tentative knowledge is allowed if uncertainty and evidence are clear. No graph or existing Belief is required. When new observations change an explanation or its applicable conditions, revise the related note by its ID and give a revision_reason instead of adding a duplicate dated conclusion. Earlier versions remain available as provenance; normal retrieval returns the latest available version. When the task yields reusable causal structure, call update_causal_cognition with selected final directed relations, their hypothesized / supported / contradicted verdicts, confidence, context, lag, mechanism, and concise notes. Ground variables to existing canonical concept IDs whenever possible; ambiguous or weak groundings should be resolved rather than used to create another concept. If the relation already exists, add this episode claim or useful metadata instead of duplicating it.

The model investigates uncertain relationships with available evidence. Causal Scout can compare alternatives; effect estimation requires suitable data and assumptions. In the testing conversation, the agent explains qualitatively how the tested population changes positivity and why the testing strategy matters to its interpretation. The response makes its assumptions and uncertainty explicit.

> Investigating explanations and answering

When to compare explanations. 

When the current Belief/query has multiple plausible local mechanisms, use causal_scout with structured candidate_hypotheses. 

Inputs and returned guidance. causal_scout: Assistive causal hypothesis search. Pass a typed query plus candidate_hypotheses and, when available, required/forbidden edges, a skeleton, temporal order, and tested conditional-independence facts. The tool checks candidate DAGs with d-separation, progressively relaxes lower-confidence conflicting CI facts, and returns competing graphs, compelled/reversible directions, rejection reasons, and discriminating probes. It never proves hidden confounders, rewrites the active Causal Belief, or changes causal_infer. 

Choose an appropriate analysis. 

Tool selection remains proportional: use algorithms only when their data and assumptions apply. Use web_search only when the task needs external mechanism evidence; do not add browsing as a ritual. With data: Run causal_diagnose once when the data profile is not already known. Follow its cost-aware primary recommendation and run ONE bounded discovery method first. 

causal_infer: Identify and estimate a causal effect for an explicit intervention. Without data: Reason qualitatively –- describe direction and plausible magnitude of key effects. State what data or experiments would be needed for quantitative estimation. Record assumptions in causal belief notes. 

Return a grounded answer. 

Be transparent. Present conclusions with explicit confidence levels and assumptions. Never present results as definitive if key assumptions are unverified.
