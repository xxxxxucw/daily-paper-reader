# Uncertainty quantification for expectation-calibrated predictions 

## Georgios Gavrilopoulos ∗ Johanna Ziegel ∗

Abstract 

The existing literature on model calibration focuses mainly on classification and prob-abilistic predictions. In this work, we address calibrated point predictions for the con-ditional mean. Although existing impossibility results preclude exact out-of-sample cal-ibrated predictions, we develop calibrated confidence intervals that provide uncertainty quantification around such predictions. Our results come with distribution-free theo-retical guarantees and are applicable in model-agnostic, finite-sample settings under ex-changeability by leveraging conformal prediction. Calibrated confidence intervals rely on a general underlying binning scheme. We present two examples of such a binning scheme, one based on a data-independent partition and the other on isotonic regression. Under structural assumptions, we prove that calibrated confidence intervals based on isotonic re-gression come with strong asymptotic consistency properties and have an asymptotically vanishing width. We illustrate the empirical performance of our methods by applying them first in a simulated setting and then to a highly imbalanced insurance dataset. 

Keywords: Calibration, conditional mean, conformal prediction, uncertainty quantifica-tion, isotonic regression, isotonic calibration. 

# 1 Introduction 

The concept of forecast calibration was developed in the meteorology literature, to evaluate probabilistic forecasts for binary events, such as the probability of precipitation. A probabilistic forecast for a binary event is calibrated if its predicted class frequencies align with the observed frequencies. Formally, if Y is a binary random variable, a prediction p = p(X) for the probability 

P(Y = 1 | X) is called (out-of-sample) calibrated if 

P (Y = 1 | p(X)) = p(X) almost surely . (1) Throughout the 1960s and 1970s, calibration appeared in the literature with various names, such as unbiasedness in the small, realism, realism of confidence, and reliability (Lichtenstein et al., 1977). A weaker notion of calibration, called unbiasedness in the large, had also been proposed (Murphy and Epstein, 1967). A concise review of probabilistic forecasting in those early days is given by Dawid (1986). Calibration originally attracted a lot of attention because it incentivizes honest predictions (Dawid, 1986). Recently, calibration has also been considered important because it facilitates interpretability of black-box models. For example, classification models often rely on the com-putation of a score in [0 , 1] , which is then turned into a binary output via some prespecified 

> ∗Seminar for Statistics, ETH Zurich, Zurich, Switzerland.

1arXiv:2608.26703v1 [stat.ME] 27 Aug 2026 decision rule. This score is often interpreted as a class-membership probability, and it is used to quantify model uncertainty. If the original model is calibrated, this interpretation is valid. However, for some commonly used classification models, such as naive Bayes classifiers, SVMs, and modern neural networks, the predicted scores often do not align with the observed class frequencies (Zadrozny and Elkan, 2002; Guo et al., 2017). This has prompted the develop-ment of post-processing methods to improve calibration properties. Examples of such methods include histogram binning (Zadrozny and Elkan, 2001; Gupta et al., 2020), isotonic calibration (Zadrozny and Elkan, 2002), and Platt scaling (Platt, 2000). Relying on histogram binning and isotonic calibration, Vovk et al. (2004) and Vovk and Petej (2014) developed Venn and Venn-Abers predictors, respectively. These are post-processing recalibration methods with set-valued outputs, which are guaranteed to contain a calibrated prediction. The advantages of Venn and Venn-Abers predictors are their theoretical validity guarantees and their empirical performance. Calibration can be generalized beyond binary outcomes. Given random variables X ∈ X 

and Y ∈ R, a (random) function f : X → R is called (expectation-)calibrated if 

E[Y |f (X)] = f (X) almost surely. (2) We can think of f as a predictive model for the conditional mean E[Y |X]. The definition of expectation-calibration was introduced by Tarpey and Flury (1996) using the term self-consistency . The same property later appeared in Gupta et al. (2020); Wüthrich and Ziegel (2024); Van der Laan and Alaa (2024) as auto-calibration and perfect calibration. Gneiting and Resin (2023) generalized it to other functionals by introducing the concept of T-calibration. A more general interpretation of (2) is through nested information sets. Ideally, f (X) makes use of all the relevant information in X concerning Y , and in that case, (2) implies that f (X)

is equal to its target functional E[Y |X]. If, on the other hand, f (X) makes no use of that information and is calibrated, then f (X) is equal to the marginal expectation E[Y ], which is uninformative. The information set ( σ-algebra) generated by f (X) itself can be thought of as a good compromise between these two extremes. This motivates definition (2). According to that, f (X) captures the information carried by itself, which explains the term self-consistency .In finite samples, even the uninformative marginal expectation E[Y ] is impossible to access. Therefore, in practice, the task of deriving precisely calibrated predictions is generally impossi-ble. Gupta et al. (2020) formally showed the impossibility of this task for binary classification and proved that, even for (2) to be satisfied approximately, f must induce a finite or countably infinite partition on the space X . They used histogram binning to obtain such approximately calibrated models f for binary classification and they showed that such models can be used to construct confidence intervals that contain E[Y |f (X)] . However, careful hyperparameter tuning is needed to avoid inflated intervals. Wüthrich and Ziegel (2024) used a slightly different technique, which borrows ideas from isotonic calibration (Zadrozny and Elkan, 2001) and Venn-Abers predictors (Vovk and Petej, 2014). After training a model ̂ f , they fit isotonic regression to (̂ f (X1), Y 1), . . . , (̂ f (Xn), Y n),where {(Xi, Y i)}ni=1 is a holdout calibration set. They show that the fitted function satisfies (2)

This is not the first time that Venn-Abers predictors are generalized to the continuous setting. Recently, Van der Laan and Alaa (2024) developed conditionally valid prediction sets for Yn+1 , i.e. sets ̂ Cn(Xn+1 ) such that P(Yn+1 ∈̂ Cn(Xn+1 ) | f (Xn+1 )) ≥ 1 − α, where f

Moreover, our targets are confidence intervals that contain a calibrated prediction ˜f (Xn+1 ), not prediction sets for Yn+1 itself. The work of Petej and Vovk (2026), which was developed concurrently with ours, also ex-tends Venn-Abers predictors to continuous labels. However, they only develop intervals with marginal coverage guarantees, which can be expressed as a special case of our confidence inter-vals. Moreover, they do not study the asymptotic behavior of their intervals or the asymptotic properties of recalibrated predictions. To construct calibrated confidence intervals, we make use of prediction sets for Yn+1 . Our method does not put any restrictions on how these prediction sets are generated. Nevertheless, conformal prediction offers a theoretically solid, interpretable, and distribution-free uncertainty quantification framework that yields prediction sets with almost exact coverage guarantees. Systematic treatments of conformal prediction can be found both in the seminal work of Vovk et al. (2005) and in the most recent books by Angelopoulos and Bates (2023) and Angelopoulos et al. (2024), which also provide insight into more recent developments in the field. In our applications, we use both the standard version of conformal prediction and confor-malized quantile regression (Romano et al., 2019), which outputs prediction sets with locally adaptive width. The prediction sets derived by Van der Laan and Alaa (2024) could also be 3used, but at a much higher computational cost. While we focus on point forecasts in this paper, a large strand of literature has proposed generalizations of (1) to probabilistic forecasts. There, the target is the prediction of the entire (conditional) distribution of Y . As it turns out, (1) can be generalized in various ways, giving rise to probabilistic calibration (Dawid, 1984; Diebold et al., 1998), threshold calibration (Henzi et al., 2021), marginal calibration (Gneiting et al., 2007) and auto-calibration. An extensive analysis of these notions of calibration is given in Gneiting and Resin (2023). Various methods to construct calibrated probabilistic predictions have been proposed. Con-formal predictive systems, which were introduced by Vovk et al. (2019), give rise to probabilisti-cally calibrated predictive distributions. Recent works by Boström et al. (2021) and Allen et al. (2025) used binning and isotonic distributional regression (Henzi et al., 2021), respectively, to construct predictive distributions with stronger calibration properties. This paper has the following structure: In Section 2, we define calibrated confidence intervals 

and we present our main result about the construction of such intervals. In Section 3, we provide methodological details for the implementation of calibrated confidence intervals, and in Section 4, we discuss the predictive performance and the consistency of calibrated predictions. We conclude the theoretical part of the paper with Section 5, where we provide asymptotic results about the width of calibrated confidence intervals. Section 6 contains an implementation of our methods on a simulated dataset. Section 7 presents a case study on third-party motor liability claims that demonstrates the adaptivity of our method to different types of data and unbalanced datasets, such as those often seen in insurance. Code and replication material for the simulations and the case study are available at https://github.com/GGavrilos/Calibrated_Intervals .

Both X and Y are defined on a probability space (Ω , F, P). We denote the joint distribution of (X, Y ) by P(X,Y ). ## 2.2 Isotonic calibration 

Isotonic regression offers in-sample calibration guarantees, but it is not recommended for pre-diction because it is applicable only when X ⊆ R and its performance relies heavily on the validity of the isotonic shape constraint. Nevertheless, it can be used as a post-processing, 

recalibration tool. The idea to use isotonic regression for recalibration in binary classification originated in Zadrozny and Elkan (2002), who used it to recalibrate probabilistic predictions made by SVMs, Naive Bayes models and decision trees. This method was later refined by Vovk and Petej (2014) and it was generalized to a continuous setting by Wüthrich and Ziegel (2024). The main idea is to fit isotonic regression to the sample (̂ f (X1), Y 1), . . . , (̂ f (Xn), Y n), where ̂

f (X) is any prediction for the conditional mean E[Y |X]. If ̂ T : R → R denotes the isotonic regression fit, then ̂ T (̂ f (Xi)) is in-sample calibrated, because the binning procedure ̂ T satisfies the conditions of Proposition 2.1; see Example 2.2. This method is known as isotonic calibration .Isotonic calibration combines the calibration properties of isotonic regression with the pre-dictive power of ̂ f . It relies on the assumption that a flexible model, like a neural network or a boosted tree, can capture the order relationships among E[Y |X = x1], . . . , E[Y |X = xn], i.e. if ̂ f (Xi) ≤̂ f (Xj ), then E[Y |X = Xi] ≤ E[Y |X = Xj ]. (10) In practice, we would like to use a model ̂ f that satisfies (10) for as many pairs i, j ∈ { 1, . . . , n }

as possible, so that isotonic regression is appropriate for the points {(̂ f (Xi), Y i)}ni=1 .One advantage of isotonic calibration is that it can be used on top of any predictive model ̂

f , which enables its application to a vast variety of domains and data types. Additionally, it does not compromise predictive performance. As Van der Laan and Alaa (2024) noted, the in-sample performance of ̂ T (̂ f (Xi)) is at least as good as that of ̂ f (Xi), as isotonic regression minimizes ∑ni=1 (Yi − T (̂ f (Xi))) 2 over all increasing functions T , including the identity map. On the downside, ̂ T (̂ f (Xn+1 )) is only in-sample calibrated. This is not sufficient for most practical scenarios, where (Xn+1 , Y n+1 ) is actually sampled from the same distribution as {(Xi, Y i)}ni=1 and not from their empirical distribution. 62.3 Calibrated confidence intervals

# 5 Width of calibrated confidence intervals 

Theorem 2.2 provides validity guarantees for Cn+1 ,α , but it does not address their statistical power. There are two standard ways to do this. One is through the distance ( coverage gap ) between the nominal coverage 1 − α and the true coverage P(θ ∈ C α). When Cα is a valid confidence interval, it always holds that P(θ ∈Cα) − (1 − α) ≥ 0. Ideally, the value of this quantity is equal to zero. Larger values might indicate that we have used an unnecessarily large, overconservative confidence interval. In the literature, this approach was used by Van der Laan and Alaa (2024) to show that, under some assumptions, the coverage gap of their prediction sets vanishes asymptotically. The coverage gap of conformal prediction sets has also been studied. More specifically, conformal prediction sets have a coverage gap of at most 1/(n + 1) , where n is the size of the calibration set (Angelopoulos and Bates, 2023; Vovk et al., 2005). The second way to determine the informativeness of a confidence interval is through its width. Narrower confidence intervals are preferable, because they provide a better estimate of the unknown parameter. Moreover, in classical statistics, shortest-width intervals are a standard alternative to equal-tailed ones. The width of calibrated confidence intervals derived by simple binning (Algorithm 1) is equal to (hn+1 − ℓn+1 )/(# Dj⋆ ) =: w(Cn+1 ,α ). Using a heuristic argument, we can show that w(Cn+1 ,α )

typically converges to zero (in probability). Indeed, if Iα(Xn+1 ) is a conformal prediction set, then, under different types of assumptions (Lei et al., 2018; Yao et al., 2025), its width converges to q1−α/ 2(Y |Xn+1 ) − qα/ 2(Y |Xn+1 ) in L1, where qα/ 2(Y |x), q 1−α/ 2(Y |x) are the α/ 2-and (1 − α/ 2) -quantiles of Y | X = x. Provided that q1−α/ 2(Y |Xn+1 ) − qα/ 2(Y |Xn+1 ) is bounded and that #Dj⋆

> P

→ ∞ as r, n → ∞ , it follows that w(Cn+1 ,α ) P

→ 0. Therefore, ensuring that bin size goes to infinity yields calibrated confidence intervals with vanishing width. It has previously also been analyzed by Noll et al. (2020) and Loser (2025), and it was employed by Wüthrich and Ziegel (2024) to demonstrate in-sample recalibration. Given the strong interest in actuarial science in calibrated predictions (Wüthrich and Ziegel, 2024; Krüger and Ziegel, 2021; Denuit et al., 2021), freMTPL2freq is suitable for an empirical study of the (out-of-sample) calibrated confidence intervals developed in this paper. It consists of n = 678 013 rows, which represent different insurance policies. The policies were observed mostly over one year, over an overall period between 2011 and 2013. It also contains covariate information, such as the driver’s age, the vehicle type, and the duration of the insurance contract. A detailed list of all covariates is given in Table 4 in Appendix H. # 8 Discussion 

We provide a new methodology for constructing non-parametric, distribution-free confidence intervals for calibrated predictions. The main ingredient of this methodology is a coupling of isotonic calibration with conformal prediction. We have provided a theoretical framework for these confidence intervals that encompasses both validity and statistical power. Directions for further theoretical work include establishing optimal (minimax) convergence rates for the asymptotic expected width of calibrated confidence intervals, and generalizing the assumptions of Theorem 5.1. The flexibility of the main theoretical backbone of the methodology (Theorem 2.2) re-garding the choice of the prediction set Iα(Xn+1 ) provides numerous possibilities for tailoring calibrated confidence intervals to specific applications at hand. In addition to standard con-formal prediction sets that offer marginal coverage, other options such as conformal prediction sets with sample-conditional coverage (Duchi, 2025) or parametric prediction sets could provide interesting choices.

# F Construction of the conformal prediction set 

Theorem 2.2 does not enforce any restrictions on the choice of the prediction set Iα(Xn+1 ).As we have discussed in Subsection 2.3, it is often practical to use conformal prediction sets, which are valid under exchangeability. In our simulations in Section 6, we derived Iα(Xn+1 )

using conformalized quantile regression (CQR) (Romano et al., 2019). CQR is a conformal-prediction-based method that is adaptive to the test point Xn+1 . Adaptivity here translates to higher flexibility with respect to the size of the conformal prediction set. CQR obtains its adaptive properties by leveraging estimates of the upper and lower quantiles of Y | X and by taking the number of data points that are available from each part of the X-domain into account. Split CQR consists of three basic steps: • The dataset is split into a training set (X−r, Y −r), (X−r+1 , Y −r+1 ), . . . , (X0, Y 0) and a cal-ibration set (X1, Y 1), . . . , (Xn, Y n).• A quantile prediction model ̂ qγ is fitted on the training set, where γ ∈ (0 , 1) . This model can be linear quantile regression, a quantile regression forest (Meinshausen, 2006), a quantile neural network (Taylor, 2000), or any other model that predicts the γ-quantile of the conditional distribution L(Y |X = x). In our simulation, we use quantile regression forests, which perform well in terms of prediction set size and conservativeness (Romano et al., 2019, Section 6). • We then compute the scores 

si = s(Xi, Y i) = max {̂ qα/ 2(Xi) − Yi, Y i −̂ q1−α/ 2(Xi)} , i = 1 , . . . , n. 

Let ̂ q be the ⌈(1 − α)( n + 1) ⌉-th smallest calibration score – ̂ q = + ∞ if (1 −α)( n+1) > n .For any test point Xn+1 , we output the prediction set 

C(Xn+1 ) =

qα/ 2(Xn+1 ) −̂ q, ̂ q1−α/ 2(Xn+1 ) + ̂ q

The general idea of CQR is to penalize bad predictions via the score s and then inflate the miscalibrated prediction set (̂ qα/ 2(Xi),̂ q1−α/ 2(Xi)) depending on the overall penalty. Under ex-changeability, the CQR prediction sets enjoy coverage guarantees conditionally on the training set Romano et al. (2019, Theorem 1). An interesting feature of CQR is that its validity does not depend on the coverage guarantees of the base model ̂ qγ . Even if this model is arbitrarily bad, the CQR prediction sets will still satisfy the marginal coverage condition P(Yn+1 ∈ C (Xn+1 )) ≥ 1 − α. Therefore, even if we choose a different value of γ for the base model ̂ qγ , CQR will continue to be valid. Inspired by this observation, and in order to improve power, Romano et al. (2019) suggest that we tweak the parameter γ of the two fitted quantile prediction models. In their imple-mentation, they choose γ = 0 .15 by default, instead of γ = 0 .1. However, they also mention 40 that this approach can be optimized even further by treating γ as a hyperparameter, tuned by cross-validation. The cross-validation error is defined as the size of the prediction set C(Xn+1 ).Therefore, the chosen pair γ1, γ 2 – possibly a different value for each quantile level – is the one that leads on average to the smallest sets C(Xn+1 ).

## H.2 Boosted regression tree 

The second model we fit to the data is CatBoost (Prokhorenkova et al., 2018). This is a gradient boosting approach that is particularly suitable for categorical features. As in Noll et al. The base models come from a pre-specified function class (e.g. trees) and they are added to the ensemble sequentially.
