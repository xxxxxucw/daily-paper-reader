## Abstract 

Estimating the average treatment effect (ATE) remains a fundamental challenge in obser-vational studies in the presence of poor or limited covariate overlap. Although the inverse probability weighting (IPW) estimator is a widely used approach for estimating the ATE, its performance can deteriorate substantially when overlap is limited, often resulting in increased finite sample bias and unreliable confidence intervals. One common strategy is to shift attention from the original target estimand, the ATE, to alternative estimands that are less sensitive to extreme propensity scores; however, doing so changes the scientific question of interest. In this manuscript, we propose a novel ATE estimator that preserves the original target estimand, the ATE, while improving robustness to limited overlap. A key idea is that a class of estimands can be expressed by a polynomial function of a hyper-parameter characterizing the estimands. Exploiting this structure, the proposed method computes IPW estimators for a sequence of such estimands, models these estimates using a polynomial function, and extrapolates to recover the ATE. We show that the estimator has consistency and asymptotic normality under weaker overlap conditions than required

Simulation studies demonstrate that the proposed method improves estimation accuracy and interval performance in settings with limited overlap. In addition to its theoretical and empirical advantages, the proposed approach has a clear interpretation and is easy to implement using standard statistical software.

## 1 Introduction 

In observational studies, the propensity score plays a central role in adjusting for mea-sured confounders and enables the estimation of causal effects of interest (Rosenbaum and Rubin, 1983). A conventional target is the Average Treatment Effect (ATE), which represents the causal effect if all subjects were exposed to the treatment or not, and is typically estimated using propensity score methods (Imbens and Rubin, 2015). In this manuscript, we focus on the inverse probability weighting (IPW) and augmented IPW (AIPW) estimators. The IPW estimator has desirable large sample properties such as consistency and asymptotic normality, and is widely used in practice for estimating the ATE (Zhou et al., 2020; Chesnaye et al., 2022). However, the IPW estimator may suffer from extreme propensity scores close to 0 or 1, a phenomenon known as poor or limited overlap . Such extreme propensity scores inflate the variance of the IPW estimator, resulting in larger variability compared with settings without extreme propensity scores (Kang and Schafer, 2007; Li et al., 2019; Heiler and Kazak, 2021). To address limited overlap, one strategy is to modify the (causal) estimand from the ATE. An attractive alternative is the ATE for the overlap population (ATO; Li et al., 2018). The ATO focuses on subjects with propensity scores near 0.5 and can be interpreted as the causal effect among subjects in clinical equipoise (Rizk, 2025). Consequently, sub-jects with extreme propensity scores have relatively small influence on the IPW estimator 2for the ATO. Extensions such as the beta weight family (Matsouaka and Zhou, 2024) have also been proposed; however, these approaches involve a trade-off, as the resulting estimands differ from the primary target of interest, the ATE. Although Barnard et al. (2026) recently propose a procedure for selecting an optimal estimand by evaluating both variance and bias relative to the ATE, our interest lies in retaining the ATE as the target estimand. Crump et al. (2009) propose a trimmed weighting procedure that excludes observa-tions with extreme propensity scores from the IPW estimator. Their method is intuitively reasonable, easy to implement (St¨ urmer et al., 2010; Zhou et al., 2020), and has been extended in recent years. Ma and Wang (2020) and Chaudhuri and Hill (2025) intro-duce extensions of Crump et al. (2009) that determine optimal cut-off points for extreme propensity scores and reduce the bias relative to the ATE, and study their asymptotic be-havior. Although these approaches are designed to recover the ATE asymptotically even under severe lack of overlap, they require assumptions that are not necessarily intuitive and additional modeling for extrapolation beyond the trimmed region, which may com-plicate their practical implementation. In this manuscript, we propose a novel ATE estimator that leverages the structure of the beta weight family (Matsouaka and Zhou, 2024). Specifically, we obtain estimators for a sequence of estimands indexed by the hyperparameter of the beta weight family, model their relationship with the hyperparameter using polynomial regression, and ex-trapolate the fitted model to recover the ATE. The key idea underlying our approach is that the trajectory of these estimands can be approximated by a polynomial function of the hyperparameter. The proposed method, called the Polynomial approximation and Ex-trapolation to the Target estimand (PET), has consistency and asymptotic normality when the bias from the polynomial approximation is ignorable. We discuss conditions under which the approximation bias is expected to be small and propose a hyperparameter se-lection procedure that accounts for the bias-variance trade-off. An important feature of the proposed method is that it can remain asymptotically normal in settings where the 3conventional IPW estimator has an unstable or infinite asymptotic variance, provided that the estimand trajectory can be sufficiently accurately extrapolated to the ATE. We also develop an AIPW version of the PET estimator, which enjoys double robustness when either the propensity score model or the outcome model is correctly specified. The key novelty of the PET method is that it uses the trajectory of multiple estimands within the beta weight family to recover the ATE, rather than relying on a single alter-native estimand under limited overlap. Existing approaches based on a single alternative estimand can improve estimation stability but generally change the target population. In contrast, the PET method treats multiple estimands as intermediate quantities and exploits their systematic relationship with the hyperparameter to retain the ATE as the target esti-mand. To the best of our knowledge, this use of an estimand trajectory to recover the ATE has not been previously considered. The PET method cannot address the extreme settings considered by Ma and Wang (2020) and Chaudhuri and Hill (2025). However, their meth-ods rely on working extrapolation models, whereas the extrapolation model in the PET method is based on the trajectory of multiple estimands. Moreover, the procedure has a clear interpretation and is straightforward to implement, making it attractive for practical applications. In Section 2, we introduce the standard IPW estimator and related estimands. We then discuss the relationship between the overlap assumption and the existence of the variance of IPW estimators. In Section 3, we consider polynomial representations of estimands defined by the beta weight family. Based on this idea, we propose the PET method and study its theoretical properties. In Section 4, we provide further discussion of the method presented in Section 3. Specifically, we examine the approximation bias, discuss hyperparameter selection, and present sensitivity analyses for the PET method. In Section 5, we conduct simulation studies to compare the performance of the proposed method with existing approaches. 42 Preliminaries

The results are summarized in Tables 1–2, and Figures 2–3. Under the limited overlap scenario, the ATE results exhibit large ESEs and RMSEs in both small and large sample settings, indicating unstable performance. In contrast, the results for the ATO, Crump’s method, and Yang’s method show stable performance in terms of ESE. However, there are nonignorable biases even in the large sample setting, resulting in large RMSEs. For the proposed PET methods, the bias is smaller than those of the ATO, Crump’s method, and Yang’s method, while the ESE is smaller than that of the ATE. This is consistent with our objective of achieving reasonable performance among the competitors. Regarding the selection of β1 and q, Algorithm 1 can identify a reasonable combination. For the AIPW results, the proposed AIPW-PET method shows a tendency similar to that of the IPW-based estimator. Additionally, the difference between AIPW-PET and the standard AIPW estimator becomes smaller in the large sample setting, especially under good overlap, than in the small sample setting. Overall, the PET method can estimate the ATE accurately even in limited overlap settings where the estimators for the ATE perform poorly. [Tables 1–2 and Figures 2–3 here.] 

## 6 Discussion 

In this manuscript, we propose a novel estimator for the ATE, called the PET method, based on polynomial approximation and extrapolation to the ATE. This approach is mo-tivated by the fact that the weighted ATE can be represented as a polynomial function. The PET method has several theoretical and practical advantages and demonstrates better performance than existing methods, even under limited overlap. As a simple extension, another PET-based estimator can be constructed using the trimmed weight with tuning parameter α (Crump et al., 2009). In this case, the PSweight 

function (Zhou et al., 2020) can be used for Step 1 described in Section 5.2. From a theo-retical perspective, related ideas may also be developed through the smooth version of the trimmed weight (Yang and Ding, 2018); however, the resulting estimand differs slightly from the ATE (depending on an additional weight parameter), and interpretation of the re-quired assumptions may be less straightforward. The comparison between our proposed PET method and the trimmed weight-based method is of practical interest. In many cases, the approximation bias can be ignored, as discussed in Section 4.1; however, it might not be able to be ignored in some situations. To improve performance, more flexible functions can be considered in the extrapolation step, such as kernel regres-21 sion or machine learning methods. Although these approaches may reduce approximation bias, they generally have slower convergence rates and are more difficult to implement in practice. Our proposed method can be extended to various settings, such as IPW Cox models (Morita et al., 2026) and sequential treatment settings, where extreme propensity score problems occur more frequently (Seya et al., 2026). In these settings, weighted estimands are generally difficult to interpret; however, they serve only as intermediate quantities. Therefore, an ATE estimator based on an extension of the PET method can potentially improve the performance of the IPW estimator in many settings.

42 B.2 Double robustness
