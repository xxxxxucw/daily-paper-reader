Abstract 

Estimating counterfactual outcomes over time from longitu-dinal observational data is central to clinical decision support. Existing state-of-the-art methods rely on domain confusion :adversarial training that renders learned representations in-variant to treatment assignment. Yet this invariance creates a fundamental mutual information conflict because it suppresses treatment-correlated covariate signals that are independently necessary for accurate outcome prediction. In this paper, to for-malise this tension, we derived a Jensen–Shannon divergence bound on the resulting counterfactual prediction error, and de-veloped two complementary methods to resolve it. Firstly, we adapted the selective State Space Models to yield the CSSD 

(Causal State-Space model with Direct decoder ) – a parallel multi-step decoder, which can eliminate accumulated rollout error by producing all prediction horizons simultaneously in a single forward pass. Secondly, we derived CSSPD (Causal State-Space model with Predictive regularisation and Direct decoder), which augments CSSD with Contrastive Predictive Coding (CPC) and Local Information Maximisation (LIM), which reinforce temporal predictability in the balancing rep-resentation and recover the local covariate information that domain confusion destroys, respectively. On the real-world data set MIMIC-III, CSSPD achieved the state-of-the-art re-sults: it achieved lower counterfactual RMSE than the Causal Transformer method at every horizon τ ≥ 2 at O(T ) en-coder cost, with gains growing from 0.02 (2-step) to 0.07 (6-step). On Cancer Simulation (a synthetic data) across con-founding strengths γ ∈ { 0, 1, 2, 3, 4}, CSSPD outperforms CT at γ ≤ 3 (margins 25.9%–37.0%), with CSSD achieving the lowest overall average RMSE (12.7% reduction over CT), confirming the theoretical predictions of the mutual informa-tion (MI) conflict analysis. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to formalise the balancing-prediction MI conflict and to propose a structured resolution through complementary predictive and information-theoretic training objectives. 

## 1 Introduction 

Estimating individualized treatment effects (ITEs) from longitudinal observational data is a central challenge in clinical decision support (Hernán and Robins 2020; Prosperi et al. 2020). Given a patient history Ht =(A1: t−1, Y 1: t−1, X 1: t−1) (i.e., treatments, outcomes, and co-variates), the goal is to estimate E[Yt+τ (¯ a) | Ht] — the ex-

Copyright © 2027, Association for the Advancement of Artificial Intelligence (www.aaai.org). All rights reserved. 

pected potential outcome under a hypothetical treatment se-quence ¯a — under time-dependent confounding , where treat-ment assignments correlate with both patient state and future outcomes (Robins 1986; Robins et al. 2000). Deep learning methods have made substantial progress; frameworks such as RMSN (Lim et al. 2018), CRN (Bica et al. 2020), G-Net (Li et al. 2021), and the Causal Transformer (CT) (Melnychuk et al. 2022a) each build on sequential models with domain confusion or reweighting to achieve treatment-invariant rep-resentations. Two structural limitations motivate the present work. Lim-itation 1 — The balancing-prediction tension. Domain confusion introduces an inherent mutual information conflict (formalised in the theory section). Suppressing the mutual information between the balancing representation (a learned entity encoding up to time t, containing covariates and out-come history) and treatment, i.e., I(BR t; At), forces the en-coder to discard features of Ht that are correlated with treat-ment At, but many such features, notably current vital signs 

Xt, are also independently predictive of future outcomes 

Yt+τ . Domain confusion therefore systematically removes the representational information the decoder needs, creating a bottleneck that limits counterfactual accuracy regardless of model capacity. Recent empirical analysis (Huang et al. 2024) confirms this covariate information loss in CRN and CT, corroborating the theoretical concern we formalise here. 

Limitation 2 — Autoregressive rollout error. Standard multi-step decoders predict future outcomes one step at a time, feeding each prediction back as input for the next. This autoregressive rollout compounds errors geometrically: if a single-step prediction carries error ε, the τ -step prediction carries error O(ετ ) (Taieb and Atiya 2015). At the prediction horizons relevant to clinical decision support ( τ ≥ 2), this rollout error accumulates rapidly and limits counterfactual accuracy independently of representation quality. To address both limitations simultaneously, this paper de-velops CSSPD . Our target is to make multi-step-ahead coun-terfactual outcome prediction, that is, given Ht and a hy-pothetical future treatment sequence ¯at+1: t+τmax , we want to produce simultaneous estimates ˆYt+1 , . . . , ˆYt+τmax for all horizons in a single forward pass, without autoregressive roll-out. CSSPD introduces two core innovations: the encoder and the decoder. For the encoder , we adapt the selective State Space Models (Gu and Dao 2023) that process each arXiv:2608.08288v1 [cs.LG] 8 Aug 2026 input stream in O(T ) time while retaining the capacity to selectively integrate long-range history. This should be com-pared to the O(T 2) cost of CT (Melnychuk et al. (2022a)). A CausalGatedMixer fuses the treatment, outcome, and covariate streams through scalar gates whose initialisations encode the structural causal graph, producing a Balancing Representation ( BR t) that satisfies the backdoor criterion (Pearl 2009). A parallel multi-step decoder then produces all 

τmax counterfactual predictions simultaneously, eliminating the O(ϵτ ) error accumulation of autoregressive rollout (Taieb and Atiya 2015). For the training objectives , CSSPD aug-ments domain confusion with two auxiliary regularisers that resolve the MI conflict - a CPC head that preserves temporal predictability in BR t, and a LIM head that recovers covari-ate information that domain confusion would otherwise sup-press, each targeting a distinct facet of the representational bottleneck without compromising treatment invariance. Our contributions are: 

1. We proposed a causal state-space model with direct en-coder (CSSD) for causal inference - a selective SSM en-coder with a parallel multi-step decoder that produces τ

counterfactual predictions in a single forward pass, elim-inating rollout error accumulation. 2. We further proposed a model named Causal State-Space model with Predictive regularisation and Direct decoder (CSSPD), which is a CSSD augmented with CPC and LIM objectives. This model resolves the MI conflict with-out sacrificing treatment invariance. 3. We formalised the balancing-prediction MI conflict and derived a Jensen–Shannon bound on the counterfactual prediction error induced by domain confusion. 4. We conducted empirical evaluation on the real-world data set MIMIC-III and the synthetic data set Cancer Simu-lation, showing CSSPD reduces counterfactual RMSE at all horizons τ ≥ 2 on MIMIC-III (growing margin 0.02– 0.07) and outperforms CT at γ ≤ 3 on Cancer Simulation (margins 25.9%–37.0%), with gains consistent with the MI conflict theory.

The result is a representation BR t that is invariant to treatment assignment, satisfying the backdoor criterion. It is precisely this enforced invariance, however, that introduces the mutual information conflict we formalise in the theory section .

## 3 Model Architecture 

The proposed CSSPD architecture is shown in Figure 1. Three input streams; treatments ( ˆAt), outcomes ( ˆYt), and covariates ( ˆXt), are encoded by independent SSM layers and fused by a CausalGatedMixer . The resulting balancing representation BR t is fed to a parallel multi-step decoder and CPC/LIM regularisation heads that resolve the MI conflict introduced by domain confusion (theory section) .CSSPD combines two distinct modifications to the base encoder: a structural change to the treatment stream’s se-quence operator, and the CPC and LIM contrastive objec-tives. To quantify the independent contribution of each, we introduce two additional variants. CHSD (Causal Hybrid State-Space Decoder ) applies only the structural change, re-placing the treatment-stream SSM with the standard multi-head self-attention of Melnychuk et al. (2022a), giving the encoder lossless access to the full treatment history while re-taining SSMs for the outcome and covariate streams. CHSPD 

(Causal Hybrid State-Space model with Predictive regular-isation and Direct decoder ) combines both modifications, augmenting CHSD with the CPC and LIM objectives from CSSPD with results details in the experiment section .

Contrastive Predictive Coding (CPC) and Local Information Maximisation (LIM) Heads 

Domain confusion drives I(BR t; At) → 0 but inadvertently suppresses I(BR t; Yt+τ ) (temporal predictive information) and I(BR t; Xt) (local covariate information), creating the MI conflict. CPC and LIM are targeted regularisers operating on disjoint pathways that restore these two quantities. 

CPC. A CPCHead applies K linear predictors fk to BR t,each attempting to predict BR t+k via InfoNCE over in-batch negatives (van den Oord et al. 2018). InfoNCE lower-bounds 

I(BR t; BR t+k), so maximising it forces BR t to retain tem-poral structure. The connection to I(BR t; Yt+τ ) — the quantity suppressed by domain confusion — follows from the Data Processing Inequality (DPI): for any determinis-tic function f , I(Z; f (X)) ≤ I(Z; X), since processing can only reduce information. Applying DPI twice: (i) since 

BR t+k = ϕ(Ht+k) is a deterministic function of future history Ht+k, we have I(BR t; BR t+k) ≤ I(BR t; Ht+k);

(ii) since Yt+τ is a component of Ht+k for k ≥ τ ,

I(BR t; Yt+τ ) ≤ I(BR t; Ht+k). Both BR t+k and Yt+τ are therefore compressed views of the same underlying history; maximising I(BR t; BR t+k) prevents BR t from discarding temporal structure in Ht+k, which supports I(BR t; Yt+τ )

and counteracts the domain confusion collapse. A LIM head maximises I(˜ xt; BR t) — a tractable proxy for I(Xt; BR t) — via InfoNCE, with ˜xt detached so gra-dients flow into the encoder only through BR t, recovering covariate information that CPC does not.

This section provides the theoretical foundation for the CSSPD design. We establish three results. Proposition 1 proves that the MI conflict is not a modelling artefact but an information-theoretic inevitability: any encoder trained with domain confusion is forced to discard outcome-predictive information. Theorem 1 then quantifies the resulting cost, bounding the joint factual and counterfactual prediction er-ror in terms of the Jensen–Shannon divergence between treatment-group representations — making the balancing– prediction trade-off mathematically precise. Finally, Propo-sition 2 proves that the CPC and LIM objectives provide tractable lower bounds on the two MI quantities suppressed by domain confusion ( Ipred and Iloc ), showing formally how CSSPD resolves the conflict that domain confusion creates. 

The Mutual Information Conflict 

Domain confusion suppresses I(BR t; At) by removing treatment-correlated features from BR t. Because covariates 

Xt are correlated with both At (observational data) and Yt+τ

(outcomes), this inadvertently destroys outcome-predictive information — the MI conflict . Let ϕ : H → Rd maps his-tory Ht to BR t. Define three mutual information quantities: 

Ibal = I(BR t; At), (18) 

Ipred = I(BR t; Yt+τ ), (19) 

Iloc = I(BR t; Xt). (20) 

Definition 1 (MI Conflict) . An encoder ϕ suffers the MI conflict if there exists no representation BR t = ϕ(Ht) that simultaneously satisfies I(BR t; At) = 0 (perfect balanc-ing) and I(BR t; Yt+τ ) = I(Ht; Yt+τ ) (no loss of outcome-predictive information). The conflict is non-vacuous when-ever I(Xt; Yt+τ | At) > 0, i.e. covariates carry outcome-predictive information beyond what treatment assignment alone reveals. In the following, we show that Theorem 1 quantifies this trade-off; CSSPD resolves it via CPC (maximising Ipred ) and LIM (maximising Iloc ) (Proposition 2). 

Formalisation 

Proposition 1 (MI Conflict under Domain Confusion) . Sup-pose Xt̸ ⊥⊥ At (confounding: covariates and treatment are correlated) and Xt → Yt+τ (covariates predict outcomes). Then any encoder ϕ that reduces Ibal via domain confusion also reduces Iloc , and consequently reduces the upper bound on Ipred .Proof sketch. Domain confusion minimises Ibal =

I(BR t; At) by suppressing all features of Ht that are cor-related with At. Since Xt̸ ⊥⊥ At, Xt carries information about At; therefore suppressing I(BR t; At) also reduces 

I(BR t; Xt) = Iloc . Since Xt → Yt+τ , applying the chain rule of mutual information: 

I(BR t; Yt+τ ) ≤ I(BR t; Xt) + I(BR t; Yt+τ | Xt).

Under the assumption that Xt is the dominant confounder (i.e. the residual term I(BR t; Yt+τ | Xt) is small relative to 

I(BR t; Xt)), reducing Iloc consequently reduces the achiev-able Ipred . This assumption is explicit, the proposition holds conditionally on the regime where Xt is the primary media-tor from Ht to Yt+τ , which is precisely the clinical setting of interest (current vital signs dominate short-horizon outcome prediction).

The Datasets 

Cancer Simulation (Lim et al. 2018). A PK-PD sim-ulation of NSCLC tumour dynamics (Geng et al. 2017) under binary chemotherapy/radiotherapy interventions; the confounding parameter γ ∈ { 0, 1, 2, 3, 4} controls treatment-assignment bias. Ground-truth counterfactuals are available for all patients under all treatment sequences, enabling ex-act RMSE evaluation. The data was splitted to training set 

10 ,000 , validation 1,000 , and test set 1,000 , the maximum sequence length set to Tmax = 60 and τmax = 5 . Normalised RMSE on counterfactual trajectories (divided by outcome standard deviation) were used as the metric for evaluation. 

MIMIC-III Real (Johnson et al. 2016). De-identified longitudinal ICU records of 5,000 adult patients with two binary treatments (vasopressor and ventilation), 25 time-varying vital covariates, and the diastolic blood pressure as the scalar outcome. Data split: 3,500 train / 750 val / 750 

test; Tmax = 60 , and τmax = 5 . Since ground-truth counter-factuals are unobservable in real data, we report normalised RMSE on factual held-out trajectories (divided by within-cohort outcome standard deviation), which is a necessary condition for counterfactual accuracy. 

Baseline Methods 

We compared against RMSN (Lim et al. 2018) (IPW-LSTM), 

CRN (Bica et al. 2020) (domain-confusion GRU), G-Net (Li et al. 2021) ( g-computation RNN), and CT (Melnychuk et al. 2022a) (primary baseline). We include CSSD (ours) — parallel decoder and domain confusion, but no CPC/LIM — to isolate contrastive-objective gains from architecture. 

Implementation Details 

All models are implemented in PyTorch Lightning and trained on a single CPU ( Tmax = 60 ; dmodel = 32 , dBR = 24 ,

dstate = 16 , L = 2 SSM layers). Adam optimiser was used with lr = 10 −4 (MIMIC-III: 64 batches, 300 epochs) or lr = 10 −3 (Cancer Simulation: 128 batches, 200 epochs); early stopping used patience with 20 epochs. CPC and LIM heads were activated after 120 warm-up epochs; loss weights were set to λMS = 3 .5, λCPC = 0 .05 , and λLIM = 0 .1 which were selected by grid search on the MIMIC-III validation set. To assess the impact of the regulation components, we performed ablation studies across five distinct models on MIMIC-III Real: the SSM encoder alone (CSS, i.e., Causal State Space model), The SSM encoder with τ −head decoder (CSSD), the CSSD with CPC (CSSD+CPC), the CSSD with LIM (CSSD+LIM), and the full CSSPD (CSSD+CPC+LIM). Adding the parallel τ -head decoder (CSSD), yields a score of 9.002, recovering this gap by elim-inating O(ϵτ ) rollout error and allowing each horizon head to specialise independently. ## 6 Discussion 

The MI conflict in practice. CSSD without contrastive objectives matches CT at 1-step (4.74 vs. 4.59) but falls be-hind at τ ≥ 3, confirming that rollout error is not the primary failure mode. CSSPD reverses this: its margin over CT grows monotonically from 0.02 at τ = 2 to 0.07 at τ = 6 , indicat-ing that the signature of CPC restoring temporal predictive information. CHSD (i.e., SCM gates without CPC/LIM) sug-gests that architectural constraints alone may be insufficient to resolve the MI conflict; its instability at γ ≥ 2 implicates a residual identifiability gap. We reserve this for future work. 

Limitations. Evaluation is limited to a single MIMIC-III site and diastolic blood pressure; broader evaluation across outcomes, sites, and multi-valued or continuous treatment regimes are natural extensions. No formal significance tests are reported; the 5-seed mean ± std protocol follows Mel-nychuk et al. (2022a) for comparability, and effect sizes are consistent across seeds. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We formalised the MI conflict — the provable tension by which domain confusion suppresses the covariate features most needed for outcome prediction — and derived a Jensen– Shannon bound (Theorem 1) showing this conflict directly inflates counterfactual prediction error. CSSPD resolves it with two complementary objectives: CPC restores tempo-ral predictive information ( Ipred ) and LIM recovers local covariate information ( Iloc ), together improving the error bound without sacrificing treatment invariance. Empirically, CSSPD outperforms CT at γ ≤ 3 on Cancer Simulation (mar-gins 25.9%–37.0%) and at all horizons τ ≥ 2 on MIMIC-III Real, with gains growing monotonically with prediction horizon — consistent with MI conflict resolution. CSSPD exhibits instability at the extreme confounding level γ = 4 , a limitation shared with the hybrid variants and a direction for future work. 

## Ethics Statement 

MIMIC-III data is de-identified and publicly available (John-son et al. 2016); Cancer Simulation is fully synthetic; no new patient data was collected. Counterfactual models must not be used for individual clinical decisions without appropriate clinical oversight. 

## A Related Work 

Sequential counterfactual estimation. Estimating coun-terfactual outcomes over time has been addressed by a se-quence of increasingly expressive models. RMSN (Lim et al. 2018) re-weights training samples using inverse-probability-of-treatment weights (IPW) via recurrent marginal structural networks to debias the observational distribution. CRN (Bica et al. 2020) introduces domain-adversarial training, apply-ing a gradient reversal layer (Ganin et al. 2016) to enforce treatment-invariant representations, building on the IPW-weighted adversarial approach of Lim et al. (2018). G-Net (Li et al. 2021) uses g-computation with a recurrent architec-ture to estimate potential outcomes under dynamic treatment regimes. The Causal Transformer (CT) (Melnychuk et al. 2022a) applies multi-head causal self-attention with domain confusion, achieving state-of-the-art performance at the cost of O(T 2) encoding complexity. 

State-space models for sequences. Selective state-space models (Mamba; Gu and Dao 2023) achieve O(T ) sequence modelling with input-dependent transition matrices that en-able selective long-range memory. Earlier S4 models (Gu et al. 2022) establish structured SSMs as efficient alternatives to self-attention. S5 (Smith et al. 2023) simplifies the pa-rameterisation further. CSSPD is, to our knowledge, the first application of selective SSMs to longitudinal causal infer-ence under domain confusion. 

Contrastive representation learning. Contrastive Predic-tive Coding (CPC; van den Oord et al. 2018) proposes In-foNCE as a lower bound on mutual information, learning rep-resentations by predicting future embeddings. DIM (Hjelm et al. 2019) extends this framework to maximise local mutual information. CausalContrastive (Bouchattaoui et al. 2024) applies contrastive objectives to counterfactual estimation but uses a Transformer encoder and does not formalise the MI conflict. Our LIM objective is inspired by the local MI maximisation principle of Hjelm et al. (2019), adapted to covariate recovery in the causal inference setting.

(S2) Under the dominant-confounder assumption — that Xt

From Step 1, domain confusion reduces Iloc , which con-sequently tightens the upper bound on Ipred , completing the proof.

Baseline Hyperparameters 

Baselines (RMSN, CRN, CT) are trained using the hyperpa-rameters reported in Melnychuk et al. (2022a) (Table 9 of that paper), without re-tuning on our specific splits. This is in-tentional: CSSPD is not given an advantage from additional hyperparameter search that the baselines did not receive. Specifically, CT uses hidden size 64, 2 attention layers, 4 at-tention heads, and dropout 0.1. CRN uses hidden size 64 and 2 recurrent layers. RMSN uses the stabilised IPW weighting with separate encoder and decoder RNNs (hidden size 64). G-Net uses 2 recurrent layers (hidden size 64) and is trained with g-computation on sequential potential outcomes. All baselines use Adam with learning rate 10 −3 and the same early-stopping patience of 20 on validation RMSE.

Per-step analysis. CSSPD trails CT at τ = 1 (4.74 vs.

The CPC objective looks k ≥ 2 steps ahead and provides no direct lower-bound on one-step mutual infor-mation; similarly, LIM aligns covariate representations but does not address one-step temporal prediction. Cancer Simulation Per-Confounding Per-Step Breakdown

γ and τ . At γ = 4 , CT achieves lower RMSE, suggesting that at very high confounding the 60-epoch CPC/LIM warm-up is insufficient to fully counteract the domain-confusion MI conflict before the end of training; a longer warm-up or additional tuning of λCPC and λLIM at γ = 4 warrants further investigation.
