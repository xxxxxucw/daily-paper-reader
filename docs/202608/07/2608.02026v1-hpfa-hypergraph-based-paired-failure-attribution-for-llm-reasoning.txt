# Abstract 

Reflection is a powerful mechanism for LLM reasoning, yet its effectiveness hinges on accurately attributing failures to specific reasoning steps, a capa-bility that current models notably lack. Existing failure attribution methods either require expensive step-by-step counterfactual testing that scales poorly with trajectory length, or treat reasoning traces as flat sequences that ignore the inherent non-linear logical dependencies. We propose a hypergraph-based paired failure attribution (HPFA) framework that at-tributes the failure root cause by comparing the hyperedges of the targeted failure reasoning path against a reference successful path. By reducing the search space, our method efficiently localizes root causes and enables scalable synthesis of attribution data for training a lightweight attributor model via supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning. Experiments on mathematical reasoning and agentic coding tasks demonstrate that HPFA can dramatically increase attribution accuracy and efficiency, and the trained attributor consistently improves reasoning accuracy at test time, outperforming baselines that lack graph structure or paired analysis. 

# 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in complex rea-soning tasks, from mathematical problem solving to multi-step agent decision-making (Wei et al., 2022; OpenAI, 2024). A key enabler of these advances is reflective reasoning : given execution outcomes, an LLM revisits the reasoning trace, identifies potential mistakes, and iteratively refines its output (Shinn et al., 2023; Madaan et al., 2023; Yin & Wang, 2025a; Ding et al., 2026). The capability is fundamental to optimize LLM systems (Yuksekgonul et al., 2025; Yin & Wang, 2025b), build complex compound agentic systems (Agrawal et al., 2026), or optimize formal programs for robots (Yang et al., 2026). However, effective reflective reasoning critically depends on failure attribution : localizing the intermediate step that is the root cause of downstream failures . Recent evidence shows that LLMs are surprisingly poor at locating their own reasoning errors, even though they can correct mistakes once the error location is provided (Tyen et al., 2024). This limitation makes failed reasoning hard to explain and debug, especially in high-stakes settings where understanding the failure cause is essential for trust and safety (Qu & Färber, 2026; Braitsch et al., 2026). Zhang et al. (2025b) defined failure attribution in multi-agent systems to identify the failure cause without relying on the model’s own introspective judgment. They also introduced the Who&When benchmark, showing that existing LLMs have limited ability to localize failure points. A growing body of work has attempted to tackle this problem. We therefore focus on constructing scalable attribution data for training such an attributor. However, reliably attributing complex, long-chain trajectories remains challenging for two reasons. (1) Expensive data synthesis. Step-by-step counterfactual labeling repairs each candidate step and re-executes the downstream trajectory, so its cost scales quickly with trajectory length (Zhang et al., 2025a). Tasks such as mathematical proofs and agent planning containing dozens of steps could suffer from expensive large-scale synthesis. (2) Non-linear dependencies. Prior methods represent trajectories as linear sequences, overlooking non-local and higher-order dependencies across steps. For example, in a multi-hop proof, a later conclusion may depend jointly on several earlier parallel math derivations that are far apart in the reasoning trace. (1) Paired trajectories for counterfactual search. For each query, a successful trajectory provides a valid reference for the failed trajectory, which reduces search to divergences instead of probing every step. (2) Dependency hypergraphs capture non-linear error propagation. We represent atomic reasoning steps as nodes and their higher-order depen-dencies as hyperedges, allowing attribution to follow non-linear dependency paths back to upstream root causes. # 2 Related Work 

Failure Attribution in LLM Reasoning. Failure attribution plays an important role in reflective reasoning. It identifies the root cause in a failed reasoning trajectory and provides feedback signals for reflection or RL training (Lightman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024). Self reflection comprises error detection, error localization, and reasoning revision, with localization connecting failure recognition to targeted revision (Samanta et al., 2026). As LLMs struggle to locate logical mistakes, providing failure causes can improve revision accuracy (Tyen et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). (1) Methods for acquiring this capability span three paradigms. Prompting methods elicit self-rethinking, error type analysis, or first error localization, but remain limited by the model’s own verification ability (Tong et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Samanta et al., 2026). (2) Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) methods use erroneous traces and mistake annotations to train reasoners that learn from mistakes, while dedicated mistake locators can be trained from step labels (Tong et al., 2024; Tyen et al., 2024). (3) In reinforcement learning, process reward models (PRMs) play a similar role. PRMs provide stepwise critics that localize the first error (Zha et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026), which can assign credit to correct prefixes and erroneous suffixes (Liu et al., 2026). 

Failure Attribution in Agentic Systems. Different from LLMs, agentic systems are com-pound systems where failures can stem from reasoning, memory, tool execution and agent communication (Ji et al., 2024; Ning et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2025; Cemri et al., 2025), as upstream errors can propagate across components and subsequent decisions, thereby sepa-rating observed failures from their responsible components and originating steps (Zhu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a;b). Early approaches frame agent failure analysis as specification-based verification. For example, PDoctor checks plans against constraints and test oracles (Ji et al., 2024), whereas VeriLA verifies outputs against human-defined criteria (Sung et al., 2025). These methods can detect known failure patterns but cannot determine which com-ponent caused an end-to-end failure or at which step the failure first occurred. Who&When marks the shift from specification-based verification to automated failure attribution (Zhang et al., 2025b). Following this shift, AgentDebug uses a taxonomy to retrospectively diagnose full trajectories and generate corrective feedback (Zhu et al., 2025), whereas AgenTracer trains a lightweight attributor for agent- and step-level localization using labels derived from counterfactual replay and fault injection (Zhang et al., 2025a).and critical step (Zhang et al., 2025a). Despite the success of the aforementioned methods in reasoning or agentic systems, there are two remaining challenges: (1) Such a labeling method is costly with repeated interventions and executions. (2) Existing methods do not explicitly model complex dependencies among agents, steps, and environmental factors. Our work aims to address the two challenges in failure attribution for LLM reasoning and agentic systems.

3.1 Failure Localization via Hypergraph-based Paired Failure Attribution (HPFA) 

A direct way to localize root cause is to probe every step of a failed trace with counterfactual rollouts R, which scales poorly with trace length and perturbation budget, especially on long-horizon reasoning or agentic tasks where each probe is expensive. To address the challenge, we propose Hypergraph-based Paired Failure Attribution (HPFA) in Algorithm 1, which reduces the search space by providing a successful reasoning reference, and guiding the search in non-linear dependency via a hypergraph. 

Step 1: Paired-trajectory generation. To narrow down the search space of failure causes, we directly sample additional reasoning trajectories from the same model and find a successful one as a reference. Prior work on test-time scaling has shown that repeatedly sampling from the same model can surface at least one correct solution far more effectively (Snell et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2021; Cobbe et al., 2021). As a result, the search complexity is proportional to 1 /p instead of reasoning steps, where p is the probability of getting the successful trace in one shot. In HPFA, we introduce trajectory pairing : once a successful rollout T + emerges on a query, comparing it to a failure T − supplies a reference of valid intermediate structure and narrows root-cause search to genuine divergences instead of probing every possible step in the trace. For each query Q, we roll out the target LLM or agent under a fixed protocol until both outcomes appear or we exhaust a per-query budget of K attempts. If only one outcome appears, we drop Q. We keep one success-failure pair (T +, T −) with 

1(T +) = 1 and 1(T −) = 0. When several qualify, we keep the pair with the closest length. Trajectory-sampling prompt templates (coding) are listed in Section D. 

Step 2: Dependency hypergraph construction. Even with reference, it is still challenging to find the failure location by comparison, because the step-to-step dependencies in reasoning could be non-linear, complicating the search. For example, later content relies on earlier and non-consecutive propositions, tool observations, or subgoals. Rather than treating the trace as a flat sequence, we summarize these dependencies in a structured graph-like object G = ( V, Eh) over aligned abstract steps, so that we can walk backward from the final prediction toward regions where a root cause is plausible. 3.2 Boosting Reasoning by Test-time Attributors 

To boost the reasoning at test time where ground truths or references are not available, we train a lightweight attributor πϕ that maps (Q, Tfail ) to a structured diagnosis: the erroneous step, its error type, and a concise description of the mistaken content. Supervision is harvested by HPFA: each training tuple includes the failure trace T −, the verified root index k, a short rationale, and optionally the counterfactual span used when a repair rollout succeeds, i.e., 1(R(T −, k, s′)) = 1. 

Training attributor. The training follows a two-stage schedule that separates imitation from on-policy refinement. (1) SFT. We fit πϕ offline with the standard causal language-modeling objective. Prompts concatenate the task instruction, Q, and the indexed failure trace; targets are templated diagnoses (step, error type, mistaken content). For math, paired and negative-only root-cause prompts (Section D) match the root_cause tag schema used by the format reward. (2) GRPO. We use the final model to initiate the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) on failures rolled out on-policy from the target model. Thus, the training distribution can match deployment-time errors. For each (Q, Tfail ) we sample a group of attributions from πϕ, score each with the sequence-level reward below, normalize within-group rewards to advantages (zero mean, unit vari-ance, with a small stability constant), and update using clipped policy ratios and KL regularization to πref . Reflective reasoning with attributor. In Algorithm 2, we incorporate the trained attributor to enhance the reasoning at test time. Different from failure localization, the attributor here is not aware of the ground truth or the successful path. At test time, the system sees a single backbone rollout for Q. We call πϕ on (Q, T ), without graph or T +. We design the algorithm to (1) infer the correctness of the answer and (2) attribute and correct the trajectories upon the assumed wrong answer. We use verbal confidence score S(T ) to decide whether to trigger the attribution. If S(T ) < τ, the LLM passes its reasoning trajectory to the attributor, and then regenerates the trajectory based on the output of the attribution model. # 4 Experiments 

We organize evaluation along two components: (i) failure localization with HPFA, and (ii) a trained attributor used for reflective reasoning. 

4.1 Failure Localization 

We evaluate HPFA in the paired-trajectory setting: for each query we contrast a successful trajectory T + with a failed one T −, build a dependency hypergraph, and use counterfactual rollouts to verify the attributed root step (Section 3.1). The subsections below specify where 

we test (datasets), how we obtain pairs and baselines, what we measure, and then interpret accuracy, convergence, and cost together. 

Datasets. We evaluate HPFA in three representative settings: mathematical reasoning, agentic coding, and a privacy scenario. (i) Mathematical reasoning: We study multi-step mathematical problem solving on MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021) and GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), using their official training splits. (ii) Agentic coding: We study coding agents on KodCode (Xu et al., 2025) and MBPP (Austin et al., 2021). We use the KodCode training split and a 3 : 1 train–evaluation split on MBPP. (iii) Privacy scenario: We study reasoning failures caused by privacy-preserving de-identification. To construct the dataset, we use PrivateAI to de-identify CoQA-based inputs (Reddy et al., 2019). More details are in Section C.

# 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we introduced Hypergraph-based Paired Failure Attribution (HPFA), a framework that localizes the root causes of LLM reasoning failures by contrasting successful and failed trajectories and modeling their dependencies with a hypergraph. Paired trajectories narrow counterfactual verification to meaningful divergences, while hypergraph-guided search captures non-linear interactions that flat step sequences miss. Across mathematical reasoning, coding, and privacy-sensitive tasks, HPFA achieves a better localization cost– success trade-off than stepwise counterfactual and retrospective baselines, with the largest gains on long and heterogeneous traces. The verified attributions further provide scalable supervision for training a lightweight attributor, which improves reflective reasoning from a single trace on repair-capable backbones without modifying the underlying reasoning model. These results demonstrate that structured root cause feedback offers a practical path toward more reliable and scalable reflection.

# A Reliability of Hypergraph Construction 

Validation targets. We separately evaluate atomic decomposition, cross-trajectory merge decisions, and hyperedge extraction, followed by an end-to-end review of the finished hypergraphs. 

Cross-model agreement. On 200 problems, we compare the Qwen3-8B builder with three independent comparison models. Each model re-runs the same construction stage on the same input. An LLM alignment judge then matches units that express the same operation or dependency, allowing for wording differences. The units are atomic steps in stage 1, cross-trajectory merge decisions in stage 2, and hyperedges in stage 3. We aggregate matched units across problems and report micro-F1 agreement: 100 · 2M

|builder | + |comparison | ,where M is the number of matched builder–comparison unit pairs. Table 7 reports the resulting agreement. 

Human review. Cross-model agreement measures construction stability rather than cor-rectness. To complement it, two human annotators independently review 50 finished hy-pergraphs for the end-to-end reasonableness of their atomic steps, cross-trajectory merges, and dependencies. A graph passes only when both annotators judge it reasonable; 44 of 50 graphs pass under this strict criterion, yielding an 88% pass rate. 

Conclusion. Agreement ranges from 76.3% to 92.2% across stages and comparison models. The semantic-equivalence stage has the lowest agreement, consistent with the ambiguity of deciding when differently worded steps should be merged. Together with the strict 88% human pass rate, these results support the stability of the construction while leaving semantic alignment as its main source of uncertainty. # C Privacy Scenario Details 

This appendix details the privacy scenario introduced in Section 4.1, where failures caused by privacy-preserving de-identification must be distinguished from errors in the LLM’s reasoning. 

Dataset and experimental setup. We construct the privacy slice from CoQA conversational questions (Reddy et al., 2019). We retain 108 cases in which the model fails on the de-identified input and the privacy checks below are satisfied; attribution then determines whether each failure is scrub-induced or reasoning-induced. All LLM-based components use GPT-4o, while PrivateAI is used only for de-identification. 

Evaluation procedure. For each instance, we apply the following four steps: 1. De-identify. PrivateAI replaces sensitive spans in the original query q and its context with typed placeholders, producing the scrubbed view ˜ q.2. Generate paired trajectories. In controlled evaluation, we obtain a reference trajectory 

T + from q and a failed privacy-constrained trajectory T − from ˜q; only ˜q represents the input available in deployment. 3. Localize and verify. HPFA aligns the two trajectories, constructs their dependency hypergraph, and counterfactually verifies the earliest divergent step. 4. Attribute the cause. We classify the verified step using the taxonomy below. Scrub-induced failures motivate changes to redaction or placeholder policies, whereas reasoning failures motivate changes to prompting or decoding. 

Privacy constraints. We apply two checks. First, the scrubber must only replace content rather than introduce new factual text. Let strip ( ˜q) remove placeholder tokens from the scrubbed query; after whitespace normalization, we require strip ( ˜q) ∈ Substr (q). (2) Here Substr (q) is the set of normalized contiguous substrings of q. Second, the generated answer must not reproduce any private field from q verbatim. We evaluate correctness 16 Published as a conference paper at COLM 2026 against the CoQA reference answer, and a counterfactual repair counts as successful only if it is correct and passes the no-leakage check. 

Taxonomy of scrub-related and reasoning failures. To make the attribution actionable, we classify the localized cause using the five categories in Table 10. The first, second, fourth, and fifth categories indicate failures introduced by the privacy transformation; Reasoning Failure is reserved for cases in which the scrubbed input still contains all evidence needed to answer correctly. Table 10: Error taxonomy for the privacy scenario. 

Category Localized cause 

Cross-reference The same entity is replaced by different placeholders, preventing the model from recognizing that the mentions co-refer. Embraced Scrub-bing The target answer is hidden within or split across scrubbed spans, making it unavailable for extraction or reconstruction. Reasoning Failure All necessary evidence is preserved in ˜q, but the model makes an incorrect inference. Unresolved Coref-erence Redaction prevents a pronoun or generic expression from being linked to its antecedent. Scrubbing Diver-gence An entity is represented inconsistently across the question and context, such as a placeholder in one view and plain text or a different label in another. 

Takeaway. This privacy scenario extends the localization evaluation in Section 4.1 beyond ordinary reasoning errors. By identifying both the failed reasoning step and whether its cause lies in scrubbing, HPFA provides a verified target for repair without treating every failure as a decoder problem.
