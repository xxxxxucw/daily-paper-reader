# Semiparametric Prediction with Efficient Interval Length under a Right-Censored Covariate 

Kihyun Han ∗ Yanyuan Ma † Karen Marder ‡ Tanya P. Garcia §

Abstract 

In cohort studies, a clinical outcome sometimes cannot be measured at a patient’s visit when a resource-intensive measurement is skipped, and a prediction interval provides a range of values the unmeasured outcome may plausibly take, reported with the prediction level. Constructing the interval requires knowing how far the patient’s disease has advanced, and a time-to-event covariate, the time until an anchoring event common to all patients, places each patient at a comparable point in the disease. For many patients, however, this event has not occurred by the end of their follow-up, so the time-to-event covariate is right-censored: its value is not observed and is known only to exceed the time to study exit. Conformal prediction methods can be adapted to this right-censored covariate setting, but these methods produce intervals whose length and coverage rate vary substantially from study to study. Such variability cannot support reliable clinical decisions, since the outcome range would overly depend on the study sample rather than on the true disease process. We develop a semiparametric prediction method that recasts the construction of the prediction interval as semiparametric estimation of its half-length, using the distributional information that conformal prediction methods discard. The method achieves the smallest possible variance in the estimated half-length and remains consis-tent even when the model for the time-to-event covariate or the model for the censoring time is misspecified. Simulation studies confirm substantially more stable interval lengths and coverage rates than conformal prediction methods across censoring rates. In a Huntington disease study with 77.2% censoring, our method achieves reliable coverage with stable interval lengths, while conformal prediction methods produce either persistent undercoverage or intervals too wide to be informative. 

Keywords: conformal prediction; double robustness; Huntington disease; nuisance model; right-censoring; semiparametric efficiency.

Huntington disease progresses for years before clinical signs appear, and studying this period relies on cohort studies, in which patients attend clinic visits and a range of measurements—from cognitive and motor testing to brain imaging—is taken [13, 14]. However, not every measurement is completed at every visit: a resource-intensive measurement may be skipped when the equipment or trained staff it requires is unavailable that day. An outcome, such as a cognitive score or a composite score of several measures, is then unmeasured whenever a required measurement is missing. What is needed at such a visit is the outcome the patient would plausibly have shown, reported not as a single value but as a range, since a single value would not convey how uncertain the unmeasured outcome is. Such a range is a prediction interval, reported together with a prediction level, the probability the range is intended to contain the outcome. Unlike a traditional pre-symptomatic outcome trajectory, which averages over the population, the prediction interval is constructed from patient-specific features and can therefore support downstream analyses such as risk assessment of individual patients. Constructing the prediction interval requires knowing how far a patient’s disease has advanced. The solution is to define a single time scale on which every patient can be located—one anchored to an event common to all patients, so that the same value of the scale means the same disease advancement across patients. A natural anchoring event is Stage 2 of the Huntington Disease Integrated Staging System (HD-ISS), the stage at which clinical signs first appear [15]: measuring each patient by the time until they reach Stage 2 places all patients on a common scale, so that two patients with the same time to Stage 2 are at a comparable point in their disease. Yet the time to Stage 2 is not observed for most patients, since many leave the study, or the study ends, before Stage 2 is reached, so the time to Stage 2 is known only to exceed the time to study exit. The time to Stage 2 is a time-to-event covariate, and the time to study exit is a censoring time; a time-to-event covariate known only to exceed a censoring time is right-censored, and we call this the right-censored covariate setting. Prediction intervals with a valid coverage have been developed for a range of prediction problems [5, 16], but not for the right-censored covariate setting. Conformal prediction offers a natural starting point, because conformal prediction constructs a prediction interval with valid coverage [19, 8] and requires only quantities computed from the realized sample. Conformal prediction adapted to the right-censored covariate setting builds the residual directly from the right-censored covariate, so that the time-to-event covariate need not be recovered before prediction can proceed. The prediction interval is then constructed in two steps: a center is fixed first, a predicted value of the outcome from the observed data, and a half-length is set second, so that the prediction interval runs from the center minus the half-length to the center plus the half-length. Constructing the prediction interval reduces to choosing the half-length, because the center is fixed in advance. The half-length is chosen from the residuals, the distances between the observed outcomes and the center: the half-length is set so that a chosen fraction of the residuals fall within the half-length. Choosing the half-length from the residuals alone, however, produces a half-length, and therefore an interval length, that varies substantially from one sample to the next. The empirical quantile of the residuals is a function of the realized residual values and nothing else, so the half-length inherits the sampling variability of those values in full. High variability in interval length is the problem that the conformal prediction methods leave unsolved. A prediction interval is useful only when its length can be trusted, since the length is what the predicted range contributes in place of the unmeasured outcome. An unstable length is a problem for two reasons. First, the width of the interval tells a reader how precisely the unmeasured outcome is known. A highly variable width reports a precision that depends heavily on the sample, which 2can change the conclusions drawn from downstream analyses. Second, because the coverage rate is determined by the length, a short length and a long length each fail in a different way. A short length produces a coverage rate below the prediction level, so the interval covers the unmeasured outcome less often than promised. A long length produces a range too wide to be informative, so the interval says little about where the outcome lies. Reliable use of a prediction interval therefore depends on an interval length that is stable across samples. We develop the semiparametric prediction method for the right-censored covariate setting. The setting requires the outcome model for the outcome given the time-to-event covariate and the fully observed covariates, the time-to-event model, and the censoring model; the time-to-event model and the censoring model are nuisance models, needed to handle the right-censoring rather than to describe the outcome directly. The three models determine the distribution of the residuals, and so determine the half-length that achieves the prediction level, but the conformal prediction methods estimate that half-length from the residual values alone. The semiparametric prediction method estimates it from the distributional information the three models carry. Using the distributional information may appear to cost the properties that make conformal prediction attractive, because given the center, the semiparametric prediction method depends on the outcome model and the nuisance models where conformal prediction depends on neither. The semiparametric prediction method gives up neither property. The prediction interval retains a coverage rate that converges to the nominal prediction level at the same order as for the confor-mal prediction methods. The estimator of the target half-length is doubly robust: the estimator remains consistent as long as one of the two nuisance models—the time-to-event model or the cen-soring model—is correctly specified, so a researcher who specifies one of the two nuisance models incorrectly still obtains a consistent estimator. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the right-censored covariate setting and formulates the target half-length of a prediction interval. Section 3 develops conformal prediction methods that estimate the target half-length using empirical residual quantiles. Sec-tion 4 constructs the semiparametric estimator for the same target half-length and derives theoret-ical properties of the estimator and the coverage rate of the resulting prediction interval. Section 5 presents simulation results showing that the semiparametric prediction method yields prediction intervals with more stable length and empirical coverage rate than conformal prediction methods across different choices of centers and censoring rates. Section 6 applies the semiparametric predic-tion method to the Enroll-HD dataset and shows that the resulting prediction intervals for several clinical outcomes, including three cognitive test scores and one composite test score, achieve stable interval lengths with valid coverage, whereas conformal prediction methods yield either overly wide prediction intervals or prediction intervals with low coverage. Section 7 concludes with a discussion of the methodological and clinical implications of the method.

# 3 Conformal prediction under a right-censored covariate 

Conformal prediction is an established framework for constructing prediction intervals with coverage guarantees [19, 8]. To our knowledge, however, no conformal prediction method has been developed for the right-censored covariate setting. We therefore adapt three widely used methods—split conformal prediction, full conformal prediction, and jackknife+—to this setting. Once the center 

m(·) is estimated, all three methods estimate the target half-length ζ using empirical residual values and therefore do not require the distributional information in the outcome model, the time-to-event model, the censoring model, or the fully observed covariate model to establish valid coverage. For a prediction interval constructed from the sample, we define its expected coverage rate as the probability that the interval contains the new outcome Y0, where the probability averages over both the observations in the sample and the new observation. The expected coverage rate is a standard criterion for evaluating prediction intervals [5, 16], and we use it to assess the conformal prediction methods below. The first method is split conformal prediction, which is widely used for its computational sim-plicity and its finite-sample coverage guarantee. # 4 Semiparametric prediction under a right-censored covariate 

4.1 Recasting prediction to semiparametric estimation 

The conformal prediction methods in Section 3 estimate the target half-length ζ using empirical residual quantiles. We now consider an alternative approach that uses the distributional informa-tion in the outcome model, the time-to-event model, the censoring model, and the fully observed covariate model to estimate ζ directly. Because the target half-length ζ is the population quantile of r(O, β), it is determined by the observed-data likelihood fY,W, ∆,Z. [7], Lemma 1 states that the outcome model and the models 

fX|Z, fC|Z, and fZ are identifiable, in the sense that each model is uniquely determined by the observed-data likelihood. Therefore, ζ can be uniquely expressed as a function of the outcome model and these models. We treat fX|Z, fC|Z, and fZ as nuisance models. Estimating ζ through the outcome model and the nuisance models transforms the prediction problem into a semiparametric estimation problem. We adopt a semiparametric framework for two reasons. First, because knowing the true nui-sance models is difficult in practice, the framework should accommodate different choices of nui-sance models. Second, the framework should guide the construction of an estimator with the smallest possible variance. Semiparametric theory provides both: the tangent space describes how the observed-data likelihood varies with the nuisance models, and the efficient influence function identifies the estimator that achieves the smallest variance within that framework. A smaller variance in the estimator of the half-length ̂ ζ matters for three reasons. First, when researchers analyze datasets of similar size from the same underlying population, a less variable estimator makes the resulting prediction method more reproducible across studies. Second, em-pirically, smaller variance in the estimated half-length tends to produce smaller variation in the coverage rate, which improves the practical stability of the prediction method. Third, when com-paring prediction methods that achieve the same coverage, a less variable ̂ ζ makes the interval length a more reliable measure of predictive performance. To establish the semiparametric framework, denote η1 ≡ fX|Z, η2 ≡ fC|Z, and η3 ≡ fZ. Then the target half-length ζ is a function of the outcome model parameter β and the nuisance models 

η1, η2, and η3. A semiparametrically efficient estimator ̂ ζ is typically obtained from an estimating equation based on the efficient influence function ϕeff , which is the unique influence function that lies in the tangent space T . The tangent space T is defined as the closure of the linear span of the score functions of all parametric submodels of the outcome model and the nuisance models [17]. Because T spans the scores of the outcome model and the three nuisance models, it decomposes into four corresponding components: one for the outcome model carried by β, and one for each nuisance model η1, η2, and η3. Remark 1. Whether the outcome-model component ˜Λβ belongs in the tangent space at all is what separates our problem from [7], which studied the estimation of β under the same censoring mechanism. When the target is the model parameter, semiparametric theory works with the nuisance tangent space Λ1 ⊕ Λ2 ⊕ Λ3 alone, and ˜Sβ plays the role of the efficient score function for β, obtained by projecting Sβ clear of the nuisance models. In that role, ˜Sβ is the object used to estimate the target, and it stays outside the nuisance tangent space rather than sitting inside it. Estimating a model parameter in this way is the problem studied in [7], who developed Λ1, Λ2,

Λ3, and the projection ˜Sβ for estimating the outcome model parameter β. In our problem, the 8target is instead ζ, which is a function of β, and β is no longer the target. The contribution from 

β must therefore be carried inside the tangent space, as the component ˜Λβ. As a consequence, the efficient influence function must simultaneously account for uncertainty from the nuisance models and from β. We apply the prediction methods to a dataset from a Huntington disease study, where the goal is to predict a clinical outcome that is unmeasured at a patient’s visit. When a required measurement is missing, the outcome the patient would have shown is unknown, and what is needed is a prediction interval for that unmeasured outcome—a range that plausibly contains it, reported with a prediction level. Because a patient’s outcome depends on how far the disease has advanced, the interval must be constructed for the patient’s own position in the disease, measured by the time until Stage 2 of HD-ISS [15]. Existing outcome references in Huntington disease adjust for age, sex, and education [6, 11] but do not condition on the time to Stage 2, and provide no way to handle a time to Stage 2 that is right-censored. Two patients observed for different lengths of time without reaching Stage 2 are at different points in the disease, yet existing references treat them the same. The semiparametric prediction method conditions on the time to Stage 2 and accounts for the right-censoring, and so supplies a patient-specific prediction interval that existing references do not. The prediction interval is useful only when the range can be trusted. The coverage rate must match the nominal prediction level, so that the interval genuinely contains the unmeasured outcome with the stated probability. Also, the interval length must be stable, so that the interval a patient receives reflects the disease process rather than the study sample used to fit the method. The analysis below evaluates whether the prediction methods deliver a range of that kind. Evaluating the coverage rate requires the clinical outcome to be observed, so the analysis uses patients whose clinical outcome is measured and holds out a portion of the outcomes to check how often the outcomes fall inside the constructed prediction intervals. The held-out outcomes serve only to evaluate the coverage rate; the prediction interval itself is what the semiparametric prediction method supplies for a patient whose clinical outcome is not measured. We use the Enroll-HD dataset, an international observational cohort study consisting of 30,511 Huntington disease patients. We take entry into HD-ISS Stage 2, the stage at which clinical signs or symptoms of Huntington disease first appear [15], as the anchoring event for each patient, and we take the clinical outcome to be measured at Stage 0 of HD-ISS, the stage at which a patient has CAG repeat length ≥ 40 . The time from Stage 0 to Stage 2 is the time-to-event covariate, which is right-censored when a patient exits the study before reaching Stage 2. We translate the clinical prediction problem into the notation of the paper as follows. Let X

denote the time from Stage 0 to Stage 2, and let C denote the time from Stage 0 to study exit. For the Stage 0 outcome Y , we consider four variables: three cognitive test scores, Stroop Color (SC), Stroop Word (SW), and Stroop Interference (SI), and one composite score, the composite Unified Huntington Disease Rating Scale (cUHDRS). For the fully observed Stage 0 covariates, we use Z = ( Z1, Z 2, Z 3), where Z1 is the normalized CAG-age-product (CAP) score [21], Z2 is an indicator for female sex, and Z3 is an indicator for International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) level at least 4. The CAP score summarizes disease risk based on CAG repeat length and age, while sex and education are included as baseline demographic and socioeconomic covariates [3, 9]. To implement the semiparametric prediction method with the Enroll-HD dataset, we perform data cleaning. We exclude individuals whose baseline age is below 18 years, and we exclude individuals with X = 0 and with C = 0 to avoid zero-inflated distributions for X | Z and 

C | Z. After the exclusions, 2,809 individuals remain, and the censoring rate is 77.2%. 25 7 Discussion 

We developed the semiparametric prediction method, which constructs a prediction interval for a clinical outcome when a time-to-event covariate is right-censored by recasting the construction of the prediction interval as semiparametric estimation of the target half-length. The recasting is what carries the contribution beyond the Huntington disease study. In the right-censored covariate setting, conformal prediction provides a natural distribution-free starting point: the target half-length is estimated from empirical residual values, so the coverage guarantees do not require using the distributional information in the outcome model, the time-to-event model, the censoring model, or the fully observed covariate model. The semiparametric prediction method takes a different route by expressing the target half-length as a quantity to be estimated from the observed-data likelihood. This brings the prediction problem into the semiparametric framework, so that the efficient influence function, double robustness, and efficiency bounds can be used to study the half-length directly. The recasting also addresses a tension between two properties that may appear to be in opposition. A half-length of conformal prediction asks for no distributional information but can be variable across samples, while a model-based half-length uses distributional information but may appear to forfeit the robustness of the distribution-free construction. The semiparametric prediction method shows that these properties can be combined: using the distributional information yields an efficient estimator of the half-length when the nuisance models are correctly specified, while the estimated half-length remains doubly robust and the coverage rate remains valid under the stated conditions. The recasting also connects inference on the half-length to the choice of nuisance models. Be-cause the estimator of the target half-length is asymptotically normal, a researcher can attach a confidence interval to the estimated half-length and test differences in half-length across nuisance model specifications. This turns the choice among nuisance model specifications, which conformal prediction methods leave unexamined, into a comparison that accounts for estimation uncertainty. In the Huntington disease study, the semiparametric prediction method produces a prediction interval for a clinical outcome that conditions on time to Stage 2 and accounts for the right-censoring of that time, which existing outcome references do not. The interval gives the range of outcomes a patient may plausibly have at their time to Stage 2, which supports downstream risk assessment. Its usefulness rests on the two properties the semiparametric prediction method establishes: a coverage rate that matches the nominal prediction level, so that the interval contains the patient’s true outcome as often as the level promises, and a stable length, so that the interval a patient receives does not depend on the particular sample used to fit the method. A prediction interval that conditions on disease stage and handles its right-censoring is usable beyond Huntington disease, wherever a clinical outcome is described as a function of a time-to-event covariate that is right-censored for many patients. The semiparametric prediction method estimates a single target half-length, which yields a pre-diction interval whose length is constant across covariate values rather than one whose length varies with the covariates. Unlike this marginal prediction interval, a prediction interval whose length varies with clinically relevant characteristics, such as ∆ or components of Z, could be tailored to patient subgroups. The interval would treat the target half-length as a function of the conditioning variables while reusing the influence-function estimation and coverage results developed here. Prediction in the right-censored covariate setting had offered only half-lengths estimated from residual values, which secure a valid coverage but leave the interval length unstable. Recasting the prediction interval as a semiparametric estimation problem changes what is possible in the setting: the half-length becomes a quantity that can be estimated efficiently, made robust to misspecification, and equipped with inference, all while the coverage rate stays valid. The same recasting that delivers these guarantees for the marginal interval length is what a covariate-varying 26 interval length and other estimands in the right-censored covariate setting can now be built on.
