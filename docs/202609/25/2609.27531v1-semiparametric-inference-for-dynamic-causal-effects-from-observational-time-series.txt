# Semiparametric Inference for Dynamic Causal Effects from Observational Time Series 

## Shibo Yu, Yan Chen, Jin-Hong Du, Guodong Li ∗

## Department of Statistics and Actuarial Science, The University of Hong Kong September 24, 2026 

Abstract 

In observational time series, statistical inference for dynamic causal effects of a one-time intervention across horizons is complicated by high-dimensional observed pre-treatment information, unmeasured confounding, and serial dependence. To address these challenges, we develop a semiparametric framework for inference from a single serially dependent time series, integrating debiased machine learning with instrumental variables through buffered block cross-fitting. Under geometric beta-mixing, we derive non-asymptotic bounds on estimation error, asymptotic normality at each fixed hori-zon, and feasible inference that accommodates serial dependence. We further show how learner-specific prediction guarantees under temporal dependence can be used to verify the nuisance-rate conditions required for orthogonal inference. In a monetary-policy application with 468 months and 1464 lagged FRED-MD controls, we show an instru-mented policy tightening lowers housing starts at medium horizons, with sensitivity analyses that support the finding. 

Keywords : Dynamic causal effects; instrumental variable; local projection; high-dimensional adjustment; debiased/double machine learning; time series.

1arXiv:2609.27531v1 [stat.ME] 23 Sep 2026 1 Introduction 

Many scientists and policymakers ask how an outcome trajectory would have changed if, at a specific time, a treatment or policy variable had been shifted by an external intervention while the pre-treatment history was held fixed (Adamek et al., 2024). For example, a central bank cares about how inflation and real activity would respond over the following months to a change in its policy rate (Romer and Romer, 2004; Stock and Watson, 2018), a fiscal authority considers how a tax change propagates through the economy (Mertens and Ravn, 2013), and a security analyst investigates whether airstrikes at one date raise or lower insurgent violence in the weeks that follow (Papadogeorgou et al., 2022). Similar examples can be found in time series experiments (Bojinov and Shephard, 2019) and adaptive treatment of a single patient (van der Laan and Malenica, 2018). In observational time series data, such questions are complicated, as such counterfactual shifts are not directly observed. Treatment assignment often responds to the evolving state, so its ordinary association with future outcomes combines the causal effect of interest with endogenous selection. Instrumental-variable (IV) methods address this problem by isolating treatment variation induced by an external source and are therefore widely used across fields of causal inference (Wright, 1928; Stock and Watson, 2018; Khalaf et al., 2026). However, credible use of such variation may require adjustment for a rich pretreatment information set, both to support the identifying assumptions and to sharpen estimation by accounting for predictable variation in treatment and outcome (Tan, 2006; Chernozhukov et al., 2015). This often results in high dimensional state covariates recording such information, thwarting the inference of the causal target. Our empirical application of FRED-MD data offers a motivation. We ask how U.S. in-flation, unemployment, industrial production, and housing starts respond to a change in the monetary-policy stance. The policy rate is set in response to hundreds of macroeconomic indicators and to private forecasts that never enter the public record (Romer and Romer, 2004; Bernanke et al., 2005), so there can always be confoundedness caused by latent condi-2tions, no matter how many covariates are chosen. Luckily, the Romer–Romer shock (Romer and Romer, 2004; Wieland, 2021) serves as a widely-used instrument for policy shocks. How-ever, to adjust for known confounders and purify causal effects, a high-dimensional state for adjustment is also needed, which, in our application, is twelve lags of the FRED-MD panel containing 1464 variables (McCracken and Ng, 2016). Given the sample size being only 468 months, a sophisticated analysis framework is hence necessitated. Formally speaking, the causal target is the horizon-specific response matrix {Bh : h ∈ H} ,whose entries record the expected change in the outcome vector Yt+h that an externally induced unit shift in the period-t treatment Wt would produce when the pre-treatment history is held fixed and the system evolves naturally afterward. This can be viewed as a semiparametric local-projection IV (LP–IV) target in the sense of Jordà (2005) and Stock and Watson (2018). Here, dynamic refers to the propagation of one intervention across horizons, as in the impulse-response tradition, and not to a sustained treatment regime. We will show that under certain conditions, Bh coincides with the normalized structural impulse response of proxy-SVAR analysis (Mertens and Ravn, 2013; Montiel Olea et al., 2021). Estimating this target involves three difficulties at once, namely an endogenous treatment identified only through an external instrument, an adjustment state whose dimension may exceed the length of the series, and serial dependence along the single trajectory. Related methods address these difficulties under different identifying and modeling assumptions. LP– IV and proxy-SVAR methods accommodate an instrument but adjust for a low-dimensional prespecified control set, and their inference theory is built around that fixed-dimensional ad-justment (Stock and Watson, 2018; Montiel Olea and Plagborg-Møller, 2021; Montiel Olea et al., 2021; Plagborg-Møller and Wolf, 2021). High-dimensional time series methods, from sparse vector autoregressions and Lasso-based local projections to factor-augmented regres-sions, permit many predictors (Basu and Michailidis, 2015; Kock and Callot, 2015; Cher-nozhukov et al., 2021; Krampe et al., 2023; Adamek et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2023), but focus generally on prediction or on structural impulse responses under prespecified identification 3restrictions (Adamek et al., 2024; Krampe et al., 2023), and their guarantees are tied to a particular learner. A common approach to identifying effects of time-varying treatments as-sumes sequential ignorability, meaning that the observed history suffices to control confound-ing at each treatment decision (Robins, 1986; Murphy, 2003; Lewis and Syrgkanis, 2021). That assumption does not fit our settings. Debiased machine learning (DML) (Chernozhukov et al., 2018) separates a low-dimensional causal parameter from an arbitrary nuisance learner through a Neyman-orthogonal score and cross-fitting, and its IV form handles endogenous treatments. In the independent-data setting, sample splitting makes the fitted nuisance in-dependent of the held-out data. We will show that adopting DML to a single dependent trajectory is nontrivial, because the training and validation blocks remain dependent, and the independent-data guarantees do not apply directly. Recent work brings orthogonal esti-mation to dependent data. Semenova et al. (2023) leave out temporal neighbors in dynamic panels with many units. Ciganovic et al. (2026), which propose reverse cross-fitting for par-tially linear regressions, require unconfoundedness and very strong assumptions on temporal dependence. Ballinari and Wehrli (2025) construct orthogonal impulse-response estimators for binary treatments under conditional unconfoundedness, whereas Gonçalves et al. (2026) consider finite additive shifts of an i.i.d. structural shock. To our knowledge, no existing re-sult covers an endogenous treatment instrumented by an external variable, a learner-agnostic adjustment for a high-dimensional state, and a single temporally-dependent trajectory, to-gether with a theory that converts a generic prediction guarantee into the conditions the orthogonal estimator needs in a block cross-fitting scheme. In light of these, we make the following contributions. First, we formulate the total response to a single counterfactual intervention, derive its semiparametric local-projection representation, and identify it through residualized IV moments; see Section 2. Then, we develop an orthogonal estimator with buffered block cross-fitting for a single time series with high-dimensional adjustment in Section 3. Under geometric β-mixing and regularity con-ditions, we derive a non-asymptotic estimation-error bound and, under additional stability 4conditions, joint asymptotic normality and feasible HAC inference at each fixed horizon; see Section 4. Furthermore, we establish a transfer principle that converts prediction guar-antees of a temporal learner into nuisance-error rates under buffered block cross-fitting in Section 5. This makes a framework for time-series prediction theory to be usable in orthogo-nal inference across different choices of nuisance learners, and can be applied to other similar settings. We exemplify how to verify these conditions for a factor model and sparse spline in the supplement, so that concrete results can be readily obtained. In the FRED-MD application in Section 7, we find a decline in housing starts at medium horizons after an instrumented tightening, which persists under multiple ways of residualiza-tion. To check that the procedure is reliable, we also run a plasmode experiment to establish our method’s robustness. Simulations in Section 6 also evaluate our method.

# 2 Model setup and causal target 

Let {(Yt, Wt, Zt, Ut)}Tt=1 be the observed time series, where Yt ∈ Rp is the outcome vector, 

Wt ∈ Rs is the observed treatment random vector, Zt ∈ Rr is an instrumental variable, and 

Ut ∈ RqT is an observed pre-treatment adjustment state. Let H ⊂ { 0, 1, . . . , H } denote the finite set of horizons of interest. The dimension qT may grow with T , while p, s, r, and H

are treated as fixed or small. We ask how the expected outcome Yt+h changes when only 

Wt is externally shifted, holding the pre-treatment history fixed and allowing subsequent treatments and covariates to respond through their natural mechanisms. The state Ut may include lagged outcomes, lagged treatments, lagged instruments, and 5a large panel of pre-treatment covariates in applications. However, it should not contain post-treatment variables or mediators, as they may block the causal path from treatment to outcome. The instrument Zt is a source of exogenous variation in the treatment, which may be a policy shock or a randomized encouragement, which will be detailed in Section 2.2. 

## 2.1 Structural equation for the causal target 

The causal target is counterfactual by nature. Let W ⊆ Rs denote the set of all possible deter-ministic intervention or treatment values. For a fixed intervention w ∈ W , let Yt+h(w) ∈ Rp

denote the potential outcome at horizon h that would be observed if the period-t treatment were externally set to w. We introduce Ct to denote the full pre-assignment state informa-tion, represented as a sigma-field. It contains the observed pre-treatment history and may also include latent macroeconomic fundamentals that affect both the endogenous part of the treatment and future outcomes. The observed state Ut is then a coarsening of this full information set, in the sense that σ(Ut) ⊊ Ct, serving as observed confounders. On the other hand, the treatment random variable Wt is often unmeasurable with respect to Ct, since it may contain exogenous variation, including that induced by the instrument Zt.To formally derive the causal target, we impose a semiparametric linear response model for each h ∈ H conditional on the full pre-assignment information. For any fixed intervention 

w ∈ W , we assume the structural equation 

E[Yt+h(w) | C t] = Bhw + mh(Ct), (2.1) where Bh ∈ Rp×s is the horizon-specific causal effect matrix and mh(Ct) ∈ Rp is an unknown function of the full pre-treatment state. Equivalently, for any increment δ ∈ Rs such that 

w, w + δ ∈ W , (2.1) implies the constant conditional causal contrast 

E[Yt+h(w + δ) − Yt+h(w) | C t] = Bhδ. (2.2) Thus Bh is defined as the causal response parameter. The horizon h records how the effect of this intervention evolves over time. 6To bridge the counterfactual target to the observed outcomes, we first decompose the potential outcome for each fixed w into its conditional mean (2.1) and a residual as 

Yt+h(w) = Bhw + mh(Ct) + ηt+h(w), (2.3) where, by construction, E[ηt+h(w) | C t] = 0. To connect the counterfactual response to ob-served outcomes, we impose conditional mean exchangeability given Ct, so that E[Yt+h(w) |Ct, Wt] = E[Yt+h(w) | C t] for every w ∈ W , which is weaker than conditional independence of the potential outcome and treatment (Imbens, 2004). Under (2.3), it is equivalent to 

E[ηt+h(w) | C t, Wt] = 0 for every w ∈ W . (2.4) It then follows that E[ηt+h(Wt) | C t, Wt] = 0 as the treatment random variable Wt is also supported on W. By the tower property, E[ηt+h(Wt) | C t] = 0.For the observed outcomes, the consistency condition dictates Yt+h = Yt+h(Wt). Eval-uating (2.3) at the observed treatment Wt yields the full-state structural equation 

Yt+h = BhWt + mh(Ct) + ηt+h, E[ηt+h | C t] = 0,

where ηt+h := ηt+h(Wt) is the future structural shock evaluated at Wt.However, the complete information set Ct is never observed in full. Therefore, we project the unobserved baseline mh(Ct) onto the available state Ut by defining the observed-state nuisance function gh,t (Ut) = E[mh(Ct) | Ut] as a surrogate to be estimated. Substituting this into the full-state equation yields the observed-data local projection (LP) representation 

Yt+h = BhWt + gh,t (Ut) + εt+h, E[εt+h | Ut] = 0, (2.5) where εt+h = mh(Ct) − gh,t (Ut) + ηt+h is the composite error term, whose zero conditional mean follows by the tower property. The difference mh(Ct) − gh,t (Ut) is the unobserved confounding effect from the latent state, and the term ηt+h is the pure structural future shock. However, because the observed treatment Wt may depend on latent components of 

Ct, e.g., private information not captured in Ut, we generally have E[εt+h | Wt, Ut]̸ = 0.7We therefore introduce an instrumental variable (IV) Zt to overcome this endogeneity and identify Bh, by isolating externally generated variation in Wt; see Section 2.2 below. 

Remark 1. Without the linear response restriction in (2.1) , an IV estimand would gen-erally have a local or weighted interpretation analogous to LATE-type estimands. Such heterogeneous-effect interpretations go beyond the focus of this paper, as the target here is the global, horizon-specific causal response matrix Bh in (2.2) . At the sample level, (2.5) cleanly aligns our causal target with the structural equations commonly seen in the LP literature.

## 2.3 Alternative interpretations of the model 

We relate Bh to structural impulse responses and to causal contrasts used in sequential-treatment analysis. These comparisons depend on how the intervention is imposed and on how subsequent treatments are determined. 

Structural impulse responses. From the perspective of the structural time series, the dynamics of treatment and outcome is jointly generated from a set of unobserved structural shocks that propagate through the system, and an intervention is imagined as exogenous shift in some parts of a shock ζt. The form (2.11) also encompasses the stable finite-order SVAR by the Wold representation theorem. In this language, Ut plays the role of the information available before the period-t

shock, such as the lagged state in a VAR. The nuisance gh,t aims to forecast the part of the outcome after accounting for the predictable component of Wt. The instrument Zt is called a proxy for the target shock in Wt in the sense that it is only non-orthogonal to target shocks. After removing the components predictable from Ut, taking moments with the residualized proxy isolates the target-shock contributions to Wt and Yt+h. The resulting normalized structural impulse response equals Bh under the proxy-SVMA assumptions of Example A.1 (Stock and Watson, 2018; Mertens and Ravn, 2013; Montiel Olea et al., 2021). This gives Bh the economic interpretation of being a response to structural shocks normalized 10 by their treatment impact. We can therefore estimate these structural responses through horizon-specific IV moments without estimating a transition model for the full system. 

Sequential-treatment effects. In sequential-treatment analysis, causal contrasts de-pend on the continuation regime (Robins, 1986, 1994; Murphy, 2003). For example, a struc-tural nested mean model (SNM) can describe the effect of changing the current treatment while fixing subsequent treatments at reference values. In contrast, our parameter Bh in-cludes the effects transmitted through changes in later treatments induced by the initial intervention. This generally differs from the effect obtained by holding later treatments fixed. We impose (2.1) directly on the total response and identify it through the period-t in-strument, without specifying effects under other continuation regimes. Our framework thus permits IV inference on this total response from a single dependent trajectory, even when flexible adjustment for the high-dimensional Ut cannot remove treatment confounding. 

# 3 Orthogonal estimation via block cross-fitting 

This section proposes an estimation pipeline for Bh via debiased machine learning (DML) route, in which flexible learners first fit the conditional means and the causal target is then estimated from a single moment equation, and then states the requirements for the pipeline. Before proceeding, note that identification in Proposition 1 placed no restriction on how the conditional laws evolve across time, since the moment equation (2.9) holds for each t

even when the state distribution drifts. However, estimation cannot proceed at that level of generality. A single realized trajectory supplies only one draw of (Yt+h, Wt, Zt) in each period, whereas the conditional means μY,h , μW , and μZ can be recovered only by pooling observations over t so that the sample size can increase. # 5 Nuisance estimation and rate transfer 

All inferential properties above depend on satisfaction of the two conditions on the population and block-sample behaviours of the nuisance errors. # 7 Empirical application 

We estimate how U.S. macroeconomic outcomes respond to an instrumented change in mon-etary policy, which responds to a broad information set and to latent economic conditions, 28 so this application calls for both high-dimensional adjustment and an external instrument. We present main results here, with additional details and diagnostics in Section G. 

## 7.1 Data and estimation 

We combine the April 2026 vintage of FRED-MD (McCracken and Ng, 2016) with the up-dated Romer–Romer monetary-policy shocks (Romer and Romer, 2004; Wieland, 2021). The instrument Zt = resid_full t covers 468 months from January 1969 through December 2007. # 8 Conclusion and discussion 

We develop semiparametric inference for horizon-specific causal effects of an endogenous treatment observed along a single time series via instrumental variables. The proposed procedure residualizes the outcome, treatment, and instrument against a high-dimensional pre-treatment state, combines block cross-fitting with a temporal buffer, and estimates the causal response through a Neyman-orthogonal IV moment. We develop non-asymptotic es-timation error bound for the estimand and feasible HAC inference for fixed-horizon causal matrices and contrasts. The buffered rate-transfer argument shows when existing prediction guarantees for a generic temporal learner can imply the nuisance conditions an orthogo-nal estimator generally requires. In the monetary-policy application, the clearest empirical pattern is a decline in housing starts at medium horizons. The present analysis also leaves limitations for future work. First, the identification and inference theories require the average first stage to be well conditioned (or over-identified), so weak-IV-robust tests would be a future direction. Second, the current Gaussian approx-imation is horizon-wise. Simultaneous inference over a growing collection of horizons that accounts for cross-horizon dependence and HAC studentization would be an important topic. Third, estimation pools observations under time-invariant conditional mean functions. Al-lowing the nuisance functions and score distribution to drift smoothly, or to change across 31 structural breaks, would broaden the method to more nonstationary environments.

## G.2 Sensitivity analyses and calibration 

G.2.1 Nuisance specifications and response sensitivity 

We compare the baseline Lasso learner with ridge regression and regression on eight principal components of the 1,464-dimensional state. All three learners use the same 468 observations, 109 five contiguous folds, temporal buffer, and HAC lag. We standardize controls within each training fold.
