A B S T R A C T 

Ship-to-home fulfillment requires a retailer to select a fulfillment location and carrier before the delivery time is known. These decisions often rely on static transit-time estimates, which cannot represent how delivery-time uncertainty varies across orders and fulfillment options. This paper constructs conformal predictive distributions (CPDs) using Conformal Predictive Systems (CPS) for regression and the cross Venn–Abers predictor (CVAP) for multiclass classification, and uses them in a contextual optimization model for fulfillment decisions. The model is solved by contextual sample-average approximation (C-SAA). The regret of each fulfillment decision, its expected cost above that of the best decision, is bounded by forecast error, scenario-evaluation error, and the candidate-generation gap. The forecast term depends on how well calibration residuals or class probabilities represent the current order. On more than five million proprietary order records, CVAP raises exact-day accuracy from 49.5% for the rule-based estimate to 64.1%. Paired with a decision rule that selects a quantile weighting late errors more heavily, the split CPS (SCPS) gives a point estimate that raises late-delivery detection from 3.8% to 79.4%. Residual-based CPS distributions also meet or slightly exceed nominal interval-coverage targets. In decision experiments with a JD.com simulator over six test dates, C-SAA with CVAP lowers mean total cost by 7.1% and C-SAA with the Mondrian CPS (MCPS) lowers it by 6.7%, relative to C-SAA with the original nonconformal forecast. Both cut the late-delivery rate from 5.7% to below 4.0%. 

## 1. Introduction 

Ship-to-home fulfillment requires a retailer to choose a fulfillment option, a pairing of a fulfillment location with a carrier service, before the delivery outcome is known. The fulfillment decision determines shipping expense, opportunities to consolidate items, and the likelihood that the order arrives early or late. Many systems make this choice using static transit tables that specify service times, pickup cutoffs, and valid operating days. These tables are easy to maintain, but they do not adapt to the order, current fulfillment operations, or conditions in the carrier network. Machine-learning methods are increasingly used to predict delivery times, but accurate prediction remains difficult because the outcome depends on every stage the order passes through: processing and shipping at the fulfillment location, entry into the carrier network, line-haul transport, and the last-mile leg to the customer (Salari et al., 2022; Merchán et al., 2024; Ruan et al., 2022). Figure 1 traces these stages from order placement through delivery. For the fulfillment decision, the form of the forecast matters. A point forecast supplies one estimated delivery date but not the likelihood or extent of an early or late arrival. The difference is important when the two types of deviation carry asymmetric costs and when fulfillment options with similar expected delivery dates have different levels of uncertainty. A predictive distribution supplies the scenarios needed by contextual stochastic optimization and can also be summarized as a point forecast, quantile, or interval (Vovk et al., 2020). The operational question is how the representation of delivery-time uncertainty affects fulfillment decisions. Two related studies motivate this question. Ye et al. (2025a) formulate contextual stochastic optimization for omnichannel multicourier fulfillment and use estimated delivery-time distributions to account for item consolidation and delivery uncertainty. They estimate these distributions with a multiclass classifier for each carrier, calibrated by isotonic regression, and with tree-based quantile regression, neither of which carries a finite-sample validity guarantee.

Ye et al. (2025b) develop conformal predictive distributions (CPDs) for fulfillment-time forecasting and evaluate their distributional and point accuracy on proprietary order records. The present study uses those forecasting methods to construct inputs for the stochastic fulfillment model, bounds the cost of the resulting fulfillment decisions, and evaluates the policies they produce in the simulator of Ye et al. (2026). The forecasting methods of Ye et al. (2025b) construct CPDs in two ways. Conformal Predictive Systems (CPS) convert regression residuals, the differences between observed outcomes and fitted predictions, into a predictive distribution. Split CPS (SCPS) uses all calibration residuals, whereas Mondrian CPS (MCPS) uses residuals from groups with similar predicted values. The cross Venn–Abers predictor (CVAP) constructs a CPD by calibrating probabilities from a multiclass delivery-deviation model. The forecasting comparison also includes two-stage classify-then-regress forecasts and delivery-date estimates chosen to reflect the different consequences of early and late errors. In the decision study, the CPDs supply delivery scenarios to a contextual fulfillment model solved by contextual sample-average approximation (C-SAA). C-SAA evaluates feasible fulfillment decisions by averaging their costs over sampled delivery and future-demand outcomes. Using the same solution procedure for every forecast focuses the downstream comparison on the distribution supplied to the optimizer. The methodological contribution is to represent delivery-time uncertainty with CPDs for each order and fulfillment option, the level at which the optimization model compares fulfillment options. A general result bounds the regret of the implemented decision, the amount by which its expected cost exceeds that of the best decision for the current order. The bound combines the error in the expected delivery cost that the forecast assigns to each fulfillment option, the sampling error in evaluating candidate decisions, and the loss from choosing among a finite set of candidates. Two further results bound the error in expected delivery cost. For CPS, the bound depends on calibration-group size, regression error, and how well calibration residuals match the current order. For class-probability forecasts such as CVAP, it depends on their accuracy at the current order. An example shows that exact marginal calibration does not bound this error, so these results identify the order-level accuracy that the decision depends on. The empirical contribution evaluates these forecasts on more than five million order records and compares the fulfillment policies they produce in a simulator.

## 2. Related Work 

This paper connects three literatures. Delivery-time forecasting supplies the inputs, probabilistic and conformal forecasting determine their form, and contextual optimization turns them into fulfillment decisions. 

2.1. Delivery-Time Forecasting 

Delivery-time forecasting has been studied at the customer-offer, fulfillment, line-haul, and last-mile stages. Wen et al. (2024) provide a recent review. Methods include deep travel-time models (Wu and Wu, 2019; de Araujo and Etemad, 2021; Li et al., 2021; Ruan et al., 2022), tree-based models (Salari et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023), and models tailored to delivery exceptions and service operations (Cao et al., 2023; Yi et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Most of this literature predicts the customer promise date. This paper instead predicts delivery-time deviation when a fulfillment option is considered, before the fulfillment decision is made. The timing of the forecast follows Ye et al. (2025a), but the present study replaces its incumbent distributional forecasts with CPDs and adds carrier-network features.

2.2. Probabilistic and Conformal Forecasting 

Probabilistic forecasting represents delivery-time uncertainty through a predictive distribution. Parametric service-time models specify a distributional family for the outcome (Shang et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2023; Raj et al., 2024). Other approaches, including quantile forests (Meinshausen and Ridgeway, 2006; Guo et al., 2022; Salari et al., 2022; Arora et al., 2023) and neural distributional models (Liu et al., 2023; Faulkner et al., 2025), estimate the distribution more flexibly. The resulting distributions take two forms. Regression-based methods often estimate a set of delivery-time quantiles, such as the median and upper-tail percentiles, whereas multiclass models assign a probability to each possible integer-day deviation. Conformal prediction adds a finite-sample guarantee to such forecasts. Under exchangeability, it provides marginal calibration, a guarantee averaged over calibration and test examples, without assuming a parametric outcome distribution (Vovk et al., 2005; Manokhin, 2022). For regression, CPS ranks possible outcomes against held-out calibration residuals to construct a predictive cumulative distribution function (CDF) with this finite-sample guarantee (Vovk et al., 2020). Venn–Abers was developed for binary classification and returns a pair of probabilities with a finite-sample validity guarantee (Vovk et al., 2015; Manokhin, 2017). Related logistics applications use conformalized quantile regression in a classify-then-regress model (Faulkner et al., 2025) or construct prediction sets for inbound load planning (Bruys et al., 2024). 

2.3. Contextual Optimization 

Contextual optimization uses information available before a decision to model uncertainty and choose an action. Feature-based newsvendor models and general predictive-to-prescriptive methods helped establish this literature (Ban and Rudin, 2019; Bertsimas and Kallus, 2020). Sadana et al. (2025) review its main approaches and applications. The approach closest to the CPS construction is residual-based contextual sample-average approximation (SAA): a regression model supplies a prediction for the current context, and shifted residuals provide scenarios for the stochastic program. Kannan et al. (2025) analyze three such data-driven SAA constructions and establish consistency, convergence rates, and finite-sample guarantees under conditions on the data-generating process, prediction model, and stochastic program. Sun et al. (2023) calibrate held-out residuals of an arbitrary prediction model for robust contextual linear programs. Their coverage guarantees are marginal, and their expected-cost rate assumes that the conditional mean residual varies smoothly with the covariates. CPS resembles residual-based contextual SAA because it also adds calibration residuals to a regression prediction, but the two methods attach different guarantees to this construction. Residual-based SAA uses residuals to approximate the conditional distribution needed by the optimization model, whereas CPS ranks calibration residuals to obtain marginal predictive validity. This validity is marginal because distribution-free guarantees conditional on the covariates require additional structure (Barber et al., 2021). Other studies use conformal methods to guide decisions directly. Under finite decisions and bounded utility, Vovk and Bendtsen (2018) provide an asymptotic decision result for universally consistent predictive systems. Another line of work links conformal prediction to robust optimization by using a calibrated prediction region as the uncertainty set (Johnstone and Cox, 2021; Patel et al., 2024; Yeh et al., 2025), or chooses actions for risk-averse agents from conformal sets (Kiyani et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2026). Those approaches protect a decision against outcomes in a region, with guarantees that hold marginally or conditionally on the chosen action. By contrast, the model here samples full predictive distributions within a stochastic program to compare expected fulfillment costs. A related literature asks what calibration implies for decisions. A forecast that ignores the covariates can be perfectly calibrated (Gneiting et al., 2007), and average notions of calibration need not give accurate estimates of decision loss (Zhao et al., 2021; Sahoo et al., 2021). These studies propose calibration notions tied to classes of decisions, but their guarantees remain averages over the population. By contrast, the results in Section 5.2 bound the error in expected cost at the fulfillment options of the current order. 

## 3. Problem Setting 

When an order arrives, the retailer chooses among fulfillment options before the delivery time is known. Each fulfillment option pairs a fulfillment location with a carrier service and differs in shipping cost, delivery-time uncertainty, and use of inventory. The problem therefore has two linked components: a delivery-time forecast for each fulfillment option and a sequential fulfillment decision that accounts for current operating conditions and later orders.

## 4. Delivery-Time Forecasting Methods 

This section describes how the forecasts estimate the conditional distribution 𝐹 (⋅ ∣ 𝒙 𝑎,𝑡 ) of Section 3. The estimate assigns probabilities across early, on-time, and late outcomes, so it can be used to construct prediction intervals, sample delivery scenarios for contextual optimization, or provide a single estimated date. Regression and multiclass classification give two routes to such a distribution. 

4.1. Conformal Predictive Distributions for Regression and Classification 

Regression and classification models produce different outputs, so their calibration steps differ. CPS turns a regression forecast and its past errors into a predictive distribution. Venn–Abers calibration adjusts the probabilities assigned by MC-CLF to the possible delivery-day classes. Both approaches express the result as an estimated CDF over delivery-time deviations. 

4.1.1. Conformal Predictive System 

The Conformal Predictive System for Regression (CPS) starts with a regression estimate and describes uncertainty around it using errors on held-out calibration orders. Adding those errors to the estimate for a new order gives the delivery times that would follow if calibration-period errors recurred, and the resulting predictive distribution supplies quantiles for point forecasts and prediction intervals. 4.1.2. Venn–Abers Calibration for Multiclass Classification (MC-CLF) 

MC-CLF assigns a probability to each of the 𝐾 deviation classes, and these probabilities determine the predictive CDF. Because the raw probabilities may be systematically too high or too low, calibration compares the model scores with observed class frequencies and adjusts the probabilities while preserving their order. Venn–Abers predictors, originally developed for binary classification, perform this adjustment by combining isotonic regression with conformal calibration (Vovk et al., 2015; Manokhin, 2017). To calibrate the multiclass probabilities, this paper adopts a one-versus-rest strategy, applying a separate Venn–Abers predictor to each class against all remaining classes. The inductive Venn–Abers predictor (IVAP) considers both possible labels for a new instance. It appends the instance to the calibration data once with each label, fits a nondecreasing map from model scores to observed class frequencies each time, and thus obtains a pair of calibrated probabilities (Appendix A). The distance between these two values records how much the calibrated probability depends on the unknown label. Under exchangeability, the probability computed with the true label appended is perfectly calibrated (Vovk et al., 2015). CVAP repeats IVAP over several training and calibration splits, called folds, to reduce dependence on a single split. Because the predictive CDF needs one probability, CVAP merges the fold-specific pairs into a single forecast with a geometric-mean rule. The merged forecast gives up the validity guarantee of IVAP, although merged Venn–Abers forecasts were more accurate than existing calibration methods in the experiments of Vovk et al. (2015). The resulting one-versus-rest probabilities are then normalized to sum to one across the 𝐾 classes.

4.2. 2-Stage Classify-then-Regress Forecast 

Delivery-time deviation has an ordered numerical structure: both its sign and its distance from zero are meaningful. The 2-stage forecast represents this structure by separating delivery status from deviation magnitude. A Venn–Abers-calibrated classifier first estimates the probabilities of early, on-time, and late delivery. The forecast then mixes three component distributions with these probabilities as weights: an early-delivery component retaining the negative outcomes of a CPS distribution, an on-time component concentrated at zero, and a late-delivery component retaining the positive outcomes. Using SCPS or MCPS for the early and late deviation distributions yields the 2-stage SCPS and 2-stage MCPS variants. Here 2-stage refers to the forecasting construction, not to two-stage stochastic optimization. 

4.3. Obtaining Point Predictions from Distributional Forecasts 

A retailer can select a delivery-date estimate by choosing a quantile of the predictive distribution. The median targets its center, whereas a higher quantile shifts the estimated date later and places more emphasis on avoiding underestimation of delivery delays. The quantile can therefore be chosen to reflect the relative consequences of errors on early and late deliveries without refitting the forecasting model. For CPS, the conventional point forecast is the median of the predicted CDF. For MC-CLF, it is the most probable deviation class. A weighted-error rule instead chooses a quantile level by balancing overall prediction error against errors on late and early deliveries. For a candidate quantile level 𝑞 ∈ (0 , 1) , let ̂ 𝒚 𝑞 be its vector of predictions on a tuning sample, let RMSE be root mean squared error over that sample, and let LateRMSE and EarlyRMSE be the same error restricted to observed late and early deliveries. Fulfillment options with the same point forecast can still differ in expected early-and late-delivery penalties. C-SAA therefore samples the forecast distributions rather than point forecasts to construct delivery-cost scenarios and combines these with future-demand scenarios when evaluating feasible decisions. 5.2. Theoretical Properties 

In C-SAA, a forecast matters through the delivery-time scenarios drawn from it, and with enough scenarios its effect on the decision reduces to the expected delivery cost it implies at each fulfillment option. The validity guarantees of Section 4.1 concern the coverage of CPS prediction intervals and the calibration of binary IVAP probabilities. Both hold at the population level, or for MCPS within a Mondrian group, whereas a fulfillment decision depends on the expected costs of an individual order. A forecast that gives each fulfillment option the same delivery-time distribution for every order can satisfy these guarantees exactly, yet its decisions can exceed the optimal expected cost by an amount that grows with the late-delivery penalty (Appendix C.5). The results below therefore bound the regret of the C-SAA decision for an individual order by the error in these expected costs, and then bound that error for CPS and for class-probability forecasts. ## 6. Forecasting Study 

This study evaluates how well each method represents delivery-time uncertainty on proprietary order records. 

6.1. Data and Experimental Setup 

The forecast-level study uses an anonymized e-commerce transactional dataset provided by an industrial partner. It contains more than five million ship-to-home orders collected between July 2023 and November 2024 and is commercially sensitive, so it cannot be released. The analysis focuses on a ground carrier service selected with the industrial partner and retains delivery-time deviations from −10 to 10 days, which limits the influence of data errors and atypical cases. Within this range, the deviation is centered near zero but has substantial early and late tails. Both tails matter operationally, since late delivery incurs service penalties, whereas early arrival can disrupt customer and downstream planning (Salari et al., 2022; Cui et al., 2024). For the proprietary dataset, the forecasting models use order, fulfillment-location, and carrier features. Order features describe the shipment and customer, fulfillment-location features summarize workload, congestion, and processing time, and carrier features describe route geography, transit stages, tracking activity, recent performance, and delay exceptions. Figure 3 summarizes how these inputs enter the models and how the resulting distributions are used. Relative to Ye et al. (2025a), this dataset adds variables such as customer membership tier and timestamps describing fulfillment-location and carrier-network operations. Appendix Table B.1 lists the complete feature set, and Appendix Figure B.1 reports XGBoost feature importance, measured by each feature’s contribution to reducing the tree-training loss. The two most important features, the number of days from order to promised delivery and the shipping-offer method at order time, both describe the delivery promise from which the deviation is measured.

6.2.3. Operational Implications 

The forecasts support different operational uses. Because SCPS and MCPS intervals attain close to their nominal coverage, they can set customer delivery windows or the time windows used in last-mile route planning. The 2-stage forecasts separate the chance of a delay from its likely size, which suits simple triage rules, such as flagging orders whose probability of lateness exceeds a threshold for customer-service follow-up. Systems that accept a single date can use the MCPS median, the most accurate central estimate among the regression-based summaries. When missed delays are costly, they can use a weighted-error quantile instead, for example to send delay notices early or to schedule additional staff for shipments expected to arrive late.

7.1. Data and Experimental Setup 

The simulator processes the same 38,416 peak-hour orders from six test dates in each of 50 replications. Within each replication, all policies share a new set of delivery outcomes. These outcomes are generated separately from the optimization scenarios by a model estimated on the test-period records. The forecasting models supplied to C-SAA are trained on JD.com records that precede the test dates. C-SAA is run with five forecasts: the original nonconformal forecast, SCPS, MCPS, and MC-CLF calibrated by either the inductive (IVAP) or the cross (CVAP) Venn–Abers predictor. The nonconformal forecast is the original delivery-time model of Ye et al. (2026), which supplies conditional delivery-time distributions without a conformal calibration step. SCPS and MCPS wrap a regression model with the same architecture as the nonconformal forecast, retrained on the proper-training period so that the calibration week is held out. MC-CLF is a CatBoost classifier over integer-day durations. Because these forecasts predict delivery duration rather than deviation, C-SAA converts each sampled duration to a deviation from the promised date before applying delivery penalties. Two benchmarks are forecast-free. Greedy assigns available inventory to fulfillment options in order of base shipping cost. Empirical SAA solves the stochastic program with delivery and demand scenarios sampled from historical observations. Appendix E reports forecast fitting, service coverage, cost coefficients, and the remaining simulation details. The primary outcomes are total cost and the percentage of test orders delivered late. Total cost is the realized cost of all test orders over the six dates, including shipping costs, delivery penalties, and lost sales.

Replacing the forecast distribution alone changes the fulfillment decisions and their cost. Relative to C-SAA with the original nonconformal forecast, CVAP lowers the mean total cost by 7.1% and MCPS by 6.7%, while the optimization model and its solution procedure stay the same (Table 3). The improvement holds on each of the six test dates, where CVAP, MCPS, and SCPS reduce the total cost by 4.5% to 10.5% and deliver fewer orders late (Figure 7). Total cost under CVAP, MCPS, and SCPS also stays below that under the nonconformal forecast at every reported percentile (Appendix Table D.1). CVAP, MCPS, and SCPS perform similarly. MCPS and SCPS have overlapping confidence intervals, and CVAP differs from MCPS by less than 0.5%. IVAP, which relies on a single calibration split, trails CVAP by a clear margin, and part of this gap may reflect the larger training set of CVAP, whose folds span the whole pre-test period. All machine-learning forecasts outperform the forecast-free benchmarks, consistent with the value of conditioning delivery-time uncertainty on the current order and fulfillment option. The savings come from fewer late deliveries. Late unit-days, the number of units multiplied by their days of delay, fall from 0.137 per order under the nonconformal forecast to 0.101 under MCPS and 0.100 under CVAP. At the late-delivery penalty of $40 per unit-day, this reduction accounts for 93% of the cost saving under MCPS and CVAP and 83% under SCPS, and shipping and early-delivery costs account for the rest. Lost sales are identical under these four forecasts, at 0.062 units per order. The conformal forecasts therefore change which fulfillment options receive the units, while leaving lost sales unchanged. For a retailer, these results suggest that conformal calibration can be added to an existing delivery-time forecast without changing the optimization model. The choice between the two leading forecasts depends on what else the retailer needs. MCPS suits a retailer that also needs calibrated prediction intervals, for example to quote delivery dates, whereas CVAP suits one that already forecasts delivery-day classes. The cost differences rank the policies under the simulator and do not estimate savings in live operations, a limitation discussed in Section 8. 

## 8. Conclusions 

This paper evaluates conformal predictive distributions of delivery time both as forecasts and as inputs to contextual fulfillment optimization. The forecasting study shows how these distributions represent uncertainty on proprietary order records. The decision study samples delivery scenarios from CPS and CVAP distributions for a rolling C-SAA policy and examines how the distributions affect fulfillment choices in the JD.com simulator. The two studies assess the forecasts in different ways. On the proprietary records, machine-learning forecasts improve delivery-date accuracy and late-delivery detection, while CPS provides well-calibrated prediction intervals.  

> T. Ye et al. Page 15 of 27 Conformal Predictive Distributions for Order Fulfillment

The 2-stage forecasts give narrower intervals and a delivery-status decomposition, with less consistent coverage. In the decision study, CVAP and MCPS give the lowest mean total cost among the machine-learning forecasts. Their advantage over the original nonconformal forecast shows that a conformal delivery-time distribution can improve decisions without any change to the optimization model or its solution procedure. The study has limitations that point to future work. The decision study compares policies in a simulator, so its cost differences show how the policies rank rather than what they would save in practice. Measuring the actual effect requires a live deployment, such as an A/B test against the current policy, which would also show whether the advantage persists as conditions change. The theory also shows that the cost of a fulfillment decision depends on the accuracy of expected delivery costs for the current order, which marginal conformal guarantees do not control. Further work could calibrate forecasts for these expected costs, and extend the regret bounds of Section 5.2 from a single fulfillment decision to the sequence of decisions over the planning horizon, in which each decision changes the inventory available to later orders.

## Appendix E. Computational Reproducibility 

This appendix records the remaining forecast and decision implementation details. 

Forecast implementation. The CPS models use Crepes (Boström, 2022). The CVAP code is adapted from the Venn–Abers Calibration library. The proprietary forecast study uses ten initial MCPS bins. The decision study uses five and reduces the count when a carrier-specific bin would contain fewer than 20 calibration observations. An unseen test bin uses the nearest calibration bin. CVAP calibrates across expanding temporal folds. Calibration labels absent from a fold’s proper-training classes are mapped to the nearest observed delivery-day class. Optuna (Akiba et al., 2019) tunes the base models. 

Decision sample. The shipping, consolidation, and delivery terms follow Ye et al. (2025a). The unmet-quantity penalty and sequential inventory dynamics follow Ye et al. (2026). The decision experiment uses March 26–31, 2018. Carrier service 3 has too little data for a carrier-specific model and therefore uses a model covering multiple carrier services, the same fallback for every forecasting method. The other 17 services use carrier-specific forecasts. The delivery penalties are 𝛾 + = 40 .0 for lateness and 𝛾 − = 0 .2 for earliness. The lost-sales penalty is 𝜌 = 200 per unit, and the consolidation discount is 0.5. Shifted CPS duration forecasts are clipped to [0 , 5] days, and MC-CLF uses the same integer-day duration support. C-SAA rounds each duration draw and subtracts the promised duration before computing delivery penalties. The per-unit shipping cost plus delivery penalty is rounded to three decimal places before multiplication by the assigned quantity. 

Candidate generation. Following the replicated SAA structure of Kleywegt et al. (2002) and the implementation of Ye et al. (2026), each candidate solve uses 𝑁 1 = 50 paired delivery-cost and future-demand scenarios, and the common evaluation sample has 𝑁 2 = 500 scenarios. The implementation targets 𝐿 = 10 candidate first-stage decisions from independent 𝑁 1-scenario samples. It retains candidates for which Gurobi returns an OPTIMAL status within the 30-second time limit, so an order can have fewer than ten retained candidates. Candidate generation and selection therefore use 𝐿𝑁 1 + 𝑁 2 = 1 ,000 sampled scenarios per order. Algorithm 1 summarizes the rolling policy, and Ye et al. (2026) give the complete first- and second-stage formulation of (5), including the recourse allocation constraints. 

Hardware and solver. The decision experiments used a shared compute cluster. The optimization models were solved with Gurobi 13.0.2 through gurobipy 13.0.2, using MIPFocus=3 , Threads=4 , and a 30-second time limit.
