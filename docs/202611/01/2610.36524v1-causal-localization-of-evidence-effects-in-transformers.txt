# Causal Localization of Evidence Effects in Transformers 

## Jipeng Li Pramod Khargonekar Yanning Shen 

Abstract 

Can controlled symbolic inputs reveal attention heads that influence a language model’s decisions from natural language evidence? We introduce controlled evidence aggregation (CEA), which varies the counts and order of two symbols in otherwise fixed prompts and measures changes in a frozen Transformer’s preference between two answer tokens. The preference is measured by their logit difference; the prompts prescribe no correct answer. Balanced utility causal selection (BUC) identifies compact head sets by jointly evaluating how much ablating their outputs removes the count effect and how closely patching mean outputs between count conditions recovers the effect in both directions. We derive conditional bounds linking head attribution to intervention effects and specifying when the attribution shortlist can be recovered from finite samples. In Pythia-410M, ablating sets of 1–12 heads selected separately for four ordering regimes removes 65.5–91.6% of the count effect. The sets retain their influence on count comparisons not used for selection. To test relevance beyond symbolic inputs, we evaluate a natural language task asking which state is supported by more reports. Ablating eight heads selected only on symbolic yes/no prompts lowers mean accuracy by 8.2 percentage points, versus 0.8 for random sets matched by size and layer. Our results demonstrate that controlled symbolic probes can identify compact attention head sets whose causal influence extends to natural language decisions. 

# 1 Introduction 

Understanding how language models use evidence requires identifying internal components that connect input changes to answer changes (Elhage et al., 2021; Bereska and Gavves, 2024; Rai et al., 2024). Natural language reports pose a control problem: changing the balance of support can change wording and invoke different learned associations. Symbolic inputs allow more restricted changes, but the identified components’ relevance to language behavior remains an empirical question. Prior studies demonstrate component reuse across tasks and prompt variants (Merullo et al., 2024; Nainani et al., 2024). We ask: Can controlled symbolic inputs identify attention heads whose causal influence extends to decisions from natural language evidence? 

We introduce controlled evidence aggregation (CEA), which varies the counts and order of B and C in prompts to a frozen Transformer while fixing the template, symbol identities, sequence length, and local answer context. The F RONT 

ordering, for example, compares 

50 B | 150 C | 50 A ←→ 150 B | 50 C | 50 A .

Numbers denote repetition counts; both sequences share a tail of 50 A symbols. Both prompts request either various 

or successful . Their logit difference measures answer preference; its change between count conditions is the count effect . Neither answer is designated correct. CEA therefore supplies a controlled response to localize without training a symbolic rule or assuming pretrained associations are absent. To localize the count effect, balanced utility causal selection (BUC) evaluates compact head sets through two complementary interventions at the final prompt position. Ablation zeros selected outputs and measures how much the count effect decreases. Mean patching replaces them with averages from the other count condition and measures how closely replacement reproduces the count effect in both directions. Removal and recovery are distinct requirements, consistent with prior work on intervention and evaluation choices (Miller et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2024). BUC uses both scores in greedy search, evaluating sets jointly rather than summing individual head effects. Reuse tests keep selected heads fixed; reselection for each target would test a different claim. Our theory separates attribution accuracy from selection uncertainty. Under a bound on attribution mismatch, we bound errors in predicting removal and recovery scores from head attribution. We also derive finite sample conditions for recovering the population attribution shortlist. A separate greedy bound applies under monotonicity and weak submodularity to a weighted sum of unnormalized intervention gains, whereas BUC optimizes normalized removal and recovery scores. 1arXiv:2610.36524v1 [cs.CL] 27 Oct 2026 To test symbolic localization on labeled behavior, we introduce a natural language (NL) task with sixteen reports supporting two mutually exclusive states. Affirmative and negated reports balance total state word frequencies: the item is open and the item is not closed , for example, both support open . The model answers whether one state has more supporting reports than the other; reversing the comparison reverses the correct yes/no answer. At equal head budget, we compare sets selected only on symbolic yes/no prompts, sets selected on NL prompts, and random sets matched by layer. Patching in the expanded study uses NL donor outputs, so transfer concerns head identities rather than unchanged activation vectors. In Pythia-410M, ablating sets of 1–12 heads selected separately for four ordering regimes removes 65.5–91.6% of the count effect. The same sets affect count comparisons not used for selection (Section 5.3). In the original NL evaluation, ablating eight heads selected only on symbolic yes/no prompts lowers mean accuracy by 8.2 percentage points, versus 0.8 for matched random sets (Section 5.5). Further tests assess reuse across orderings, answer tokens, and replacement operators. The results show that controlled symbolic probes can identify compact attention head sets whose causal influence extends beyond selection prompts to predictions in a labeled language task. 

# 2 Related work 

Behavioral targets. Studies of induction, factual recall, indirect-object identification, and task vectors localize compo-nents in pretrained models (Olsson et al., 2022; Geva et al., 2021; Meng et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022; Hendel et al., 2023; Todd et al., 2024). Task-trained symbolic models permit comparison with a designed algorithm (Brinkmann et al., 2024); structured tasks also reveal compositional failures (Dziri et al., 2023). CEA controls count and order without training a symbolic rule, then tests fixed-set effects on labeled evidence. 

Circuit reuse and evaluation. Cross-task reuse (Merullo et al., 2024), prompt-variant generalization (Nainani et al., 2024), ablation dependence (Miller et al., 2024), and tests of preservation, localization, and minimality (Shi et al., 2024) establish distinct aspects of circuit validity. CEA examines which controlled changes preserve the effect of the same head set selected for a count-dependent response, separating this question from successful reselection. 

Selection methods. Activation and path patching test a supplied behavioral target (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2023; Zhang and Nanda, 2024). Automated circuit discovery (ACDC) uses intervention-based search; edge attribution patching (EAP) estimates relevant edges (Conmy et al., 2023; Syed et al., 2024). BUC scores set-level removal and recovery in both directions; equal head budgets do not equate search cost or intervention granularity. Appendix G provides extended comparisons of behavioral targets, selection methods, circuit reuse, and in-context learning interpretations.

## 3.3 BUC selection and evaluation 

BUC ranks all heads by | c∆si(r)|, screens the attribution shortlist with single-head interventions, and runs forward greedy search in the resulting candidate pool Pr . For a weight λ ∈ [0 , 1] , the set objective is 

Qλ(H, r ) = λ shrink( H, r ) + (1 − λ) match avg (H, r ). (3.10) Each candidate is evaluated jointly with the current set, so its value can change as heads are added. The search accounts for measured interactions that a fixed ranking of individual effects omits. The main configuration uses λ = 1 /2 and at most 12 heads. The FP32 rule takes the first prefix with shrink ≥ 0.85 and mean match ≥ 0.70 , or returns the full budget with a failed-threshold flag. Search also records all 12 prefixes for fixed-budget comparisons. Layer-matched controls preserve set size and the number of heads per layer. Selection and evaluation use separate batches, but deterministic regimes repeat inputs. Exceeding controls, passing both thresholds, and predicting NL labels correctly are distinct criteria. ## 5.6 Scope checks 

The L AST -versus-S EQ comparison tests intervention location. Applying the selected sets only at symbolic-sequence positions yields shrink 0.001–0.059 and match −0.006 to 0.006. For the reported scope diagnostic, these heads have much larger effects at the final prompt position. The R ANDOM diagnostic is not documented as the same five-execution cohort as the main table (Appendix B.7). Post-selection split patches distinguish query–key (QK) attention routing from output–value (OV) writing. Represen-tative F RONT , I NTERLEAVE , and R ANDOM heads have OV-only match 0.88/0.92/0.85 versus QK-only 0.35/0.12/0.18. BACK has QK/OV match 0.72/0.61, indicating a more mixed contribution (Appendix B.6). Word-symbolic, signal-token, and short-sentence inputs retain mean shrink/match of 0.812/0.845, 0.820/0.852, and 0.765/0.790. Across Pythia-70M through Pythia-1B, GPT-2, and Qwen3-4B-Base (Radford et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2025), four-regime mean BUC shrink/match ranges from 0.650/0.700 to 0.895/0.925 (Appendix B.19). Positive effects do not establish shared head identity across models. Input-only Greedy Occlusion gives held-out shrink/recovery of 0.795–0.915/0.812–0.898, versus random-subset recovery of 0.248–0.262. Input recovery does not penalize overshoot and is not the head-patching match score. BUC requires activation access.

Scientific and practical scope. Count generalization and limited transfer are compatible. A selected set can influence several count contrasts without being independent of ordering or readout. Comparing layer-matched sets controls head count and layer allocation, so the difference concerns which heads are selected rather than simply how many or how deep. The labeled bridge adds evidence that a fixed symbolic set affects predictions, but does not identify whether the shared computation processes reports or writes the yes/no response. Phrase-based strategies remain possible; general NL reasoning is not established. Interventions identify writes at the final prompt position, not complete head–MLP paths; compactness is neither uniqueness nor global minimality. 

Selection and statistical scope. Head-budget comparisons do not control search cost or isolate greedy search from ranking by the same joint objective in the same pool (Appendix C.4). Bootstrap CIs condition on fixed sets and donors; repeated deterministic inputs do not support prompt-sampling intervals. The BF16 bridge lacks its required bootstrap intervals. Expanded FP32 model summaries have no CIs, use one seed for causal selection, and average TR only over estimable conditions. 

Use and open questions. Further tests should distinguish evidence aggregation from answer-readout effects, examine computations at earlier tokens and along head–MLP paths, and extend to longer heterogeneous reports and decisions involving multiple tokens. Localized components could enable targeted manipulation; the present audits do not certify deployment safety. 

# 7 Conclusion 

CEA tests whether controlled symbolic inputs can identify attention heads relevant to natural language decisions. BUC selects compact head sets by jointly evaluating ablation and bidirectional mean patching. Conditional theory links attribution to intervention effects and specifies assumptions for shortlist recovery and greedy bounds on raw utility. In Pythia-410M, selected heads affect count contrasts not used for selection. Ablating a fixed symbolic yes/no set reduces labeled NL accuracy more than random controls matched by size and layer. Audits characterize how reuse depends on ordering, readout, and replacement operators. ## B.2 Data splits, selection pipeline, and control construction

Normalized objective versus raw theory. The executable selection objective is Qλ in Equation 3.10. The raw signed utility Jλ in Equation 4.1 is a different set function. Theorem 4.5, as stated for Jλ, is not automatically an approximation guarantee for Qλ. The same greedy argument would require monotonicity and an appropriate submodularity-ratio condition for Qλ itself. The finite audit below tests the empirical objective, including negative marginal gains and violations of its required conditions. Neither first threshold crossing nor an empirical greedy curve proves a unique or globally smallest causal set. Numerical candidate refinement is specified in Appendix F.3. 

## E.3 Statistical units and uncertainty 

The basic sampling unit for intervention uncertainty is a unique matched high/low input pair, not an execution row. Symbolic pairs are identified by unpadded token hashes, while NL pairs use paired evidence identifiers; reversed query orientations remain in the same cluster. We use 1,000 cluster-bootstrap replicates and report two-sided 95% percentile intervals, with endpoints given by the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. Within each replicate, the same resampled cluster indices are used for the clean, ablated, HL-patched, and LH-patched arrays. The intervals are conditional on the selected head set and fixed donor bank; head selection is not rerun inside the bootstrap. For normalized intervention metrics, only bootstrap replicates satisfying | b∆| ≥ 0.01 are retained. These intervals are therefore conditional on a non-weak resampled contrast. Raw contrasts and raw utilities retain their resampled values without this conditioning. Baseline differences use matched bootstrap indices for the two methods. At a fixed symbolic count, FRONT, BACK, and INTERLEAVE each contain only one unique symbolic sequence. Repeated execution of that same sequence is not treated as independent prompt sampling. Consequently, a prompt-sampling bootstrap interval is not reported for a condition containing only one unique matched pair. RANDOM conditions use distinct matched permutations as sampling clusters. Execution-seed variation and variation across stochastic operator draws are summarized separately from prompt-sampling uncertainty. For stochastic operators, the 12 intervention repetitions are first averaged at the readout level for the 35 primary score; the across-repetition standard deviation and standard error of the separately computed scores are Monte Carlo summaries and are not bootstrap confidence intervals. Repeated deterministic executions likewise do not increase the number of independent prompt units. Cross-regime or cross-condition point summaries are arithmetic summaries of the corresponding condition-level point estimates. We do not assign them a cross-regime confidence interval by treating regimes as independent samples. Any aggregate interval reported for an experiment with genuine sampling units is obtained by recomputing the same aggregate within every bootstrap replicate before taking the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. Comparisons in the paper do not claim simultaneous confidence coverage. The condition-level NL procedures and the point-only model aggregation are specified separately in Appendix E.10; Table 6 has no model-level CI. Counts and observed condition spans are descriptive. Table 7 reports finite operator/T3 summaries without population confidence intervals. Appendix H specifies which statistics receive intervals.

Here b∆B is the empirical clean contrast on target task B, and QB (HB ) − QB (RB ) is the target-selected set’s score in excess of its own layer-matched random control. Otherwise the point ratio is unavailable, not clipped or set to zero. Ratios outside [0 , 1] are possible; a ratio is not a fraction of a uniquely identified circuit. 

Condition-level transfer intervals. For each estimable comparison, the interval procedure uses 1,000 replicates. Matched target clusters are resampled, and the draws within each of the two random-control families are resampled independently. The same target indices are used for the source, target-selected, and arrays from random sets. Replicates must pass the same gates for the raw contrast and excess target score. A condition-level interval is suppressed if more than 5% of replicates fail either gate; otherwise the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the stable replicates define the interval. These intervals condition on fixed selected sets and donor banks, not on repeated causal selection. A finite point ratio remains in the output when its interval is suppressed. 

Transfer coverage and aggregation. For each model, forward symbolic-to-NL transfer has 4 × 3 = 12 regime–bank target conditions. Let nest be the number of finite ratios satisfying both gates for estimating a point ratio, and let nsuppr be the number of these estimable conditions whose interval is suppressed. The model-level TR in Table 6 is the unweighted arithmetic mean of all nest finite condition-level ratios, including those with suppressed intervals. It is not the ratio of separately averaged numerators and denominators. Conditions without an estimable point ratio are excluded from the TR mean, not assigned zero. In Pythia-410M, Pythia-1B, and GPT-2 order, nest = 11 , 12 , 9, and nsuppr = 1 , 0, 3. Thus 12 − nest , the number of unavailable point ratios, is 1, 0, and 3, respectively. The number of retained condition-level intervals, nest − nsuppr , is 10, 12, and 6, respectively. These counts do not provide a model-level interval or the number of conditions with positive transfer. The labeled NL analysis records clean and ablated accuracy and their paired difference. CSV differences are fractions and are multiplied by 100 for reporting in percentage points. The expanded causal module evaluates the main gap only, so it does not estimate the ablated full-count slope reduction reported for the BF16 bridge. Fixed-set Jaccard overlap is reported separately from behavioral and intervention transfer.

## F.1 Native model path and reference computation 

The implementation is divided into the experiment definitions in anc/code/cea_native_suite.py , the prefix-cache engine in anc/code/cea_fast_v9.py , and numerical routing in anc/code/cea_numeric_v9.py .Source checksums in the accompanying protocol identify the implementation. Models use the native Hugging Face GPT-NeoX/Pythia or GPT-2 implementation, in evaluation mode without gradients. The output embedding is obtained through get_output_embeddings() , and model-specific projection layouts are handled explicitly. FP32 inference is retained without autocast, TF32, lower-bit weight conversion, or model fine-tuning. Attention uses the native eager path. The reference clean readout for a cached prompt is obtained from a full native forward pass. Storage can use −1 padding to hold unequal-length inputs, but the model never receives those padding values: inputs are grouped by unpadded length, and outputs are restored to the original row order. Native reference calls use unpadded singleton inputs where required for the established reference path. The numerical protocol distinguishes exact mathematical identities from finite floating-point comparisons. Replace-ment of head channels before a linear output projection implements the specified write intervention, but reordering batch computations or reusing attention caches can change floating-point results. Native and cached intervention outputs are therefore compared numerically. 

## F.2 Causal-prefix caching and independent intervention branches 

All supplemental white-box interventions act at the final prompt position. Causal attention prevents changes at that position from modifying earlier positions. For a prompt of length T , the engine selects a suffix length s from {1, 8, 32 , 64 },limited to the available context length. A full native prefill stores the key/value tensors for positions before the suffix. For each head-set evaluation, the engine reuses only the unmodified prefix and recomputes the suffix, including the final token, every affected attention operation, all downstream layers, layer normalizations, and MLP computations. The intervention is still applied only at the final position. Earlier suffix tokens are recomputed without head replacement. The prefix tensors are read-only. Each intervention branch receives an independent cache object, and the selected head mask and donor row are aligned with the original prompt/branch indices. Multiple head sets for the same prompt can be evaluated in parallel; each distinct prompt has its own prefix and donors. There is no addition of isolated single-head scores to approximate a set effect. This optimization does not apply unchanged to an intervention that alters prefix tokens or earlier positions, such as the original S EQ scope experiment. ## F.3 Validation stages and native candidate refinement 

Software checks. Before the pretrained-model experiments, the notebook runs synthetic causal-attention regressions, model-interface checks, and a full small-budget software workflow. These exercise both GPT-NeoX and GPT-2 layouts, cache isolation, interventions, migration, and resume behavior. Synthetic tests check software behavior and are distinct from pretrained-model measurements. 

Pretrained replay checks. Each pretrained checkpoint is compared with its full native forward path on symbolic and NL probe prompts, original and yes/no readouts, and nonempty head interventions. Candidate suffix lengths and branch sizes are tested before the main computation. Raw readout differences, full-logit maximum errors, and reversed branch-order errors use the thresholds in Table 47. The initial global diagnostic also records normalized score errors for sufficiently large probe contrasts ( | b∆| ≥ 0.05 ). The global diagnostic is followed by checks for each condition on the actual paired data. Every record of raw values includes the compared readout IDs, baseline, intervention values, denominator, and errors. 

Common native baseline. Both the replay and reference score calculations use the same native clean ¯yH and ¯yL. Only intervention outputs differ in the comparison. Scores computed with a clean baseline computed with replay are separate diagnostics; reported effect estimates use the native clean baseline. Small raw differences can be amplified when | b∆| is small; passing a full-vocabulary logit check alone is therefore insufficient for normalized shrink and match. 

Condition-specific paired-data checks. Each numerical condition is keyed by model, target pair, donor pair, replace-ment identity, weight, and denominator floor. The validation routine selects up to three evenly spaced nonempty candidate sets from the requested list and compares native and replay per-example readouts, raw contrasts and gains, shrink, both directional matches, their average, and Qλ. The compared arrays and errors are stored. The maximum observed raw readout error is denoted ey and the maximum score error eQ. Conditions with ey > 0.002 route their set-score evaluation to native inference. Non-selection conditions with eQ > 0.002 also use native set scores. The same tolerances apply to small-contrast conditions. 

Selection boundaries and winners. Large candidate lists can use replay to screen, followed by native refinement. Let 

Da be the denominator in Equation E.5. The empirical readout envelope is ηy = max {10 −7, 2ey }, and the initial score radius for an unrefined candidate is 2ηy /D a. Native checks of candidates near the top-k boundary or the winner update that radius to at least twice any observed replay/native Qλ discrepancy. Candidate intervals that overlap the membership or winner boundary trigger more native measurements. Every selected greedy candidate is re-evaluated with the native forward pass. The single-head screening boundary uses k = 48 ; greedy augmentation uses k = 1 . Native comparisons resolve exact ties by the same coordinate rule. This refinement rule uses a finite empirical error envelope, not a proved uniform bound over unmeasured candidates. The error envelope does not establish a uniform ranking-equivalence guarantee beyond the tested conditions. The number of natively rechecked candidates, actual errors, final radius, and selected winner are logged. ## G.1 Behavioral targets and controlled evidence 

Studies of induction, factual recall, and indirect-object identification connect model outputs to particular internal components (Olsson et al., 2022; Geva et al., 2021; Meng et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022). In those settings, the target behavior supplies a task-specific criterion for an explanation. Task-trained symbolic models offer a different starting point: the training task specifies an algorithmic problem, allowing a discovered mechanism to be compared with the computation required to solve it (Brinkmann et al., 2024). Behavioral studies of structured tasks also examine failures of compositional generalization (Dziri et al., 2023). CEA does not train a model to map symbols to answers. Its symbolic contrast changes the count or order of fixed tokens and measures the resulting output response. Count sweeps test extension beyond the selection endpoints; fixed-count order comparisons test ordering dependence. Keeping a head set fixed distinguishes persistence of its effect from the ability to select another set. The symbolic target has no external correctness label, so the labeled NL task provides a separate test of behavioral relevance. Lexical balance and query reversal constrain simple shortcuts but do not eliminate all phrase-based strategies or pretrained associations. 

## G.2 Interventions and component selection 

Activation patching replaces internal activations to measure their effect on a supplied output metric; path patching restricts the routes through which a replacement can influence that metric (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2023; Zhang and Nanda, 2024). ACDC searches a computational graph using intervention tests, while EAP ranks candidate edges using attribution estimates (Conmy et al., 2023; Syed et al., 2024). Their targets, graph units, and search budgets need not match a head-set comparison. BUC uses head writes at the final prompt position as its intervention unit and evaluates removal plus recovery in both patching directions on the same set. Its scores include set interactions because interventions are evaluated jointly. The BF16 ActPatch baseline instead ranks single-head high-to-low recovery within a sensitivity shortlist before jointly evaluating the selected union. These comparisons change the ranking objective as well as the search procedure. They therefore demonstrate a measured removal–recovery trade-off, not the independent benefit of greedy search over ranking single heads with the same objective or an end-to-end cost advantage (Appendix C.4). No quantitative result in the paper is attributed to a separate reproduction of EAP or ACDC. 

## G.3 Component reuse across tasks and prompts 

Merullo et al. (2024) examine shared components in indirect-object identification and the Colored Objects task. Their intervention analysis provides evidence of common computational steps across these tasks. Nainani et al. (2024) test 48 the indirect-object-identification circuit under prompt variants and find reuse with additional input edges. These studies establish that circuit reuse can occur; neither reuse nor failure should be presumed for another experimental setting. More recently, de Varda et al. (2026) study whether circuit overlap and loading predict arithmetic accuracy across numeric and verbal formats. Their predictive question differs from CEA’s direct measurement of a fixed source set’s intervention effects on specified target conditions. CEA makes no claim to predict correctness on arbitrary new tasks. Our comparisons distinguish changes in count, ordering, wording, output coordinates, and replacement operators. In the labeled bridge, the fixed symbolic set and an NL-selected set are evaluated on the same NL targets at equal head budget. Source selection does not use NL prompts; the expanded FP32 transfer uses target-specific donor calibration. Positive effects support partial reuse under that intervention protocol. Because both tasks use the yes/no readout, the comparison does not isolate shared evidence aggregation from shared answer processing. Likewise, the seven targeted readout pairs and the larger registered scan are different samples, not a paired estimate of a reselection advantage. 

## G.4 Faithfulness, localization, and statistical tests 

Miller et al. (2024) show that circuit faithfulness scores can change substantially with ablation methodology. Our operator audit measures that dependence for fixed CEA head sets under removal and two patch directions. It tests sensitivity of the reported effect to the replacement distribution rather than assuming a unique operator-independent circuit. Shi et al. (2024) develop statistical tests for behavior preservation, localization, and minimality. Our selection-matched null asks a different question: whether the observed utility is unusual under the specified within-pair label-exchangeability null when the adaptive selection pipeline is repeated. The trial design and eligibility rules are in Appendix E.9. Rejection of that null neither establishes minimality or uniqueness nor assigns semantic correctness to the symbolic response. Causal effects of small sets, hypothesis rejection, and cross-setting reuse remain separate claims. 

## G.5 Representations and in-context learning interpretations 

Analyses of information routing and residual representations provide context for interpreting writes at the final prompt position (Geva et al., 2023; Dar et al., 2023; Ferrando and Voita, 2024). Task-vector and function-vector work studies internal representations of demonstrated functions and their effects in other contexts (Hendel et al., 2023; Todd et al., 2024). CEA retains the identity of selected heads while allowing their activations to depend on the target inputs and donor distribution. A fixed head set is therefore not a fixed activation vector or a complete description of the earlier computation producing its writes. Bayesian and gradient-based accounts of in-context learning relate model behavior to learning rules under specified tasks and assumptions (Xie et al., 2021; Garg et al., 2022; Akyürek et al., 2022; Von Oswald et al., 2023; Panwar et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2023; Ahn et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023). The exact two-token log-odds identity in CEA supplies a scalar measurement; it does not identify a Bayesian update or optimization algorithm. Our theory instead states conditions for attribution fidelity, shortlist recovery, and greedy utility bounds. The finite empirical audits do not establish all those conditions for the implemented normalized objective. 

Remaining mechanism questions. The evidence leaves open whether NL transfer reflects shared count/order process-ing, shared answer readout, or both; which computations at earlier tokens and along head–MLP paths supply the writes at the final prompt position; and whether the same components remain effective across pretraining checkpoints or longer heterogeneous evidence.
