Accurate MRI-free PET Quantification and Reliability Assessment via CT-to-MRI
Synthesis and Conformal Prediction
Jungwoo Seoa, Seung Yun Choia, Kun Ho Leeb, Seun Jeonc,∗, Byoung Seok Yec,∗, Jiook Chaa,∗
aSeoul National University, Seoul, Republic of Korea
bChosun University, Gwangju, Republic of Korea
cYonsei University, Seoul, Republic of Korea
Abstract
Standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) quantification of amyloid PET commonly relies on a subject-specific T1-weighted MRI, yet
a usable MRI is not always available. We therefore combined CT-to-MRI synthesis with conformal prediction for MRI-free PET
quantification with reliability assessment. A slice-consistent Brownian Bridge Diffusion Model was strengthened with multi-dataset
training and data-source conditioning to enrich the anatomical prior learned from MRI, and with expanded multi-slice CT context to
exploit the through-plane continuity of subject-specific anatomy in CT. Synthetic MRI was used for FreeSurfer-based anatomical
parcellation and PET quantification, while deep-ensemble uncertainty was calibrated with conformal prediction to estimate a
plausible range for the corresponding real MRI-based SUVR. On 11C-PiB PET from OASIS-3, the proposed method achieved the
highest synthesis and parcellation fidelity among the compared methods. Mean cortical SUVR from the proposed synthetic MRI
agreed closely with the real MRI-based reference (MAE 0.016 ± 0.015, Pearson r = 0.9987) and yielded the same amyloid-positivity
classification as the real MRI in all 87 test subjects at the prespecified cut-off. Conformal prediction achieved empirical coverage
close to the prespecified levels, and at 90% target coverage, 85 of 87 subjects had prediction intervals that supported an unambiguous
amyloid-positivity decision. Zero-shot evaluation on an independent external cohort showed that the proposed CT-to-MRI model
could support PET quantification without retraining, although conformal calibration required target-cohort recalibration to recover
the intended coverage. These findings support CT-to-MRI-based MRI-free PET quantification as a promising approach for providing
accurate SUVR measurements and an explicit assessment of their reliability.
Keywords: amyloid PET, MRI-free PET quantification, CT-to-MRI synthesis, diffusion models, conformal prediction, uncertainty
quantification
1. Introduction
Amyloid-β deposition is one of the earliest detectable patho-
logical changes in Alzheimer’s disease and may begin years
before measurable cognitive decline [1, 2]. Positron emission
tomography (PET) enables this molecular pathology to be vi-
sualized and quantified in vivo [3]. As Alzheimer’s disease
is increasingly defined and staged according to its underlying
biological pathology, biomarker-based assessment has become
increasingly important [2]. Quantitative amyloid PET can char-
acterize overall pathological burden, regional deposition patterns
relevant to disease staging [4], and longitudinal changes in amy-
loid accumulation [5].
Amyloid PET is commonly quantified using the standard-
ized uptake value ratio (SUVR), which normalizes tracer uptake
in cortical target regions to uptake in a relatively amyloid-sparse
reference region. Accurate SUVR estimation requires the target
and reference regions to be defined according to each subject’s

anatomy. Because PET has limited spatial resolution and weak
anatomical contrast, conventional quantification pipelines typi-
cally rely on a subject-specific T1-weighted MRI for anatomical
parcellation [6, 7].
A usable MRI, however, is not always available because of
implanted devices, claustrophobia, patient motion, acquisition
burden, or additional cost [8]. This has motivated MRI-free
PET quantification methods based on PET spatial normalization
[9, 10, 11], low-dose attenuation CT [12, 13, 14], or CT-to-
MRI synthesis [15]. PET-only methods provide limited subject-
specific anatomy, whereas CT offers insufficient soft-tissue con-
trast for fine cortical parcellation. CT-to-MRI synthesis is there-
fore particularly promising: it can complement the anatomical
evidence available in CT with structural knowledge learned from
MRI while retaining established MRI-based segmentation and
quantification pipelines.
Despite this promise, CT-to-MRI synthesis remains intrinsi-
cally ill-posed because CT provides substantially less informa-
tion than T1-weighted MRI about gray–white matter boundaries
and cortical folding. Multiple anatomically plausible MR images
may therefore be consistent with the same CT observation, mak-
ing faithful recovery of subject-specific fine anatomy inherently
challenging. More generally, medical image translation mod-

els can produce visually plausible outputs without necessarily
preserving all subject-specific anatomical features [16]. This am-
biguity is particularly important for MRI-free PET quantification
because anatomical errors in the synthesized MRI can propagate
through brain parcellation and ultimately affect regional SUVR
estimates.
Conceptually, CT-to-MRI synthesis can be viewed as esti-
mating the conditional distribution of possible MR images given
the observed CT:
p(MRI | CT) ∝p(CT | MRI)p(MRI).
Here, p(MRI) represents anatomical prior knowledge learned
from MRI data, whereas p(CT | MRI) reflects compatibility
with the subject-specific anatomical information available in
CT. Faithful synthesis therefore requires both a sufficiently rich
anatomical prior and sufficient subject-specific evidence from
the input CT to identify the anatomy most consistent with the
individual subject.
Previous work demonstrated that CT-derived synthetic MRI
can be incorporated into an MRI-based PET quantification pipeline,
establishing the feasibility of CT-to-MRI-based MRI-free PET
quantification [15]. Separately, slice-consistent Brownian Bridge
diffusion improved CT-to-MRI synthesis by jointly modeling ad-
jacent slices and promoting through-plane consistency [17, 18].
Nevertheless, the anatomical prior learned from limited training
data and the subject-specific information extracted from a nar-
row CT input may still be insufficient for recovering complex
individual cortical anatomy.
In this study, we strengthen both sources of information. We
use multi-dataset CT–MRI training to learn from a broader dis-
tribution of brain anatomy and introduce data-source condition-
ing so that this broader prior can be learned without conflating
dataset-specific image characteristics. We also provide a wider
multi-slice CT context to make greater use of subject-specific
anatomical information available across adjacent slices. We hy-
pothesize that a richer anatomical prior together with stronger
subject-specific CT constraints will produce more faithful syn-
thetic MRI and, in turn, improve anatomical parcellation and
MRI-free PET quantification.
Existing MRI-free PET quantification methods, including
CT-to-MRI-based quantification [15], are typically evaluated
by their overall agreement with the real MRI-based reference
but do not provide, for each subject, a plausible range for the
SUVR that would have been obtained with that subject’s real
MRI. High overall agreement, however, does not indicate how
reliable an MRI-free SUVR estimate is for an individual subject.
In the intended MRI-free setting, the real MRI is unavailable,
so the discrepancy between an MRI-free SUVR estimate and
the corresponding real MRI-based value cannot be directly mea-
sured. This is particularly important near an amyloid-positivity
decision cut-off, where even a small quantification error may
affect the resulting classification.
To address this limitation, we combine deep-ensemble uncer-
tainty with split conformal prediction [19, 20, 21, 22]. Model-
derived uncertainty has been used to characterize output reli-
ability in medical image segmentation [23] and neuroimage
enhancement [24], while conformal prediction has been applied
to assess the reliability of diagnostic predictions in AI-assisted
pathology [25]. In our setting, these ideas are used to assess
the reliability of CT-to-MRI-based PET quantification by esti-
mating a plausible range for the corresponding real MRI-based
SUVR. Comparing this range with the amyloid-positivity deci-
sion cut-off allows cases to be identified as confidently negative,
confidently positive, or indeterminate, providing a way to assess
decision reliability when the real MRI is unavailable.
For clinical applicability, the proposed approach should re-
main reliable beyond the datasets used for model development.
We therefore evaluated zero-shot generalization on an indepen-
dent external cohort and examined whether the conformal cali-
bration could also be transferred to this new setting.
Accordingly, this study makes four main contributions:
1. Improved CT-to-MRI synthesis. We strengthen slice-
consistent CT-to-MRI synthesis by enriching the anatom-
ical prior learned from MRI through multi-dataset train-
ing with data-source conditioning, and by exploiting the
through-plane continuity of subject-specific anatomy in
CT through an expanded multi-slice CT context.
2. More accurate MRI-free PET quantification. We show
that the improved anatomical inference yields more faith-
ful anatomical parcellation and closer agreement with real
MRI-based composite and regional SUVR quantification
than PET-only and CT-based baselines and existing CT-
to-MRI-based approaches.
3. Reliability assessment using conformal prediction. We
construct calibrated prediction intervals for composite and
regional SUVR estimates and use these intervals to iden-
tify cases in which the reliability of an MRI-free amyloid-
positivity decision is uncertain.
4. Zero-shot external validation. We evaluate CT-to-MRI
synthesis, PET quantification, and conformal reliability
assessment on an independent external cohort never used
for training, demonstrating the feasibility of using the
synthesis model for PET quantification without retraining
and showing that conformal calibration requires target-
cohort recalibration.
The complete approach is evaluated using 11C-PiB amyloid
PET from OASIS-3 [26] and, zero-shot, using 18F-florbetaben
PET from the independent Gwangju Alzheimer’s & Related
Dementias (GARD) cohort [27], with PET quantification based
on each subject’s real MRI serving as the reference in both
cohorts.
2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Overview
The proposed pipeline replaces the unavailable T1-weighted
MRI with a synthesized MRI from a brain CT and then applies
the same PET quantification procedure used in the conventional
MRI-based pipeline (Fig. 1). The framework consists of four
stages. First, a CT-to-MRI synthesis model generates a subject-
specific T1-weighted MRI from the input CT. Second, the syn-
thetic MRI is processed with FreeSurfer recon-all [28] to

obtain subject-specific anatomical parcellations. Third, amyloid
PET uptake is quantified as standardized uptake value ratios
(SUVRs) using subject-specific anatomical regions and the cere-

the MRI-free SUVR estimate is quantified using a deep ensemble
[19] and calibrated with conformal prediction [22] to construct a
prediction interval for the corresponding real MRI-based SUVR.
The interval is then used to distinguish confident from indetermi-
nate amyloid-positivity decisions. The downstream parcellation
and PET quantification procedures are identical to those used
with the real MRI, with only the anatomical image replaced.
Building on the slice-consistent BBDM framework [17, 18],
we introduce multi-dataset training, data-source conditioning,
and expanded multi-slice CT context to better capture anatomical
variation and subject-specific information from CT. The resulting
CT-to-MRI-based PET quantification pipeline is evaluated zero-
shot on a fully independent external cohort without retraining
or adaptation of the synthesis model. We additionally examine
the transferability of conformal calibration to the external cohort
and the effect of target-cohort recalibration on external coverage.
2.2. Datasets
To develop a CT-to-MRI generative model that captures
diverse anatomical and acquisition characteristics and to evaluate
its generalization to a cohort independent of the datasets used
for model training, we used two publicly available datasets, one
in-house clinical cohort, and one independent external cohort.
OASIS-3 [26] served as the primary dataset for CT-to-MRI
model development and evaluation of the CT-to-MRI-based
MRI-free PET quantification pipeline. SynthRAD2023 [29]
provided additional paired CT–MRI data for synthesis training.
The internal Severance Hospital cohort was used for CT-to-
MRI synthesis training and evaluation, providing an additional
clinically acquired CT–MRI dataset with potential applicability
to downstream PET quantification. The Gwangju Alzheimer’s &
Related Dementias (GARD) cohort [27] was reserved as a fully
independent external cohort to evaluate zero-shot generalization
to an unseen cohort without retraining or fine-tuning.
OASIS-3. OASIS-3 is a publicly available longitudinal multi-
modal neuroimaging dataset spanning normal aging and Alzheimer’s
disease [26]. We used low-dose CT acquired as part of PET/CT
examinations together with T1-weighted MRI for CT-to-MRI
model development and evaluation.
CT–MRI pairs were constructed by matching each CT ses-
sion to the temporally closest T1-weighted MRI from the same
subject within 365 days, yielding 1,441 candidate pairs from 879
subjects. Subjects were assigned at the subject level to a training
set, a validation set for model selection, a calibration set for con-
formal prediction, and a test set for final evaluation. All available
CT–MRI pairs from training subjects were retained, whereas
one pair per subject, selected by the smallest CT–MRI acquisi-
tion interval, was used for the validation, calibration, and test
sets. This resulted in 1,052 CT–MRI pairs before quality con-
trol. After excluding 22 pairs because of data or preprocessing
abnormalities, including registration failure, corrupted volumes,
or incomplete brain coverage, 1,030 usable pairs remained.
For PET quantification, we used 11C-PiB PET and selected
subjects whose PiB PET and paired T1-weighted MRI were
acquired on the same day. This yielded 93 subjects for conformal
calibration and 87 test subjects for the primary MRI-free PET
quantification analysis.
SynthRAD2023. SynthRAD2023 is a publicly available multi-
institutional dataset developed for synthetic CT generation in
radiotherapy and provides paired CT and MRI data from multi-
ple contributing centers [29]. We used the brain subset of Task 1,
which provides paired MRI–CT data for MRI-to-CT synthesis,

Severance Hospital cohort. The Severance Hospital cohort is an
in-house clinical dataset comprising paired CT and T1-weighted
MRI acquired at Severance Hospital, Seoul, Republic of Korea.
CT and MRI examinations were paired when acquired within

200 mA; gantry rotation time, 0.5 s; total collimation width, 10

and a three-dimensional T1-weighted turbo-field echo sequence
with the following parameters: axial acquisition matrix, 224 ×
224; reconstructed matrix, 256 × 256 with 170 slices; voxel size,
0.859 × 0.859 × 1 mm³; field of view, 220 mm; echo time, 4.6

GARD cohort. The Gwangju Alzheimer’s & Related Demen-
tias (GARD) cohort is a longitudinal, community-based South
Korean cohort established for multimodal investigation of aging
and Alzheimer’s disease [27]. GARD served as a fully indepen-
dent external evaluation cohort and was not used for CT-to-MRI
model training, validation, or model selection, or for the primary
conformal calibration performed using OASIS-3.
For the present study, we identified subjects with 18F-florbetaben
amyloid PET/CT and T1-weighted MRI acquired within 90
days. The CT was obtained from the same PET/CT examination
as the amyloid PET, and the temporally closest T1-weighted
MRI was paired with each PET/CT examination. For subjects
with multiple eligible examinations, the pair with the smallest
PET/CT–MRI acquisition interval was retained, yielding one
CT–MRI–PET set per subject and a final external cohort of 84
subjects.
PET/CT examinations were acquired at two centers using
GE Discovery ST and Discovery STE systems. CT acquisition
characteristics differed between the two sites, with predomi-
nant slice thicknesses of 3.75 and 2.5 mm and in-plane pixel
spacings of approximately 0.98 and 0.49 mm, respectively. All
T1-weighted MRI scans were acquired on a 3-T Siemens Skyra
system at Chosun University Hospital. The trained CT-to-MRI
models were applied to GARD without retraining or adaptation.
Data splits. All data splits were defined at the subject level to
prevent information leakage. OASIS-3 was divided into training
(773 pairs from 620 subjects), validation (20 subjects), calibra-
tion (120 subjects), and test (117 subjects) sets, with multiple
longitudinal pairs from the same subject retained only in the
training set. The Severance Hospital cohort was divided into
training (180), validation (10), and test (22) sets, and all 177
SynthRAD2023 pairs were used for training.
For CT-to-MRI synthesis, the model was trained on the
resulting 1,130 CT–MRI pairs, model selection used the 30
validation pairs, and synthesis performance was evaluated on
the OASIS-3 and Severance test sets.
For PET quantification and reliability assessment, the OASIS-
3 calibration and test sets were both restricted to subjects whose
PiB PET and paired T1-weighted MRI were acquired on the
same day. This yielded 93 subjects for conformal calibration
and 87 subjects for the primary MRI-free PET quantification
analysis. The independent GARD cohort was used only for ex-
ternal evaluation and was not involved in model training, model
selection, or the primary conformal calibration.
2.3. Image preprocessing
For model development and retrospective evaluation, paired
CT and T1-weighted MRI volumes were processed in a com-
mon space before CT-to-MRI synthesis. Both modalities were
first resampled to 1-mm isotropic resolution. To reduce un-
necessary spatial mismatch between the paired CT and MRI
volumes and to enable voxel-wise evaluation against the real
MRI, both modalities were then rigidly aligned to a common
MNI152 space. Specifically, the CT was first rigidly registered
to the corresponding T1-weighted MRI using elastix [30]. A
brain mask was then extracted from the T1-weighted MRI us-
ing SynthStrip [31] and applied to both the T1-weighted MRI
and the registered CT. The T1-weighted MRI was subsequently
rigidly aligned to MNI152 space, and the same transformation
was applied to the paired CT. In a practical MRI-free setting
where a T1-weighted MRI is unavailable, the CT can instead
be rigidly aligned directly to MNI152 space, with a brain mask
derived from the CT, before model inference.
Following Kim et al. [15], CT intensities were clipped to the
range of [−100, +200] HU, whereas T1-weighted MRI intensi-
ties were clipped to the 0.01th–99.99th percentile range. Each
modality was then min–max normalized. N4 bias-field correc-
tion was not applied in this study, following the preprocessing
conventions of [29] and Kim et al. [15].
2.4. CT-to-MRI synthesis
We developed the CT-to-MRI synthesis model based on the
slice-consistent Brownian Bridge Diffusion Model (BBDM) of
Choo et al. [17], which builds on the Brownian Bridge diffusion
framework of Li et al. [18]. BBDM formulates paired image-
to-image translation as a diffusion process between the source
and target modalities. The original framework jointly generates
three consecutive T1-weighted MRI slices from the correspond-
ing three axial CT slices and uses MRI histogram-based style
conditioning together with ISTA-style sampling across overlap-
ping slice groups. We retained these components as the base
synthesis framework.
Building on this framework, we evaluated four design strate-
gies intended to compensate for the limited anatomical informa-
tion available in CT.

Multi-dataset training (H1) jointly used paired CT–MRI
data from OASIS-3, SynthRAD2023, and the Severance Hospi-
tal cohort. Training on a larger and more diverse set of subjects
was expected to expose the model to a broader range of brain
anatomy and acquisition characteristics, thereby strengthening
the anatomical prior learned for CT-to-MRI synthesis.
Data-source conditioning (H2) was introduced to account
for heterogeneity across the training datasets. Although multi-
dataset training broadens the available anatomical information,
systematic differences in scanner characteristics, acquisition pro-
tocols, and image appearance across datasets may also interfere
with learning a consistent CT-to-MRI mapping. We therefore
provided the source dataset of each training sample as an addi-
tional conditioning variable alongside the MRI histogram condi-
tion at each denoising step, allowing the model to account for
dataset-specific variation during training.
Expanded multi-slice anatomical context (H3) increased
the through-plane CT context available for each prediction. Be-
cause gray–white matter boundaries and complex cortical fold-
ing are only weakly represented within an individual CT slice,
we hypothesized that incorporating adjacent slices would pro-
vide complementary subject-specific anatomical information
through the spatial continuity of brain structures across slices.
Accordingly, within a practical computational budget, we eval-
uated progressively wider input contexts ranging from 3 to 15
consecutive axial CT slices. We selected 15 slices for the final
model based on validation performance (Section Appendix A.6).
Segmentation-teacher feedback (H4) was explored as an
auxiliary anatomical regularization strategy. We hypothesized
that explicitly encouraging agreement in gray–white matter and
other anatomical boundaries could promote anatomically faith-
ful synthesis beyond image-level appearance alone. A frozen
pretrained segmentation model was therefore used to provide
an additional anatomical training signal. The teacher was used
only during training and introduced no additional requirement
at inference. Details of the segmentation-teacher model, aux-
iliary loss formulation, and weight selection are provided in
Supplementary Section Appendix A.4.
Final model (Fig. 2) combined multi-dataset training (H1),
data-source conditioning (H2), and the 15-slice CT context (H3),
based on the validation analyses (Supplementary Sections Ap-
pendix A.5 and Appendix A.6). Segmentation-teacher feedback
(H4) was excluded because it did not provide a significant addi-
tional improvement.
Training and inference. During training, MRI histogram condi-
tioning was obtained from the paired target T1-weighted MRI,
and the source dataset label was additionally provided for data-
source conditioning. The final model used 15 consecutive ax-
ial CT slices to generate the corresponding central three T1-
weighted MRI slices.

At inference, where the target MRI is unavailable, the mean
MRI histogram estimated from the training data was used as the
style condition. No subject-specific MRI image or histogram
was used as a model input at inference. The source dataset label
corresponding to the input CT was also provided to the model.
For GARD, whose CT acquisition characteristics were most
similar to those of OASIS-3 among the training datasets, the
OASIS-3 source label was used for zero-shot inference. Full
three-dimensional synthetic MRI volumes were reconstructed
using the slice-consistent ISTA-style sampling procedure of
Choo et al. [17]. The same inference procedure was applied
to all test cohorts, including GARD, without retraining or fine-
tuning. Detailed optimization and implementation settings are
provided in the Supplementary Material (Appendix A.1).
Baseline models. We compared the proposed model with four
CT-to-MRI synthesis baselines spanning representative image-
to-image translation approaches: a least-squares GAN (LS-
GAN; 32), an adaptive latent diffusion model (ALDM; 33),
the CT-to-MRI synthesis method developed for MRI-free PET
quantification by Kim et al. [15], and the slice-consistent BBDM
of Choo et al. [17].
Each baseline was trained and evaluated separately on each
dataset, following the conventional single-dataset setting used
in the corresponding prior methods. Thus, models evaluated
on OASIS-3 were trained using only the OASIS-3 training set,
whereas those evaluated on Severance were trained using only
the Severance training set. To evaluate the effect of multi-dataset
training (H1), the proposed model was instead trained jointly
on OASIS-3, SynthRAD2023, and Severance. The contribution
of H1, together with the subsequent effects of H2 and H3, was
examined in the cumulative ablation analysis on the validation
sets (Section Appendix A.5).
Model-specific training settings followed the corresponding
original publications where applicable, with any modifications
or additional tuning reported in the Supplementary Material
(Section Appendix A.2).
Evaluation of synthesis fidelity. Image-level synthesis fidelity
was evaluated using peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), which
reflects voxel-wise intensity fidelity, and three-dimensional struc-
tural similarity (SSIM3D), computed between the synthetic and
real T1-weighted MRI volumes within the brain mask.
To assess preservation of anatomically meaningful struc-
tures, each synthetic MRI and its paired real MRI were pro-
cessed independently with FreeSurfer recon-all. The result-
ing anatomical parcellations were compared using the Dice sim-
ilarity coefficient (DSC), which measures regional overlap, and
the 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95), which measures
boundary agreement. Higher PSNR, SSIM3D, and DSC and
lower HD95 indicate better agreement with the real MRI.
For PSNR and SSIM3D, paired subject-level comparisons
were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. One-sided
tests were used for prespecified comparisons evaluating improve-
ment of the proposed model or successive model components,
with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.
For the parcellation metrics, the proposed method was com-
pared with each baseline for DSC and HD95 in each of the six
anatomical regions using paired two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank
tests. Holm correction was applied across the 48 region–metric–
baseline comparisons, with statistical significance defined as an
adjusted p < 0.05.
2.5. PET quantification
Real MRI-based PET quantification was used as the refer-
ence against which the MRI-free PET quantification pipelines
were evaluated. Each real or synthetic T1-weighted MRI was
processed independently with FreeSurfer recon-all [28] to
obtain subject-specific anatomical parcellations. Amyloid PET
uptake was then quantified using these parcellations, with the
cerebellar cortex used as the reference region [7]. When a syn-
thetic MRI was used, the downstream FreeSurfer and PET quan-
tification procedures were kept identical to those of the real
MRI-based pipeline, with only the anatomical image replaced.
For OASIS-3, 11C-PiB PET frames acquired 40–60 min
after injection were averaged to obtain a static PET volume. For
each real- or synthetic-MRI-based pipeline, the PET volume
was rigidly registered to the corresponding T1-weighted image,
and the resulting anatomical parcellation was mapped to the
native PET space using the inverse transformation and nearest-
neighbor interpolation. Regional SUVRs were calculated by
normalizing the mean tracer uptake in each target region to the
volume-weighted mean uptake in the bilateral cerebellar cortex
[7].
Following Su et al. [7], the mean cortical SUVR (MCSUVR)
was calculated as the arithmetic mean of SUVRs in the pre-
cuneus, prefrontal cortex, gyrus rectus, and lateral temporal
regions. Following Kim et al. [15], regional agreement was addi-
tionally evaluated in six cortical regions: frontal, parietal, lateral
temporal, precuneus, anterior cingulate, and posterior cingulate.
In addition to the CT-to-MRI synthesis methods, we eval-
uated two families of non-synthesis MRI-free PET quantifi-
cation baselines, with two implementations per family: PET-
only template-based normalization and CT-based anatomical
normalization. The PET-only implementations used population-
template-based spatial normalization [9, 10]. Each PET image
was nonlinearly normalized to MNI152 space using either a PiB
PET template constructed from the publicly available GAAIN
Centiloid calibration datasets [34] or a PiB PET template con-
structed from OASIS-3 subjects outside the evaluation sets.
These implementations are referred to as PET-template (GAAIN-
derived) and PET-template (OASIS-derived), respectively. The
CT-based implementations were adapted from Presotto et al.
[12] and Matsuda et al. [13] and differed in CT preprocessing
and segmentation settings. In both implementations, PET was
rigidly co-registered to the same-day CT, the CT was normalized
to MNI152 space using SPM unified segmentation [35], and the
resulting deformation was applied to PET. Target regions were
defined using Desikan–Killiany-based atlases in MNI152 space
[36]. All four implementations used the same static PET image
as the real MRI-based pipeline. Template and atlas construc-
tion, reference-region definitions, and implementation-specific
settings are described in Section Appendix A.3.

2.6. Reliability assessment with conformal prediction
Conformal prediction. Even if a CT-to-MRI-based MRI-free
PET quantification pipeline achieves good overall agreement
with quantification based on the real MRI, this does not ensure
that the quantification error is small for every individual subject.
In a practical MRI-free setting, the real MRI is unavailable, so
the discrepancy between an MRI-free SUVR estimate and the
corresponding real MRI-based SUVR cannot be known for a
given subject. We therefore aimed to estimate, for each subject, a
range of plausible SUVR values within which the corresponding
real MRI-based SUVR would be expected to fall at a prespecified
coverage level.
Because brain CT only partially captures the anatomical
information needed for PET quantification, the difficulty of in-
ferring the missing anatomical information can vary across sub-
jects. For more difficult cases, independently trained models
may converge to different plausible solutions, leading to greater
variation in the resulting SUVR estimates. We therefore used
model-to-model variation as a subject-specific uncertainty scale,
with larger variation indicating a less stable MRI-free SUVR
estimate.
To quantify this uncertainty, we employed a deep ensemble
[19] consisting of five independently trained instances of the
proposed CT-to-MRI model, initialized differently and trained
with different mini-batch orders. Each model generated a syn-
thetic MRI that was independently processed through the same
FreeSurfer-based PET quantification pipeline, yielding five SUVR
estimates for each subject. The ensemble mean,

Reliability assessment of amyloid-positivity decisions. We fur-
ther used the conformal prediction interval to assess the reliabil-

MCSUVR estimates. Because the prediction interval represents
a plausible range for the corresponding real MRI-based MC-
SUVR, its position relative to the prespecified decision cut-off
τ = 1.42 indicates whether the decision remains unambiguous
despite uncertainty in the MRI-free estimate.
If the entire interval lay below the cut-off, the interval sup-
ported a confidently negative decision. If the entire interval lay
above the cut-off, the interval supported a confidently positive
decision. When the interval spanned the cut-off, the correspond-
ing real MRI-based MCSUVR could lie on either side of the
decision boundary, and the case was therefore classified as in-
determinate. Under this rule, a confident decision can disagree
with the real MRI-based classification only if the reference MC-
SUVR falls outside the prediction interval.
We additionally examined the robustness of this decision
rule to the choice of MCSUVR cut-off by repeating the analysis
across a range of cut-off values while keeping the conformal
intervals fixed. At each cut-off, we assessed whether disagree-
ments between the MRI-free and real MRI-based decisions were
identified as indeterminate by the conformal decision rule.

2.7. Zero-shot external evaluation
To evaluate zero-shot generalization, the trained CT-to-MRI
models were applied to the independent GARD cohort [27],
which was not used for model training or model selection, with-
out any retraining, fine-tuning, or adaptation. The same prepro-
cessing, CT-to-MRI synthesis, FreeSurfer parcellation, and PET
quantification procedures used for the internal evaluation were
applied to GARD.
CT-to-MRI synthesis and the resulting anatomical parcella-
tions were evaluated against the paired real T1-weighted MRI.
We then evaluated MRI-free PET quantification by comparing
synthetic-MRI-based SUVR estimates with the corresponding
real MRI-based SUVR values.
We further evaluated the transferability of the conformal
calibration to the external cohort. The OASIS-3 conformal cali-
bration was first applied directly to GARD without recalibration,
and the resulting empirical coverage was compared with the
prespecified target coverage. We additionally examined whether
target-cohort recalibration could recover the intended coverage
without retraining or fine-tuning the CT-to-MRI synthesis model.
For this analysis, the synthesis models were kept fixed, and
only the conformal scaling was recalibrated using subjects from
GARD. GARD subjects were repeatedly divided into calibration
and test subsets, with the conformal scaling factor estimated
from the calibration subset and coverage and interval width
evaluated in the held-out test subset.
3. Results
3.1. Compensating for limited anatomical information in CT
We first examined which design strategies could compensate
for the limited anatomical information available in CT. The
cumulative ablation analysis shows progressive improvement
over the BBDM baseline as multi-dataset training (H1), data-
source conditioning (H2), and expanded multi-slice anatomical
context (H3) are introduced (Fig. 3; Section Appendix A.5).
Among these design choices, H3 provides the largest marginal
performance gain, while H1 and H2 provide smaller incremental
improvements. In contrast, segmentation-teacher feedback (H4)
provides no significant additional improvement.
We further evaluated the extent of the multi-slice CT con-
text by varying the number of input CT slices from 3 to 15 on
the validation sets. Using a wider context than the three-slice
baseline generally improves synthesis fidelity, with particularly
consistent gains on OASIS-3 and performance approaching a
plateau on Severance. We therefore use 15 input CT slices in the
final model. Detailed results across all evaluated context sizes
are provided in Appendix A.6. Based on these analyses, the final
proposed model incorporates multi-dataset training, data-source
conditioning, and expanded multi-slice anatomical context.
3.2. Anatomical fidelity of CT-to-MRI synthesis
Having identified the final model configuration, we next eval-
uated its ability to reconstruct subject-specific anatomy on the
Severance and OASIS-3 test sets. The proposed model achieves
the highest synthesis fidelity among the competing methods (Ta-
ble 1). On the Severance cohort it attains 26.02 ± 2.04 dB PSNR
and 0.937 ± 0.018 SSIM3D, and on OASIS-3 24.69 ± 0.96 dB
and 0.877 ± 0.018, the best on both cohorts and both metrics.
Choo2024 is the second-best method, and a one-sided Wilcoxon
signed-rank test confirms that our model outperforms it with
high significance (p < 0.001) for both metrics on both cohorts.
The remaining baselines show substantially lower performance.
Qualitatively (Fig. 4), our method and Choo2024 recover
the overall cortical anatomy more faithfully than the remaining
baselines, including the cerebellar folding that defines the SUVR
reference region despite its weak contrast in CT. Kim2025 and
LSGAN produce overly smooth cortical boundaries with substan-
tial loss of anatomical detail. ALDM generates visually detailed
MRIs, but the images contain noticeable high-frequency noise
and frequently deviate from the ground truth in cortical folding
patterns and other subject-specific anatomical structures. Conse-
quently, the apparent realism of its texture does not necessarily
correspond to faithful reconstruction of the individual’s anatomy.
Although the synthesized MRIs produced by our method and
Choo2024 appear similarly realistic, the cortical error maps re-
veal consistently smaller residual errors for the proposed method.
The remaining errors are largely confined to boundary regions
around complex cortical folds.
A subject-level paired comparison against the strongest base-
line, Choo2024, shows that the improvement is consistent across
individual subjects. Our model outperforms Choo2024 for all
subjects in SSIM3D in both cohorts and in PSNR on OASIS-
3. For Severance PSNR, it outperforms Choo2024 in 21 of 22

3.3. MRI-free PET quantification
We next examined whether the improved anatomical fidelity
of the synthesized MRI translates into more accurate PET quan-
tification on the OASIS-3 test set (Table 3; Fig. 6). Accuracy
was evaluated against the real MRI-based SUVR from the same
subject using the identical quantification pipeline, with only the
anatomical information used for PET processing replaced. This
analysis therefore measures agreement with the MRI-based ref-
erence under MRI substitution rather than diagnostic accuracy
against an independent biological standard.
Among the evaluated pipelines, agreement with the MRI-
based reference was lowest for the PET-only pipelines, interme-
diate for the CT-based pipelines, and highest for the CT-to-MRI-
based pipelines. The two PET-only pipelines show the weak-
est agreement (GAAIN-derived template: MAE 0.162 ± 0.095,
r = 0.952; OASIS-derived template: MAE 0.131 ± 0.080,
r = 0.973). Their estimates are compressed toward the cohort
mean (slopes 0.75 and 0.69), overestimating amyloid-negative
subjects, with amyloid-positivity concordances of 89.7% and
96.6%. Incorporating subject-specific CT anatomy further re-
duces the error (Presotto2018: MAE 0.055 ± 0.051, r = 0.989;
Matsuda2021: MAE 0.053 ± 0.062, r = 0.982).

3.4. External generalization on the GARD cohort
We next evaluated zero-shot external generalization on the
independent GARD cohort, which was not used for CT-to-MRI
model development. All synthesis models were applied without
retraining or fine-tuning. Despite this zero-shot setting, the
proposed method maintained the highest structural similarity
to the real T1-weighted MRI among the compared methods
(Fig. 7). Among the 84 subjects with complete synthesis results,
the proposed method achieved the highest SSIM3D in 62 subjects,
whereas LSGAN achieved the highest value in the remaining 22
subjects. Overall, the proposed method achieved an SSIM3D of
0.794 ± 0.034, compared with 0.780 ± 0.029 for LSGAN, which
was the strongest baseline.
The improved synthesis fidelity was also reflected in the
anatomical parcellations derived from the synthetic MRIs (Fig. 7).
Among the 80 subjects with valid FreeSurfer results for the real
MRI and all compared methods, the proposed method achieved
the best result in 11 of the 12 region–metric comparisons across
DSC and HD95. The largest advantage was observed in the
cerebral cortex, whereas performance in cerebellar white mat-
ter was comparable to Choo2024. Notably, although LSGAN
ranked second in SSIM3D, this did not translate into comparable
FreeSurfer parcellation accuracy.
We finally examined whether the zero-shot generalization
observed in CT-to-MRI synthesis translated into MRI-free PET
quantification on the external GARD cohort (Table 5). Among
the 80 subjects with valid PET quantification results for the
real MRI and all compared methods, the proposed pipeline
maintained strong agreement with the real MRI-based refer-
ence across MCSUVR and the same six regional SUVR mea-
sures evaluated in the primary OASIS-3 analysis. The proposed
method achieved the lowest MAE in four of the six regional
SUVR measures and the highest Pearson correlation for MC-
SUVR and all six regional measures. ALDM and LSGAN
yielded a slightly lower MAE for MCSUVR, and ALDM also
for the lateral temporal region, whereas LSGAN yielded the
lowest MAE in the frontal region. Overall, the proposed method
showed the most consistent agreement across the evaluated re-
gional measures. Together, these results show that the proposed
method achieved favorable performance across CT-to-MRI syn-
thesis, anatomical parcellation, and MRI-free PET quantification
when applied zero-shot to an independent cohort.
3.5. Reliability assessment of MRI-free PET quantification
We next evaluated whether the conformal prediction inter-
vals achieved the prespecified coverage for the corresponding
real MRI-based SUVR (Table 6). For MCSUVR, empirical cov-
erage closely matched the target coverage across the evaluated
settings. Target coverages of 80%, 90%, and 95% yielded empir-
ical coverages of 80.5%, 92.0%, and 96.6%, respectively, while
mean prediction interval width increased from 0.0524 to 0.1050
SUVR units with increasing target coverage.
At the 90% target coverage, empirical coverage across the six
regional SUVR measures ranged from 88.5% to 96.6%. Mean
prediction interval width varied across regions, from 0.0693
SUVR units in the parietal cortex to 0.1847 in the anterior cin-
gulate cortex (Table 6). The posterior cingulate also showed
relatively wide intervals, whereas the parietal and lateral tempo-
ral regions showed the narrowest intervals. Regional empirical
coverage and prediction interval width at the 80% and 95%
target coverage levels are provided in Appendix A.9.
We next examined whether the prediction intervals could
support confident amyloid-positivity decisions in the absence of
the real MRI (Fig. 8). For this decision analysis, we used the
90% target coverage as the primary setting and the prespecified
MCSUVR cut-off of 1.42 as the decision boundary.

At the prespecified MCSUVR cut-off of 1.42, the amyloid-
positivity decisions obtained from our synthetic-MRI-based
MRI-free pipeline reproduced the real MRI-based decisions for
all 87 test subjects, with no false-positive or false-negative cases.
We further examined whether this agreement was sensitive to the
choice of MCSUVR cut-off and whether conformal prediction
could identify unreliable decisions when disagreements emerged.
In a supplementary sensitivity analysis, the cut-off was varied
from 1.30 to 1.50 while the 90% conformal prediction inter-
vals were held fixed. Disagreements between the MRI-free and
real MRI-based decisions occurred only at cut-offs from 1.34
to 1.41. All such cases were designated indeterminate because
their prediction intervals spanned the cut-off. Conversely, all
decisions retained as confident remained concordant with the
real MRI-based reference across the evaluated cut-off range (Ap-
pendix A.10). These results indicate that the conformal intervals
can distinguish cases requiring caution from those for which
the MRI-free amyloid-positivity decision remains stable with
respect to the real MRI-based reference.
Finally, we examined whether conformal calibration per-
formed on OASIS-3 transferred to the independent GARD co-
hort. Although MRI-free SUVR estimates retained strong zero-
shot agreement with the MRI-based reference on GARD, predic-
tion intervals calibrated on OASIS-3 substantially undercovered
the GARD reference values. For MCSUVR, empirical coverage
at the 90% target level decreased to 62.2%. In contrast, when
the same conformal procedure was recalibrated within GARD
using repeated random split-half calibration/test partitions, em-
pirical coverage returned close to the target level (90.7 ± 6.2%
across 1,000 splits). Similar patterns were observed across the
six regional SUVR measures (Appendix A.11). These results in-
dicate that conformal calibration did not transfer directly across
cohorts, but could be restored using a calibration subset from the
target cohort without retraining or fine-tuning the CT-to-MRI
synthesis model.
4. Discussion
In this study, we addressed three questions central to CT-
to-MRI-based MRI-free PET quantification. First, how can
subject-specific T1-weighted MRI anatomy be inferred more
faithfully from the limited anatomical information available in
brain CT? Second, does improving this anatomical inference
translate into more accurate MRI-free PET quantification? Third,
in a practical setting where the real MRI is unavailable, how
can the reliability of an individual MRI-free SUVR estimate be
assessed?
To address the first question, we strengthened the anatomi-
cal information available to the synthesis model through multi-
dataset training, data-source conditioning, and expanded multi-
slice CT context. The resulting improvements in CT-to-MRI
synthesis were accompanied by more accurate anatomical parcel-
lation and closer agreement with real MRI-based PET quantifica-
tion. To address the third question, we combined deep-ensemble
uncertainty with conformal prediction to estimate a plausible
range for the corresponding real MRI-based SUVR and used this
range to assess the reliability of amyloid-positivity decisions. Fi-
nally, we evaluated whether these improvements generalized to
an independent external cohort without retraining or adaptation
of the CT-to-MRI synthesis model.
Improving MRI-free PET quantification through better anatomi-
cal inference. Previous work [15] showed that CT-to-MRI syn-
thesis can be incorporated into MRI-based PET quantification
pipelines, establishing its feasibility for MRI-free PET quan-
tification. Separately, Choo et al. [17] improved CT-to-MRI
synthesis using a slice-consistent Brownian Bridge diffusion
framework [18]. Against this background, we examined whether
richer anatomical information available to the synthesis model
could further improve CT-to-MRI synthesis and, in turn, MRI-
free PET quantification. Multi-dataset training and data-source
conditioning provided incremental improvements, whereas ex-
panded multi-slice CT context contributed the largest gain in
the ablation analysis. These findings suggest that improving
CT-to-MRI synthesis depends not only on the generative formu-
lation, but also on the anatomical prior learned from the training
data and the amount of subject-specific anatomical information
available from the input CT.
These improvements were reflected in downstream PET
quantification. Among the evaluated pipelines, agreement with
the real MRI-based reference was lowest for the PET-only pipelines,
intermediate for the CT-based pipelines, and highest for the CT-
to-MRI-based pipelines. The proposed method achieved the
closest agreement with the MRI-based reference on OASIS-3
across both composite and regional SUVR measures. This is
particularly relevant because accurate SUVR quantification de-
pends on anatomical localization of both cortical target regions
and the cerebellar reference region [7].
The FreeSurfer parcellation analysis further showed that
the improvement was not limited to image-level similarity. Al-
though visually plausible synthetic images do not necessarily
preserve all subject-specific anatomical features [16], the pro-
posed method also improved regional overlap and boundary
agreement of the derived anatomical parcellations. This sup-
ports the interpretation that the improved PET quantification
was associated with better preservation of anatomy relevant to
regional PET sampling rather than image appearance alone.
Residual errors nevertheless varied across cortical regions.
Errors were relatively larger in the precuneus and anterior and
posterior cingulate cortices than in the lateral cortical regions.
These medial regions contain complex cortical folding and nar-
row gray–white matter boundaries that are only weakly repre-
sented in CT, making their individual anatomy more difficult to
infer. Small boundary differences in these structures can also al-
ter regional PET quantification. The persistence of larger errors
in these regions therefore indicates that recovery of fine medial
cortical anatomy remains an important challenge for CT-based
MRI inference.
Although the present study focused on amyloid PET, the
overall framework is not inherently tracer-specific. MRI-derived
anatomical information is also used for regional quantification
of other brain PET tracers, including 18F-FDG PET and tau
PET [38, 39]. Accordingly, the same principle could potentially
14


be extended to these applications by replacing the unavailable
MRI with CT-derived synthetic MRI while retaining the tracer-
specific PET quantification pipeline. Validation across additional
tracers and clinical applications will be necessary to establish
this broader applicability.
Estimating the plausible range of the real MRI-based SUVR.
Accurate MRI-free PET quantification does not by itself indicate
how close an individual SUVR estimate is to the value that would
have been obtained using the real MRI. In a practical MRI-free
setting, the real MRI is unavailable, so this discrepancy cannot
be directly measured for a given subject. Therefore, even when
MRI-free SUVR estimates show high overall agreement with
the real MRI-based reference, it remains important to estimate a
plausible range for the corresponding real MRI-based SUVR for
each subject.
We addressed this problem by constructing calibrated predic-
tion intervals for the corresponding real MRI-based SUVR using
deep-ensemble uncertainty and conformal prediction [19, 20,
21, 22]. Across the evaluated target coverage levels, empirical
coverage closely followed the intended coverage for MCSUVR,
with greater variation across regional SUVR measures. The
coverage confidence intervals reflect the limited precision of
these estimates from 87 test subjects. These results support the
feasibility of estimating a plausible range for the corresponding
real MRI-based SUVR within OASIS-3, where the calibration
and test subjects were drawn from the same cohort, even when
the real MRI itself is unavailable.
The width of this interval adds information that is not avail-
able from the MRI-free SUVR value alone. A narrow interval
constrains the possible real MRI-based SUVR to a relatively
small range around the MRI-free estimate, whereas a wider in-
terval indicates that a broader range of real MRI-based SUVR
values remains compatible with the MRI-free result. Such infor-
mation may help determine whether an MRI-free quantification
result is sufficiently reliable for its intended use or whether
further evaluation, such as acquisition of an MRI, should be
considered.
This becomes particularly relevant when the SUVR is in-
terpreted relative to an amyloid-positivity decision cut-off. At
the primary 90% target coverage, 85 of 87 subjects (97.7%) had
prediction intervals that remained entirely on one side of the
prespecified cut-off, whereas only two subjects were classified as
indeterminate. In this retrospective cohort, this suggests that the
large majority of subjects could have received an unambiguous
amyloid-positivity decision from the MRI-free pipeline, while
additional assessment could have been reserved for the small
subset whose prediction intervals crossed the decision bound-
ary. The sensitivity analysis further supported this interpretation,
as all observed disagreements between the MRI-free and real
MRI-based decisions were captured by the indeterminate cate-
gory across the evaluated cut-offs. In interpreting these results,
the nominal coverage level applies to the prediction intervals
and does not directly specify the classification accuracy among
subjects receiving a confident decision.
If confirmed prospectively, such an accept-or-defer strategy
could have practical implications for the use of MRI in PET
quantification. Rather than requiring an additional MRI for
every subject, MRI or another confirmatory assessment could
potentially be prioritized for cases in which the MRI-free SUVR
remains insufficiently constrained or its prediction interval over-
laps a clinically relevant decision boundary. This could reduce
additional imaging burden and potentially lower the resources
required for quantitative PET assessment, while preserving a
mechanism for referring uncertain cases for further evaluation.
A formal cost-effectiveness analysis would, however, be required
before drawing conclusions regarding economic benefit.
This selective strategy may also be relevant for patients in
whom MRI is difficult or unavailable because of contraindi-
cations, claustrophobia, motion, acquisition burden, or other
practical limitations [8]. Previous MRI-free approaches have
similarly sought to reduce dependence on structural MRI for
PET quantification [9, 12, 13, 15]. Providing both an MRI-free
SUVR and an estimate of its plausible range may extend this
goal by allowing quantitative PET assessment even when MRI
cannot readily be obtained, while explicitly identifying subjects
for whom the MRI-free result remains too uncertain to rely on
without further evaluation.
Zero-shot external generalization and conformal calibration
transfer. The independent GARD evaluation examined whether
the proposed approach could be applied beyond the datasets
used for model training. Despite differences in CT acquisi-
tion characteristics and amyloid tracer, the proposed model
largely preserved its relative ranking across CT-to-MRI synthe-
sis, anatomical parcellation, and MRI-free PET quantification
without retraining or fine-tuning of model parameters, although
its absolute errors were larger than in OASIS-3. These find-
ings provide preliminary evidence that the learned CT-to-MRI
mapping may generalize across cohorts and acquisition settings.
In contrast, direct transfer of the conformal calibration from
OASIS-3 to GARD resulted in substantial undercoverage, sug-
gesting a shift in the distribution of ensemble-normalized pre-
diction errors between cohorts. Because standard conformal
prediction relies on exchangeability between calibration and
test samples, such distributional differences can invalidate the
nominal coverage guarantee [40, 22]. Differences in cohort char-
acteristics, imaging acquisition, and amyloid tracer may all have
contributed to this shift.
Notably, empirical coverage returned close to the target level
after recalibrating the conformal scaling using a subset of GARD
subjects, while the CT-to-MRI synthesis models themselves
were left unchanged. In a new cohort, it may therefore not be
necessary to retrain the synthesis model solely to recover the
intended prediction-interval coverage. Instead, conformal cali-
bration can be updated using a target-cohort calibration set while
retaining the existing synthesis model. In the GARD analysis,
this required paired CT, real T1-weighted MRI, and amyloid
PET data from 41 subjects per split. At the 90% target cover-
age, mean MCSUVR interval width on GARD increased from
0.119 with OASIS-3 calibration to 0.203 with GARD-specific
recalibration. This suggests the possibility that, after local re-
calibration with a limited number of paired cases, the approach
could be applied to a new imaging environment and still provide
15


a plausible range for the corresponding real MRI-based SUVR
in subsequent subjects for whom MRI is not available.
Limitations and future directions. Several challenges remain
before the proposed approach can be made more generalizable
and clinically robust. First, the anatomical information available
in CT is fundamentally limited, particularly for fine gray–white
matter boundaries and complex cortical folding. The relatively
larger errors observed in the precuneus and cingulate regions
suggest that subject-specific anatomy cannot always be recov-
ered accurately from CT alone. Further work is therefore needed
to improve the recovery of fine individual anatomy from the
limited structural information available in CT.
Second, the current data-source conditioning strategy limits
straightforward application to previously unseen imaging envi-
ronments. The model was trained using discrete source labels
corresponding to the datasets included during training, whereas
a new external dataset does not have a predefined source label.
For GARD, the OASIS-3 label was used as a surrogate because
its CT characteristics were most similar, but this is not a general
solution. More generalizable conditioning strategies that can
infer acquisition or image characteristics directly from the in-
put CT, rather than relying on predefined dataset identities, will
therefore be needed for broader deployment.
Third, the present training data were not designed to system-
atically represent focal or atypical brain abnormalities, such as
brain tumors or substantial white matter lesions. Medical im-
age translation models can alter or fail to preserve pathological
features that are uncommon in the training distribution [16]. In
the present pipeline, such errors could propagate beyond the
synthesized image, because inaccurate representation of abnor-
mal anatomy may affect anatomical parcellation and regional
PET sampling, and consequently alter the resulting SUVR. Fu-
ture work should therefore develop synthesis models that can
more reliably preserve such structural abnormalities and prevent
their errors from propagating to anatomical parcellation and PET
quantification.
Fourth, the PET analyses were restricted to subjects with
same-day PiB PET and T1-weighted MRI and to scans passing
quality control, which may favor higher-quality acquisitions. In
addition, differences in synthesis or quantification performance
across age, sex, or ethnic groups were not assessed, although the
development (OASIS-3) and external (GARD) cohorts differ in
population.
Finally, this study was retrospective and external validation
was performed in a single additional cohort. Prospective multi-
center validation across broader scanners, acquisition protocols,
populations, and PET tracers will be needed to establish the
robustness and generalizability of the proposed approach.
5. Conclusion
In conclusion, this study improved the accuracy of CT-to-
MRI-based MRI-free PET quantification and showed that, when
conformal prediction is calibrated for the target cohort, the relia-
bility of individual SUVR estimates can be assessed when the
real MRI is unavailable. By strengthening the anatomical prior
learned during training and incorporating richer subject-specific
information from the input CT, the proposed approach improved
CT-to-MRI synthesis, anatomical parcellation, and downstream
PET quantification relative to the other approaches evaluated
here. The resulting MRI-free SUVR estimates showed close
agreement with the corresponding real MRI-based reference in
OASIS-3 and favorable performance when applied zero-shot to
an independent external cohort.
Beyond improving quantification accuracy, the proposed re-
liability framework used deep-ensemble uncertainty and confor-
mal prediction to estimate a plausible range for the correspond-
ing real MRI-based SUVR. In OASIS-3, this made it possible
to distinguish cases in which an MRI-free amyloid-positivity
decision was well separated from the decision cut-off from those
in which additional evaluation may be warranted. The external
evaluation further showed that conformal calibration may not
transfer directly across cohorts, but that target-cohort recalibra-
tion can restore the intended coverage without retraining the
CT-to-MRI synthesis model. Together, these findings support
the potential of CT-to-MRI-based MRI-free PET quantification
to provide both accurate SUVR measurements and an explicit
assessment of their reliability, while highlighting the importance
of robust anatomical inference and local calibration for broader
clinical deployment.

Appendix A.2. CT-to-MRI synthesis baseline implementation details
The implementation settings of the four CT-to-MRI synthesis baselines are summarized in Table A.2. The following paragraphs
describe how the published methods or released code were adapted to the present task.
Common protocol. All four CT-to-MRI synthesis baselines used the paired CT–MRI preprocessing described in Section 2.3,
including CT-to-T1 registration, brain extraction, MNI152 alignment, and the same intensity normalization and 176 × 208 × 176 crop.
Inputs were rescaled to [−1, 1] for model training. Each baseline was trained separately on the OASIS-3 (N = 773) and Severance
(N = 180) training sets; the OASIS-3 models were also applied to GARD without retraining. A fixed random seed of 1337 was used
for the baseline data splits and initialization. All methods were evaluated using the same brain-masked PSNR and SSIM3D metrics,
FreeSurfer parcellation, and PET quantification procedures.
Choo2024 (slice-consistent BBDM). We adapted the authors’ released implementation (https://github.com/MICV-yonsei/CT
2MRI) [S1], retaining its three-slice architecture, histogram conditioning, and slice-consistent ISTA-style sampling. The released
160 × 160 slices were replaced by the 208 × 176 slices from our MNI-space preprocessing. Training used the released optimizer
settings, an effective batch size of 16, and 100,000 optimizer steps. At inference, the MRI histogram condition was the training-set
mean rather than a histogram from the test subject’s MRI. We used bfloat16 autocast, clipped the predicted clean image to [−1, 1]
during sampling, and selected the checkpoint on the validation set rather than using the final checkpoint.
ALDM (adaptive latent diffusion model). We used the network modules from the authors’ released code (https://github.com
/jongdory/ALDM) [S2] and wrote a PyTorch training loop for paired CT-to-T1 synthesis. The first stage followed the released
VQ-GAN architecture with an ℓ1 training objective; its discriminator and codebook loss weights were set to zero. It was trained on
CT–T1 pairs and then frozen. We used a single MS-SPADE target branch and disabled modality-label conditioning because the task
has one target modality. Compared with the publication, our implementation used a higher learning rate (10−4 rather than 2 × 10−6), a
plain linear rather than scaled-linear noise schedule, and an effective batch size of four on a single 40 GB A100. The latent diffusion
stage was trained for approximately 100,000 optimizer steps per dataset, and inference used deterministic 100-step DDIM sampling
rather than the released code’s 200-step stochastic sampling.

Appendix A.4. Segmentation-teacher feedback
As an additional anatomical regularization strategy, we explored a segmentation-teacher module that provided an auxiliary
training signal to the CT-to-MRI synthesis model. The motivation was to encourage the generated MRI to preserve tissue boundaries
and anatomical structures that may not be fully captured by the original image-level synthesis objective alone.
The teacher was a pretrained 2.5D depthwise-separable U-Net with approximately 2.2 million parameters. It received three
adjacent T1-weighted MRI slices as input and predicted seven tissue classes, including background. The model was pretrained
using FreeSurfer-derived segmentation labels from 773 OASIS-3 training pairs (620 subjects). Gaussian intensity noise and spatial
augmentation were applied during teacher pretraining to improve robustness to variation in image appearance.
During CT-to-MRI training, the pretrained teacher network was frozen and used only to provide an auxiliary anatomical loss.
Both the generated MRI and the corresponding real MRI were passed through the teacher network, and their predicted tissue
probabilities were compared using a Dice-based loss. The loss was computed within the brain mask and averaged over the six
foreground tissue classes:

Appendix A.5. Ablation study of the proposed design choices

Appendix A.6. Effect of multi-slice CT context
To determine the extent of through-plane CT context used in H3, we evaluated progressively wider input contexts from the
original three-slice configuration up to 15 consecutive axial CT slices within a practical computational budget. All other model
components were kept fixed across context sizes. Performance was evaluated on the same held-out validation sets used for the
cumulative ablation analysis.
On OASIS-3, synthesis fidelity generally improved as the CT context was expanded, with both PSNR and SSIM3D increasing
over the three-slice baseline (Fig. A.1). On Severance, most of the improvement was obtained with a smaller increase in context,
after which performance approached a plateau. Given the continued improvement on OASIS-3 and the plateauing performance on
Severance, we selected 15 input CT slices for the final model among the evaluated settings.

consecutive context sizes were performed using two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction for multiple comparisons.

Appendix A.10. Sensitivity of amyloid-positivity decision reliability to the MCSUVR cut-off
At the prespecified MCSUVR cut-off of 1.42, the amyloid-positivity decisions obtained from our synthetic-MRI-based MRI-free
pipeline reproduced the real MRI-based decisions for all 87 test subjects. We therefore examined whether this agreement was
sensitive to the choice of MCSUVR cut-off and whether conformal prediction could identify unreliable decisions when disagreements
emerged. The cut-off was varied from 1.30 to 1.50 in increments of 0.01, while the 90% conformal prediction intervals were held
fixed. At each cut-off, MRI-free point-estimate decisions were compared with the corresponding real MRI-based reference decisions,
26


and cases whose prediction intervals spanned the cut-off were designated indeterminate.
Point-estimate disagreement with the real MRI-based reference decision was rare and occurred only at cut-offs from 1.34 to 1.41
(Fig. A.4). The maximum disagreement involved two subjects at a cut-off of 1.35. At every cut-off at which a disagreement occurred,
all discordant point-estimate decisions were designated indeterminate. This shows that cases in which the MRI-free decision differed
from the real MRI-based reference were consistently flagged by the conformal decision rule. Conversely, no discordant case remained
among subjects receiving a confident decision, and all confident decisions matched the real MRI-based reference decision across the
evaluated cut-off range.
The proportion of subjects designated indeterminate remained low throughout the sweep, ranging from 1.1% to 4.6% (1–4
of 87 subjects). Accordingly, 83–86 subjects retained a confident decision depending on the cut-off. Representative cut-offs are
summarized in Table A.7. Within this test set and evaluated cut-off range, all observed point-estimate disagreements were therefore
deferred as indeterminate, while decisions retained as confident remained concordant with the real MRI-based reference decision.

Appendix A.11. External transfer of conformal calibration to GARD
We further examined whether conformal calibration derived from OASIS-3 transferred to the independent GARD cohort. The
same nonconformity score used in the main analysis, defined as the absolute MRI-free quantification error normalized by the
subject-specific ensemble standard deviation, was used throughout this analysis. For the conformal analysis, we included 82 GARD
subjects with successful FreeSurfer processing and valid PET quantification results for all ensemble members of the proposed model.
27


For cross-cohort transfer, conformal quantiles were estimated from the OASIS-3 calibration set and applied directly to GARD
without recalibration.
Direct transfer of the OASIS-3 calibration resulted in substantial undercoverage on GARD (Table A.8). For MCSUVR, target
coverage levels of 80%, 90%, and 95% resulted in empirical coverages of 42.7%, 62.2%, and 78.0%, respectively. Undercoverage
was also observed across all six regional SUVR measures at the 90% target level, with empirical coverage ranging from 57.3% in the
precuneus to 86.6% in the anterior cingulate cortex.
We therefore evaluated whether coverage could be recovered using calibration data drawn from the GARD cohort. The 82
eligible GARD subjects were randomly divided into equal calibration and test sets (N = 41 each), and this split-half procedure was
repeated 1,000 times to reduce dependence on a particular random partition. With GARD-specific calibration, empirical MCSUVR
coverage returned close to the requested levels, reaching 81.4 ± 8.8%, 90.7 ± 6.2%, and 95.2 ± 4.6% at target coverage levels of 80%,
90%, and 95%, respectively. At the 90% target level, mean regional coverage across the repeated splits ranged from 90.2% to 90.7%.
Prediction intervals were correspondingly wider than those obtained by directly transferring the OASIS-3 calibration. These results
show that GARD-specific recalibration yielded empirical coverage close to the target marginal coverage, whereas calibration derived
from OASIS-3 did not transfer directly to the external cohort.
