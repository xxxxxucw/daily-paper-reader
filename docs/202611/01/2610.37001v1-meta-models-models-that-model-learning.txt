Every neural-network training run produces not only a model, but also evidence about how models learn. Can these accumulated experiments enable us to predict and compare new training runs before executing them? We study meta-models: models that predict the learning behavior of other models. From this perspective, final-performance prediction, learning-curve prediction, and hyperparameter selection are different queries to the same training response function. We take a first, humble empirical step toward this goal: treating real language-model training records as native numeric data, we find that an off-the-shelf tabular in-context learner, TabPFN, can match or outperform a fine-tuned LLM meta-model in multiple evaluated settings. Whereas recent work fine-tunes LLMs on tokenized configurations at a cost of tens to hundreds of GPU-hours, TabPFN requires seconds of fit-and-predict time without task-specific weight updates. We argue that meta-modeling is better treated as its own numeric modality rather than as a text task for LLMs. Going one step further, augmenting TabPFN with Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks (KANs) further improves accuracy on selected prediction tasks. Finally, we pre-registered a forecast of the Marin Hero Run, a 535B-parameter public training run, whose ground truth is revealed point by point as training progresses, putting meta-model predictions to the test against training outcomes not yet observed at the time of forecasting. 

1. Introduction 

Before launching a training run, researchers already make predictions. Will a different learning rate help? Which data recipe should receive the next compute budget? Will a promising small-scale result survive scaling up? Each completed run supplies evidence for such judgments. Can these accumulated records become a reusable model of training, allowing new experiments to be queried before they are executed? We call this predictive object a meta-model of training : a model that predicts the learning behavior of other models. In our loss-prediction setting, ̂

L(q, t ) = fθ(q, t | R ), R = {(qi, t ij , Lij )}i,j ,

where q is a training configuration, t is progress, and R contains historical observations. Querying different progress values produces a trajectory; comparing configurations supports experiment selection (Figure 1). Other measured outcomes, such as downstream accuracy, define further prediction tasks. Scaling laws connect loss to model and data size (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022); nor-malized curves reveal shared dynamics under compute-optimal training (Qiu et al., 2025). NCPL extends this line of work by using a fine-tuned LLM to represent a configuration-to-performance scaling law (Zhang et al., 2026). These advances motivate a central challenge for meta-modeling: combining accurate prediction, broad applicability, and efficient adaptation. A useful meta-model should accurately predict the outcomes of training choices, support diverse configurations and pre-diction targets, and incorporate new observations without repeated task-specific retraining.

We take a first, humble empirical step by treating language-model training records as a native numeric modality : configuration fields and measurements form input–output examples rather than tokenized descriptions. We find that TabPFN, a pretrained tabular model that learns predictive priors from synthetic tables (Hollmann et al., 2025; Grinsztajn et al., 2026), already supports useful predictions of training. Across tasks, we supply historical examples as context and new configurations as queries, reusing the same pretrained backbone without updating its weights. Our experiments reveal three capabilities of this meta-model (§2): accurate prediction of training behavior , encompassing final loss and configuration-dependent trajectories, with fur-ther applications to other training observables and downstream accuracy; reuse across training questions , as the same pretrained backbone extends from dense-model prediction to MoE expert configurations, data recipes, and different prediction targets without task-specific fine-tuning; and 

adaptation through new observations , where adding the first 20% of each target trajectory to historical context reduces run-averaged future-loss MAE by 56% across seven held-out Delphi runs (Held and the Marin Community, 2026), without updating backbone weights. We further use KANs (Liu et al., 2024) to learn inspectable configuration coordinates that improve StepLaw (Li et al., 2025) final-loss and curve prediction without updating TabPFN’s backbone (§3). To move beyond retrospective evaluation, we also froze a forecast during the Marin Hero Run and evaluated it against subsequently arriving observations (§4). These studies also expose a remaining challenge: reliable extrapolation beyond observed training conditions.

2.1 Predicting differences between training configurations 

At a fixed model size and training budget, can a meta-model distinguish better training con-figurations from worse ones? We test this capability on optimizer settings and data mixtures, withholding the outcomes of the queried configurations. Delphi’s 3,737 -run hyperparameter sweep (Held and the Marin Community, 2026), denoted Delphi-HP, lets us test whether this prediction transfers between training conditions. Inputs are model width, batch size, token budget, and seven optimizer hyperparameters; the output is final Paloma (Magnusson et al., 2023) macro evaluation loss. We withhold all outcomes at one width–batch– budget combination and give TabPFN only the configuration–loss records from the other 23 com-binations. Within each held-out group, we rank configurations by predicted loss. Across all 24 folds, mean Spearman correlation is 0.967 , versus 0.875 for XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin, 2016) (Figure 2a); TabPFN also has lower loss MAE in every group.

2.2 Predicting the course of training 

Can a meta-model forecast how a training run will unfold before observing its losses? We provide historical runs as context and query TabPFN with a configuration and training progress; successive queries reconstruct a loss trajectory. All evaluations withhold the queried run’s losses and reuse the same frozen backbone, with corpus-specific features and context. 

Configuration-dependent dynamics. TabPFN captures how learning-rate-dependent loss gaps narrow during StepLaw training (Figure 3a–b). These forecasts go beyond shifting a com-mon decay curve by a fixed offset: their error on time-varying OOD gaps is 38% lower than this control (c). Marin extends the test to different optimizers (d–e). Accuracy is not uniform, how-ever: TabPFN’s higher Marin OOD error than reproduced NCPL arises mainly in early training (f; Table 1). 

Extending to expert configurations. Adding expert count and routing settings lets the same backbone forecast Marin-MoE trajectories from historical MoE runs (Figure 3g). Across held-out OOD runs, curve MAE is approximately 70% lower than MoE-fine-tuned NCPL; this advantage persists beyond early training. The fine-tuned baseline lacks expert fields, so this comparison evaluates the systems as used, rather than isolating backbone quality under identical inputs. 

Transferring data recipes across scales. On DataDecide, records from smaller models sup-port trajectory forecasts for held-out 750M–1B models. TabPFN distinguishes the direction and approximate magnitude of recipe differences at a common scale (Figure 3h), with lower error than recipe-aware XGBoost (i). Removing recipe identity increases OOD error nearly eightfold: the forecasts use recipe information rather than scale alone. All evaluated recipes occur in context; the transfer is to larger models, not unseen recipes.

2.3 Predicting outcomes beyond loss Internal training observables. Can a meta-model predict how training evolves beyond the loss curve? We use Marin records to predict parameter and gradient norms from configuration and training progress, without observing the queried run’s weights or trajectory. Figure 4a–b compares predicted and recorded trajectories for the same two held-out runs. TabPFN captures the rise and subsequent decline of parameter norms and the early decay of gradient norms, rather than imposing a shared decreasing curve on both targets. Peak timing and magnitude remain imperfect, and the forecasts do not recover individual gradient spikes. These predictions reuse the frozen backbone with observable-specific contexts and target processing. 

Downstream performance. We next ask whether configuration–accuracy records from smaller models can forecast the benchmark scores of larger models. We supply historical checkpoint evalua-tions as context and query DataDecide’s held-out 750M–1B models, without observing their losses or benchmark scores. Figure 4c shows that TabPFN distinguishes downstream performance across configurations, although it still underestimates the strongest 1B outcomes. Ten-task OLMES (Gu et al., 2024) macro-accuracy MAE is 1.62 percentage points, versus 3.40 for XGBoost using the same historical records. Errors are lower on all ten individual tasks than under the earlier endpoint-context protocol. Together, these results extend the meta-model’s queries from loss to internal dynamics and downstream outcomes without training a new neural backbone for each target.

2.4 Adapting predictions and guiding experiments Updating forecasts during training. We supply historical training records as context and query losses at future steps for a target configuration. As target-run measurements arrive, we append these configuration–step–loss records and query the remaining trajectory again, without updating backbone weights. On Delphi’s seven larger target runs, observing the first 20% of training reduces average remaining-curve error by 56% (Figure 5a). The benefit is not uniform across runs, but demonstrates that forecasts can be revised through observations rather than another round of model training. 

Choosing training configurations. Before launching a run, we query candidate configura-tions and select the one with the lowest predicted final loss. Historical configuration–loss pairs supply the context; candidate losses are hidden during selection and used afterward to score the recommendation against the best measured candidate. StepLaw’s learning-rate and batch-size sweeps illustrate this procedure (Figure 5b). It also applies to Delphi’s seven-dimensional opti-mizer search: across 23 held-out condition groups, mean excess loss is 0.0038 nats, versus 0.0254 

for XGBoost (c). On StepLaw, TabPFN can select even better: with the learned configuration coordinates of Section 3, its selection regret on larger-model sweeps falls by 80% .

Using observations to guide subsequent experiments. In offline StepLaw replay, we reveal each selected candidate’s measured loss, append it to context, and use updated predictions to choose the next untested configuration. All policies start from the same two random evaluations. We compare history-only predictions, conditioning on the two initial outcomes but freezing context thereafter, and continual context updates. After five total evaluations, mean OOD best-found regret is 0.1398% , 0.0473% , and 0.0394% , respectively (Figure 5d). Incorporating target-condition observations brings the search closer to the best measured configuration with fewer evaluations than history-only predictions, without retraining the backbone. We next ask whether learned configuration coordinates can improve this predictor.

3. TabPFN could be better 

Good hyperparameters change with training scale. We use Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks (KANs; Liu et al., 2024) to learn scale-dependent learning-rate and batch-size references, making a config-uration’s position relative to favorable choices explicit. 

Learning a representation of training choices. Two small KANs learn reference values from the best-performing runs within each StepLaw training group, as functions of model size and token budget (Figure 6). We append log-ratios to these references to the original configuration fields. TabPFN’s backbone stays frozen, and no queried-run outcomes enter the KAN fit. 

Improving predictions and training decisions. Against the default TabPFN route, larger-model final-loss and curve errors decrease by 29% and 16% . Selecting the configuration with lowest predicted loss reduces OOD regret by 80% : references learned from final-loss sweeps also help forecast trajectories and make decisions. ID changes are inconclusive. OOD gains persist against a same-context baseline (Appendix C.1). 

Inspecting what the KAN learns. The learning-rate reference decreases with model size and increases with token budget; batch size varies nonlinearly with token budget. These inspectable relationships are fitted, not universal training laws. We next test the backbone against prospectively arriving outcomes.

5. Discussion and limitations 

Training questions can share a predictive backbone. Configuration fields, prediction tar-gets, and contextual observations can change without new TabPFN weights. This extends meta-modeling beyond a fixed configuration-to-loss mapping, while retaining task-specific representa-tions and target processing. 

From reuse to reliable decisions. Three challenges remain: representing genuinely new regimes, such as unseen data recipes; recognizing unreliable extrapolation; and demonstrating better experimental decisions. Our offline selection results motivate prospective, equal-budget comparisons counting pilot runs, search, and adaptation. Lower prediction error alone does not establish compute savings. 

6. Related work 

Scaling laws and training dynamics. Scaling laws extend beyond model and data size to mixtures (Ye et al., 2025), sparsity (Abnar et al., 2025), and learning-rate schedules (Tissue et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2025). Hyperparameter transfer (Yang et al., 2021; Wang and Aitchison, 2024) and scale-dependent tuning (Porian et al., 2024) further qualify these predictions. Our numerical response functions complement, rather than replace, such structured laws. 

Learned predictors of training outcomes. OmniPred trains language models as general regressors (Song et al., 2024); NCPL specializes an LLM to training outcomes (Zhang et al., 2026). Our question is whether a frozen numerical backbone can serve changing configuration fields, targets, and contexts, not whether text-based regression is inherently incapable of doing so. 

Learning curves and numerical in-context learning. Bayesian neural surrogates (Klein et al., 2017) and Deep Power Laws (Kadra et al., 2023) connect curve prediction to optimization. PFNs amortize inference through pretraining (Müller et al., 2022); LC-PFN, FT-PFN, and Adam-PFN specialize it to curves and hyperparameter search (Adriaensen et al., 2023; Rakotoarison et al., 2024; Athanasiadis et al., 2025). We instead reuse TabPFN’s general tabular prior (Hollmann et al., 2023, 2025) across training questions, without pretraining a dedicated training-curve model. 

Downstream prediction. Model ladders predict through intermediate losses (Bhagia et al., 2025); observational laws use a low-dimensional capability space (Ruan et al., 2024), and NeuNeu learns from accuracy trajectories and token-level losses (Hu et al., 2026). Predictability remains setting-dependent (Lourie et al., 2025). 

Inspectable adaptation. KANs learn univariate edge functions (Liu et al., 2024). We use them to learn inspectable configuration coordinates for a frozen predictor, rather than replace its backbone or establish causal training laws.

B.3 DataDecide: recipe information and context controls 

Without recipe identity, OOD final-loss MAE rises from 0.0178 to 0.1117 . Figure 8 shows why: with DataDecide records as context, TabPFN tracks recipe-dependent losses at every size, whereas context without DataDecide recipes, or NCPL’s recipe-free interface, collapses predictions into size-quantized bands. Released NCPL inputs are identical across recipes at a given size, forcing identical predictions. Size explains 97 .6% of total variance, but recipe explains 99 .2% of within-size variance, so recipe context is necessary for choosing data at fixed scale.

Figure 14 shows all 17 held-out StepLaw sweeps used for one-shot selection (5 ID, 12 OOD) with each method’s chosen candidate, together with NCPL’s and the power law’s published selections digitized from Zhang et al. At N = 1074 M and small D, the published NCPL choice lies at a larger learning rate than the released checkpoint’s, which explains the difference between printed and reproduced NCPL regret. 

Matched context-update controls. Figure 5d isolates the use of target-condition observa-tions with three policies sharing a frozen TabPFN v3 backbone (eight estimators, mean readout). Historical context contains 3,225 Marin and StepLaw training rows. All policies use the same configuration features, fixed corpus-specific Chinchilla residual target, candidate order, and two random initial observations for each of 20 seeds and 17 sweeps. The seed controls both initialization and TabPFN preprocessing. A history-only policy never receives target outcomes; an initial-frozen policy receives the two initial outcomes but no later ones; an updating policy appends each ob-served outcome before making the next choice. All recommend the untested candidate with lowest predicted loss, breaking ties by original candidate order. Each prediction call queries the same complete candidate feature matrix; already evaluated candidates are masked only at selection. No unseen target loss enters preprocessing or the predictor. Initial observations count toward the total budget of twelve. All three arms are rerun under this common protocol, producing 340 matched cases and 1,020 policy trajectories. Per-step prediction vectors and context indices verify each selection; regrets are recomputed from the original measured losses. Seeds are averaged within each condition; paired percentile bootstrap intervals resample the twelve conditions 20,000 times, not the 240 initializations as independent tasks. At budget five, conditioning on the initial outcomes reduces mean regret by 0.09249 percent-age points relative to history only (95% interval [0 .01642 , 0.17977] ). Neither replay establishes prospective training-compute savings.

C. Learned representations and adaptation 

C.1 KAN-learned configuration coordinates Data and tasks. We use 1,517 StepLaw training runs to construct reference hyperparameters and prediction contexts. Final-loss prediction (T1) is evaluated on 370 ID and 694 larger-model OOD runs. Curve prediction (T2) uses their 5,177 and 10,745 recorded checkpoints, with 21,888 training checkpoints as context and no target-run prefix. All checkpoints of a run stay in one split. Configuration selection (T3) uses T1 predictions on five ID and twelve OOD model–data-size groups: we select the candidate with minimum predicted loss and report 100( Lselected −Lmin )/L min ,where Lmin is the best measured candidate loss in that group. 

Learning reference hyperparameters. For each of the 24 model–data-size groups in the training split, we average log learning rate and log batch size over its three lowest-loss runs. These summaries are the supervised targets of two separate pykan (Liu et al., 2024) [2 , 1] networks, with standardized (log 10 N, log 10 D) inputs and standardized targets. Each network has cubic splines with three grid intervals. We fit with 200 L-BFGS iterations, prune, refit for 100 iterations, simplify the edge functions symbolically, and refit for another 100 iterations without updating the grid. The model-size edge is constrained to be linear in log space, an imposed power-law extrapolation prior. The data-size edge is chosen from {x, x 2, x 3, tanh( x)} by fit quality. A nonlinear candidate is retained only if leave-one-training-group-out RMSE is at least 5% below the linear candidate’s; this check uses only the 24 training groups and is applied once per target, before the prediction runs. It retains a linear edge for learning rate and a tanh edge for batch size. The KANs predict log reference values sη(N, D ) and sB (N, D ). The added configuration fields are 

∆η = log 10 η − sη(N, D ), ∆B = log 10 B − sB (N, D ). (1) Original configuration fields, including learning rate and batch size, are retained. The same final-loss-derived references are used for curve prediction, whose queries also include training progress. No evaluation outcomes enter these fits or the contexts.

Its final-loss and curve predictions are those of Table 1. The KAN predictor is scored with these same task-specific readouts and seeds. E.2 Changing feature order without changing a training configuration 

We apply eight fixed permutations to TabPFN’s columns (context and query alike) and to NCPL’s serialized key–value pairs, on the 1,905 Marin/StepLaw ID and OOD runs. TabPFN uses one estimator and three seeds, separating external permutations from its ensemble. Across the four splits, the permutation-averaged MAE of NCPL is 1.8–4.0× its canonical MAE (for example, 0.0115 → 0.0337 on Marin ID), rising under every permutation, whereas TabPFN stays within 0.97 –1.08 ×.

E.3 Row embeddings of the joint-context model 

TabPFN also yields a context-conditioned 512-dimensional embedding for each run (out-of-fold for context rows). Figure 17 (top) projects all 5,130 Marin and StepLaw runs of the joint context. The runs form a horseshoe: the two corpora share the arc but remain locally separated (10-nearest-neighbor same-source purity 0.985 ), and model size varies radially, with the larger held-out OOD runs on the inner band. Final loss varies along the arc, reaching its lowest values at the lower-right hook of compute-rich StepLaw runs (429M parameters, 50B tokens; Figure 18). Along a graph geodesic through the arc, deviation from the fitted scaling law decreases (Spearman −0.50 ), whereas learning rate, batch size, and weight decay are nearly uncorrelated with arc position (|ρ| ≤ 0.05 ).

E.4 Training responses: recovering known relationships 

We vary one configuration field at a time and compare each predictor’s response with measured sweeps (about 5,600 queries, seed 0, per-corpus mean predictions). These probe a surrogate, not new training runs. TabPFN and NCPL both reproduce learning-rate U-curves at all 31 anchors, the optimal-learning-rate exponent ( −0.64 ; measured −0.643 ), and batch-size saturation beyond 256; the predicted batch-optimum exponent in token budget is 0.55 versus 0.62 measured. 

E.5 A proposed canonical training record 

Main experiments use corpus-specific flat encodings. A proposed canonical schema (133 columns: 67 values and 66 masks) distinguishes recorded, inapplicable, and missing fields and fixes per-corpus units.
