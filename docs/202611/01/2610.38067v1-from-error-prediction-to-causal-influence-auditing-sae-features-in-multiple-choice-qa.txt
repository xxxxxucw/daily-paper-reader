# From Error Prediction to Causal Influence: Auditing SAE Features in Multiple-Choice QA 

Aria Nourbakhsh Adelaide Danilov Oleksandr Marchenko Breneur Salima Lamsiyah 

Department of Computer Science (DCS), University of Luxembourg 

Correspondence: aria.nourbakhsh@uni.lu 

Abstract 

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) can predict inter-nal features associated with model errors, but association alone does not establish causal in-fluence. We study this distinction in multiple-choice question answering, where answer pref-erence can be measured by the logit margin between the correct answer and the compet-ing choices. Across eight datasets, we ana-lyze Gemma 3 1B and 4B with Gemma Scope SAEs. We propose a pipeline to filter out the features that have high correlation with format and content artifacts, such as prompt and answer permutation, and evaluate the re-maining ones with causal feature interventions. Our results show that SAE activations contain predictive signals about wrongness and uncer-tainty across models and datasets. After fil-tering artifact-contaminated features, selected features for causal intervention at question and option spans produce larger absolute margin shifts and a higher wrong-to-correct rate than randomly selected features, indicating greater causal leverage. However, answer flips are rare, and we do not observe stable effect di-rections across meaning-preserving changes in prompt format and answer order. An adapted HalluSAE baseline produces larger final-token margin changes, but relies on substantially con-taminated features and does not significantly improve correction rates. Therefore, the identi-fied features reflect context-dependent answer competition rather than the mechanism of in-correct answer errors. 

1 Introduction 

Large language Models (LLMs) can produce out-puts that are incorrect or unsupported by the con-text (Ji et al., 2023). In multiple-choice evalua-tion, observed behavior can also change under se-mantically equivalent prompt forms or answer or-ders (Pezeshkpour and Hruschka, 2024; Ganesh et al., 2026). An incorrect answer may reflect a learned misconception (Lin et al., 2022), un-certainty or competition between plausible candi-dates (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024), or a surface-form bias (Yang et al., 2025). One of the tools to study the behavior of LLMs is Sparse autoencoders (SAEs). SAEs decom-pose language-model activations into sparse fea-ture dictionaries (Cunningham et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2024; Rajamanoharan et al., 2024a; Shu et al., 2025). They support large-scale feature anal-ysis and predictions (McDougall et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026), but predictive or attributive features need not be causal mechanisms (Makelov et al., 2024; Karvonen et al., 2025; Gulko et al., 2025; Kantamneni et al., 2025). A feature may correlate with an incorrect answer because it tracks uncer-tainty, answer format, option position, topic, or final-token decision pressure rather than because it causes the model to choose that answer (Sclar et al., 2024; Orgad et al., 2025). This distinction is the main idea of the current work. In this paper, we explore three questions about SAE features: whether SAE activations predict errors, whether intervening on a feature changes answer preferences, and whether those changes cor-rect errors. Forced-choice evaluation provides an explicit output space for comparing answer log-its (Stoehr et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Ganesh et al., 2026). We explore these questions on two instruction-tuned Gemma 3 models (Gemma Team, 2025); we combine behavioral probes, artifact-risk screening, contrastive direct logit attribution, and instance interventions to answer the above ques-tions. Our results show that SAE features can predict forced-choice errors, but many of them are entan-gled with format, topic, or answer-choice structure. Only question- and option-span interventions pro-duce larger distributional shifts, and only a small number of samples flip to the correct answer. Hence, our contributions are: 1arXiv:2610.38067v1 [cs.CL] 27 Oct 2026 • We introduce a pipeline for discovering behav-iorally predictive SAE features across eight datasets and six token positions. This pipeline combines multiple stages to select predictive and causal features for the model’s answer choices while filtering out features that are highly correlated and contaminated by the datasets, prompt, answer letter, and other arti-facts. • We causally evaluate the filtered features using steering. We found that features extracted from question and option spans produce larger margin changes and a higher wrong-to-correct rate than random-active controls, but answer flips are rare, and effect directions are unstable across steering methods, prompt templates, and answer permutations. • We quantify artifact entanglement in both our candidates and an adapted HalluSAE (Chen et al., 2026) baseline. HalluSAE is predictive and produces large final-token margin shifts; however, 95 .5% of its nonzero feature units fail our artifact-risk criterion. This shows that predictive performance and logit influ-ence do not by themselves establish artifact-independent causal mechanisms. 

2 Related Work 

Sparse Autoencoders for Language Model In-terpretability. Sparse autoencoders are among the promising methods for decomposing dense language-model activations into interpretable la-tent features. Early work showed that SAEs can recover features that are more interpretable than in-dividual neurons (Cunningham et al., 2024), while later work improved reconstruction and sparsity tradeoffs through larger-scale training and architec-tural variants such as Gated SAEs and JumpReLU SAEs (Gao et al., 2024; Rajamanoharan et al., 2024a,b). At a high level, an SAE is trained on internal model activations to reconstruct a dense activation vector from a much larger but sparse set of latent features. Given a model activation x ∈ Rd, the encoder maps it to nonnegative feature activations 

z ∈ Rm, often with m ≫ d:

LSAE = ∥x − ˆx∥22 + λ∥z∥1. (3) Here, each column of Wdec can be interpreted as a learned feature direction, and the scalar zi indicates how strongly feature i is active on a given token. Several recent methods uses SAE features for steering, including activation additions, refusal steering, in-context-learning improvement, and knowledge-selection control (Soo et al., 2025; Chal-nev et al., 2024; O’Brien et al., 2025; Cho and Hockenmaier, 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). These stud-ies show that sparse directions can sometimes be used as actionable control handles. 

Mechanistic analyses of multiple-choice answer-ing. Prior work distinguishes answering a ques-tion from producing its associated option label. Lieberum et al. (2023) identify attention heads that promote the correct answer letter. Wiegreffe et al. (2025) use activation patching and vocabu-lary projections to distinguish answer-symbol se-lection from its subsequent promotion in the output distribution. Wong et al. (2026) identify linearly decodable option-correctness signals and evidence that selecting answer content precedes mapping it to an output symbol. Related causal analyses iden-tify attention heads contributing to option-symbol bias (Yang et al., 2025). These findings motivate distinguishing knowledge-related behavior from format-dependent processing. Our study comple-ments this work by testing whether behaviorally predictive SAE features, screened for potential arti-facts, support reliable error correction under feature interventions and changes in prompt format. 

Evaluation, Artifacts, and Controls. The SAE literature increasingly stresses that feature discov-ery pipelines need rigorous controls. SAEBench and related evaluation work argue for task-specific benchmarks rather than relying only on qualita-tive feature labels (Karvonen et al., 2025; Makelov et al., 2024; Gulko et al., 2025). Sparse probing work questions when sparse features are actually useful for known downstream concepts (Kantam-neni et al., 2025; Peng et al., 2025), and work on feature explanation falsification highlights the risk that a single description can overstate what a fea-ture reliably represents (Ma et al., 2025). Our work builds on this line of work by ask-ing a narrower causal question: whether features 2associated with wrong forced-choice answers af-fect logit difference and preference between correct vs. wrong answers. Because predictive features can still be non-causal, polysemantic, or artifact-driven (Fereidouni et al., 2026; Kantamneni et al., 2025), we use ablation and steering as diagnostic tests and incorporate artifact controls before inter-preting a feature mechanistically.

4 Experiments 

4.1 Datasets and splits 

We evaluate on eight multiple-choice datasets, sum-marized in Table 1. We select 4000 random sam-ples, cap them at most 2000 characters, and convert each sample to the same lettered-choice interface. CommonSenseQA tests commonsense reason-ing among plausible alternatives (Talmor et al., 2019). TruthfulQA MC1 tests whether a model favors true answers over common misconceptions (Lin et al., 2022). MMLU covers academic and professional knowledge across different subjects (Hendrycks et al., 2021). SciQ contains science questions; we use only its questions and answer choices without the supporting passage (Welbl et al., 2017). HaluEval QA asks the model to dis-tinguish a supported answer from a hallucinated one using the supplied passage (Li et al., 2023). Wiki-FACTOR contrasts factual Wikipedia contin-uations with contradictory alternatives (Muhlgay et al., 2024). Med-HALT R-NOTA evaluates errors in medical reasoning (Pal et al., 2023). FEVER is converted to classification among Supported , Re-futed , and Not enough information , without evi-dence sentences (Thorne et al., 2018). We split original examples into discovery, valida-tion, and test partitions (approximately 60/20/20).

4.3 Models and SAEs 

We study the instruction-tuned Gemma 3 models at 1B and 4B scales (Gemma Team, 2025). For each model, we use the corresponding Gemma Scope 2 residual-stream SAEs (McDougall et al., 2025). We analyze layers 17 and 22 for Gemma 3 1B and layers 22 and 29 for Gemma 3 4B, using 16K- and 65K-width SAEs at each layer. 

4.4 Feature Selection 

We construct separate non-oracle (FP, Q, O, and GR) and oracle (C and TW) candidate pools. For each dataset, model, layer, SAE width, response mode, and position, we fit the six candidate-ranking probes from Section 3.1 on discovery instances, us-ing fixed regularization ( C = .1 for classification 5and α = 100 for regression). From each candidate-ranking probe, we retain up to 100 features ranked by absolute coefficient. A feature at rank r con-tributes Q/ log 2(r + 1) , where Q is the candidate-ranking probe’s validation quality (Appendix A). We then group records for the same model–layer– SAE-width–feature unit across targets, datasets, re-sponse modes, and positions. The aggregate score rewards repeated, directionally consistent evidence and penalizes artifact risk, dense activation, and discovery–validation gaps. We apply the eligibility criteria described in Sec-tion 3.1 to form separate non-oracle and oracle shortlists. DLA selection uses only discovery and validation instances, with the thresholds specified in Appendix B, yielding 55 non-oracle and 22 ora-cle feature units. Interventions are evaluated only on test instances.

4.6 Baselines 

We use two baselines. Random-active interven-tion controls sample five eligible features that are active on the test split for each forced-letter FP, Q, or O run. For each feature, we select up to ten test instances with the highest activation and apply the same interventions used for selected fea-tures. Also, for comparison, we adapted the Hal-luSAE baseline (Chen et al., 2026), which com-putes DLA on discovery instances for each dataset– model–SAE-width setting at FP and selects 100 features in total across the two selected layers. It then fits a class-balanced L1 logistic wrong-vs.-correct classifier, selecting its regularization by five-fold cross-validation on discovery data and reporting validation and test performance. We use layers 17/22 for 1B and 22/29 for 4B instead of reproducing HalluSAE’s Stage-I layer localization. We additionally run ten random-active selection repetitions, each containing 100 features. For the causal FP comparison, we match the number of HalluSAE and selected run-specific feature tests within each dataset–model–layer–SAE-width and apply the same interventions on test instances. 

5 Results and Discussion 

5.1 MCQA Performance 

The performance of the two Gemma models on each dataset differs substantially. We define the wrong-high-confidence (WHC) rate as the percent-age of all test instances on which the model answers incorrectly while assigning at least 0.70 probabil-ity to its strongest wrong answer. Macro-averaged across datasets, accuracy is 43 .8% for the 1B model and 58 .6% for the 4B model; the corresponding WHC rates are 52 .0% and 39 .3% . The 4B model has higher accuracy and a lower WHC rate on ev-ery dataset. Appendix Table 6 reports the complete dataset–model breakdown.

5.5 HalluSAE Baseline Comparison 

Appendix Table 13 summarizes the baseline’s pre-dictive performance, artifact audit, and overlap with our selected features. Most features retained by the adapted HalluSAE classifier do not pass our artifact-risk criterion. Of its 711 nonzero-coefficient model–layer–SAE-width–feature units, 679 ( 95 .5% ) exceed af = 0 .80 in every forced-letter FP setting in which they are selected. Only nine units remain below this threshold in every se-lected setting. The HalluSAE units also have zero overlap with either our top-300 shortlist. Thus, HalluSAE’s predictive features are largely distinct from those retained by our artifact-aware selection. Despite this difference, HalluSAE is predictive: across 32 dataset–model–width groups, it obtains mean test AUROC/F1 of .775 /. 674 , compared with 

.761 /. 659 for its random-feature controls. Appendix Table 15 reports the causal compari-son. We match 73 HalluSAE FP feature tests to our selected-feature budget within 42 dataset–model– layer–SAE-width. HalluSAE produces substan-tially larger |∆m| than selected(ours) or random FP features, with differences of +1 .606 [1 .327 , 1.902] 

and +1 .524 [1 .245 , 1.818] , respectively. How-ever, its W2C rate is not detectably higher than either baseline, and its DLA-aligned margin in-terval includes zero. HalluSAE features are also much denser: their median activation frequency is .580 , compared with about .0013 for selected and random FP features. The comparison therefore matches the feature-test budget, but not activation density. It means that HalluSAE identifies dense, artifact-prone directions with substantial logit in-fluence, but not features that correct errors more reliably. 

6 Conclusion 

We observed that across eight datasets, SAE acti-vations contain predictive information about cor-rectness, confidence, and uncertainty. Our artifact-aware pipeline identifies candidates whose Q/O interventions change correct-vs.-wrong margins more than random-active controls and correct a larger fraction of initially wrong events. Neverthe-less, answer flips are rare, and effects are not stable across equivalent prompt formats or answer orders. The selected features influence answer preference without providing dependable error repair. However, artifact entanglement remains the main issue. Although every final candidate passes our prespecified gate, 26 of 55 non-oracle units retain an artifact or semantic tag. HalluSAE is predictive and produces larger FP margin shifts, yet 95 .5% of its nonzero units fail our artifact threshold in every setting where they are selected, without improv-ing correction rates. Thus, neither prediction nor large logit shifts establish clean error mechanisms. Our findings are consistent with sparsity favoring low-dimensional surface cues while higher-level behavior remains distributed across features (Ma et al., 2026). 8Limitations 

Our results are limited to Gemma 3 1B and 4B at four mid-to-late residual layers, and 16K/65K Gemma Scope SAE based on the available pro-cessing power. Larger models may show different behavior; however, the cost of analysis and inter-vention increases substantially. Random-active controls are matched within acti-vation runs rather than exactly matched on activa-tion frequency or edit magnitude. We therefore re-port perturbation-adjusted analyses, although these do not include decoder norm, and our bootstrap intervals condition on the fixed candidate short-list. Our HalluSAE comparison applies its feature-selection method to the same analyzed layers rather than repeating its all-layer localization stage, and should therefore be viewed as a matched-layer adaptation. Finally, we focus on multiple-choice errors be-cause the constrained answer space enables precise measurement of correct-versus-wrong logits and controlled feature interventions. Extending this analysis to free-form wrong answers is an impor-tant direction for future work.

Adjustment for intervention size and context. 

Table 17 compares the unadjusted and adjusted selected–random differences in mean |∆m|. Be-cause the magnitude of an intervention depends partly on how far the feature is moved and how many tokens are edited, we perform an intervention-size adjustment. For each event, we measure the 

L2 displacement between the original and target feature activations across edited tokens, normal-ized by the feature’s activation standard deviation. A feature-equal regression then compares selected and random features while controlling for this dis-placement, span length, intervention variant, base-line margin and correctness, activation frequency, token position, dataset, and model configuration. The resulting coefficient estimates the selected– random difference in |∆m| for interventions with comparable measured characteristics.
