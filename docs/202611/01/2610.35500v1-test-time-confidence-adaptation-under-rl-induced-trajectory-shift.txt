## ABSTRACT 

Trajectory-based confidence estimation has shown promising performance for large language models (LLMs) by leveraging rich signals from their reasoning processes. However, we find that such confidence estimators can degrade substan-tially after agentic reinforcement learning (Agentic RL) as it induces substantial shifts in the reasoning trajectories. More importantly, despite these shifts, we uncover a reliability structure that is largely preserved across Base and RL mod-els: more stable trajectories remain more likely to produce correct answers .This preserved relationship provides a transferable, label-free reliability signal for post-RL confidence adaptation. Building on this finding, we propose StaR-TTA ,a stability-guided framework for label-free test-time confidence adaptation. StaR-TTA jointly learns from correctness supervision and stability-derived reliability supervision on labeled Base trajectories, establishing the connection between tra-jectory stability and answer reliability. At test time, it reuses stability-induced re-liability rankings from unlabeled RL trajectories to adapt confidence scores to the shifted trajectory distribution, without requiring target correctness labels. Across four QA benchmarks, StaR-TTA achieves an average AUROC of 0.721 , with con-sistent improvements across unseen datasets, model families and scales, and dif-ferent Agentic RL algorithms. ## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Reliable confidence estimation is essential for deploying large language models (LLMs) in real-world applications, particularly for reasoning tasks where plausible outputs can still be incorrect 1arXiv:2610.35500v1 [cs.AI] 26 Oct 2026 (Xiao et al., 2025; de Oliveira et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2023; Turpin et al., 2023; Guan et al., 2026; Xiao et al., 2026). Recent approaches increasingly estimate confidence from reasoning trajecto-ries, which reveal how a model arrives at its answer (Khanmohammadi et al., 2025; Subramani et al., 2025). Meanwhile, LLMs are increasingly optimized through agentic reinforcement learn-ing (Agentic RL), which can substantially reshape their reasoning and tool-use behavior (Cui et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026; Jin et al., 2025c; Singh et al., 2025; Matsutani et al., 2026). This raises a critical question: do confidence estimators learned from a base model remain reliable after Agentic RL? 

We find that they often do not. Confidence estimators that perform well on Base-model trajectories can degrade substantially when directly transferred to their RL-trained counterparts. Through con-trolled analysis, we identify post-RL trajectory shift as an important source of this degradation: even with tool results and final answers controlled, replacing RL trajectories with their Base counterparts recovers a substantial portion of the lost confidence-ranking performance. Retraining requires post-RL correctness labels, while direct transfer suffers from trajectory shift. We therefore ask: can a confidence estimator adapt to post-RL trajectory shift without target correctness labels? 

Our key observation is that, although Agentic RL shifts trajectories, it does not destroy all reliability structure. Across both Base and RL models, trajectory stability remains positively associated with answer correctness: answers that remain more stable under repeated sampling are consistently more likely to be correct. Crucially, unlike correctness labels, trajectory stability can be obtained directly from model outputs without human annotation. This creates a useful supervision bridge across the RL shift: correctness labels can ground the stability-reliability relationship on the source model, while stability remains available as a label-free reliability signal after RL .Building on this insight, we propose StaR-TTA , a label-free test-time confidence adaptation frame-work via Sta bility Ranking. During source training, StaR-TTA jointly learns from correctness su-pervision and stability-derived reliability supervision, explicitly aligning confidence scores with the reliability ordering induced by trajectory stability. At test time, correctness labels are unavailable, but the preserved stability–correctness relationship provides a transferable supervisory signal. StaR-TTA extracts stability from unlabeled RL trajectories, constructs reliability rankings, and uses them to adapt the confidence scorer to the shifted trajectory distribution while preserving the source-learned confidence scale. The adapted scores are then mapped to correctness probabilities by a frozen confidence calibrator. In this way, the same stability-based reliability structure learned along-side correctness on the source model becomes the supervision for label-free adaptation after RL. Extensive experiments on four QA benchmarks show that StaR-TTA consistently improves post-RL confidence estimation, achieving an average AUROC of 0.721 . Its gains persist across unseen datasets, different model families and scales, and different Agentic RL algorithms, demonstrating the strong generalization of our method. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We reveal that confidence estimation can degrade substantially after Agentic RL, and iden-tify post-RL trajectory shift as an important contributor to the failure of trajectory-based confidence estimators. • We uncover a transferable reliability structure under RL-induced trajectory shift: despite substantial changes in reasoning trajectories, the relationship between trajectory stability and answer correctness is largely preserved across Base and RL models. • We propose StaR-TTA , a label-free test-time confidence adaptation framework that learns stability-based reliability from source correctness supervision and reuses the preserved re-liability ordering to adapt confidence estimation to unlabeled post-RL trajectories. • Extensive experiments show that StaR-TTA consistently improves post-RL confidence es-timation and generalizes effectively across models, datasets, and Agentic RL algorithms. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Confidence Estimation. Confidence estimation predicts the probability that an LLM answer is cor-rect (Kadavath et al., 2022). P (True) provides a self-evaluated signal (Kadavath et al., 2022). Be-yond self-evaluation, CCPS (Khanmohammadi et al., 2025) predicts correctness from hidden-state 2perturbation features, while MICE (Subramani et al., 2025) uses logit-lens comparisons between intermediate and final predictions for tool-using agents. At the process level, HTC (Zhang et al., 2026b) estimates answer confidence for tool-using agents, whereas P (Sufficient) (Zhang et al., 2026a) probes whether accumulated multi-turn information is sufficient for answering correctly. DACA (Luo et al., 2026) instead calibrates post-trained model confidence through agreement and disagreement with its pre-trained counterpart, without correctness labels. These methods exploit hidden representations, intermediate predictions, trajectory behavior, and so on, but how reliably these signals transfer under model and trajectory distribution shifts remains an open question. 

Agentic Reinforcement Learning. Agentic reinforcement learning (Agentic RL) trains LLM poli-cies through multi-step interactions with environments, tools, or other agents, using trajectory-level rewards (Cui et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026). Common training algorithms include Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Ouyang et al., 2022; Havrilla et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Shao et al., 2024). Unlike supervised fine-tuning on fixed reasoning traces, Agentic RL lets models learn when to reason, search, verify, and use tools through inter-action. For example, Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025b) trains a base LLM to interleave reasoning with search, issue multiple queries, and incorporate retrieved evidence before answering. It uses multi-turn rollouts, retrieved-token masking, and outcome-based rewards without step-level annotations. Other work applies Agentic RL to self-reflection and verification (Guo et al., 2025), tool use (Singh et al., 2025), and search without supervised reasoning traces (Chen et al., 2025). These methods can change action patterns, tool use behaviors, reasoning structure, and hidden representations, shifting the trajectory distributions on which confidence estimators rely.

In par-ticular, the relationship between trajectory stability and answer correctness remains informative after Agentic RL. For each trajectory, we fix the prefix before final-answer generation and only sample multiple short answers without repeating interactions. We characterize their stability using three complementary signals: SGA , the fraction of sampled short answers matching the original an-swer; SEC , the concentration of the sampled short answer distribution; and SFG , the frequency gap between the two most common answers. We further consider their equal-weight average, Sraw3 .Figure 3 evaluates each stability signal as a predictor of answer correctness on both Base and RL trajectories. Across all four datasets, the stability signals achieve AUROC well above 0.5 under both Base and Agentic-RL models, while Sraw3 consistently reaches around 0.7–0.8. This predictive abil-ity is largely retained after Agentic RL despite substantial trajectory shifts. Thus, despite substantial trajectory shifts after Agentic RL, more stable trajectories remain more likely to produce correct answers. This observation provides a transferable reliability signal across the RL-induced trajectory shift. ## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

We use Qwen2.5-3B (Qwen et al., 2024) as our test model and Search-R1 (Jin et al., 2025a) as the Agentic RL framework. We evaluate effectiveness on TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017), HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018), MuSiQue (Trivedi et al., 2021), and PopQA (Mallen et al., 2023). For cross-dataset generalization, we train on TriviaQA and test on NQ, 2WikiMultiHopQA (Ho et al., 2020), and Bamboogle (Press et al., 2023). We also test different model scales and families using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Qwen et al., 2024) and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (Meta AI, 2024); and different RL al-gorithms: PPO (Havrilla et al., 2024), GRPO (Guo et al., 2025). We compare with five baselines: CCPS (Khanmohammadi et al., 2025), MICE (Subramani et al., 2025), HTC (Zhang et al., 2026b), P(Sufficient) (Zhang et al., 2026a), and DACA (Luo et al., 2026). MICE-LR and MICE-RF share internal features but use logistic regression and random forests, respectively. We report AUROC for ranking, ECE for calibration, and Brier score and NLL for probability quality (Fawcett, 2005; Guo et al., 2017; Brier, 1950; Gneiting & Raftery, 2007). We set λa = 0 .1, λh = 0 .5, λr = 1 .0, and 

λs = 0 .5 (details can be accessed in Appendix A.3.1 and Appendix A.3.2). 5.2 MAIN RESULTS

5.3 GENERALIZATION ANALYSIS 

5.3.1 GENERALIZATION ACROSS DATASETS 

We evaluate cross-dataset generalization in Table 4. We train the estimator on Base-LLM trajecto-ries from TriviaQA and evaluate it on Agentic RL trajectories from 2WikiMultiHopQA, Bamboogle, and NQ, with TriviaQA serving as an in-domain reference. Our method achieves the highest AU-ROC and the lowest Brier score and NLL on all four datasets. It also achieves the lowest ECE on Bamboogle and NQ. These results demonstrate strong cross-dataset generalization. 5.3.2 GENERALIZATION ACROSS MODELS 

We evaluate generalization across different model families using Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (Table 5). We retrain the confidence estimator on the new model, while keeping all other configurations fixed. On Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, we achieve the best AUROC, Brier score, and NLL on TriviaQA, Hot-potQA, MuSiQue, and PopQA, with AUROC gains of 5.6–7.8 percentage points over the strongest baseline. We also evaluate generalization across model scales using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Table 6) to test our metod. Under the 7B backbone, StaR-TTA achieves the highest AUROC on all four datasets, exceeding the strongest baseline by 1.1–10.2 percentage points, and obtains the lowest Brier score and NLL. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of our method across LLM scales and families. 5.3.3 GENERALIZATION ACROSS RL A LGORITHMS 

Finally, we verify that our findings are not specific to the PPO algorithm used for Agentic RL training. Table 7 reports results on GRPO algorithms, while keeping the same Base LLM and evaluation protocol. We achieve the best AUROC on all four datasets ( 0.756 on TriviaQA, 0.724 

on HotpotQA, 0.676 on MuSiQue, and 0.750 on PopQA), improving over the strongest baseline by 3.4 to 9.2 points, and additionally attain the best Brier and NLL on HotpotQA, MuSiQue, and PopQA. These results confirm that trajectory shift is a general phenomenon of RL training and that trajectory-based confidence estimation consistently benefits across different RL algorithms. 5.4 ABLATION STUDIES

We studied why confidence estimators trained on Base LLM trajectories degrade after Agentic RL. We find that RL-induced trajectory shifts account for a substantial fraction of the degradation. De-spite this shift, trajectory stability remains predictive of answer correctness in both Base and RL models. This preserved relationship motivates StaR-TTA, a label-free method that adjusts confi-dence scores on Agentic RL trajectories at test time. Across four datasets, StaR-TTA achieves an average AUROC of 0.721 . It also generalizes across datasets, model families, and PPO- or GRPO-trained agents.
