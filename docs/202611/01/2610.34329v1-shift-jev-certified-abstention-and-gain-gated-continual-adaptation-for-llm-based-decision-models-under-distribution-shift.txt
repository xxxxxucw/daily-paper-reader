Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) can act as decision models that return a probability distribution over a fixed set of options, but deployed decision models face domain, label-prior, emerging-class and candidate-set shift. We study how such models fail under shift and propose S HIFT -J EV , a monitoring, abstention and continual-adaptation procedure for them. It scores the risk of each decision from uncertainty, format and option-consistency signals, certifies abstention thresholds with Learn-then-Test, monitors the decision stream with drift and label-shift detectors, and accepts a model update only when an expected-gain gate finds a significant held-out improvement without loss on earlier regimes. On a benchmark of ten shifted test environments with Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen3-4B (three seeds each) and Phi-4-mini, fine-tuning with KL anchors and temperature scaling lowers calibration error under shift from 0.289 to 0.087. At a target risk of 10%, a learned uncertainty score with a certified threshold exceeds the target in 3–5 of 27 environment–seed tests, against 5–15 for a max-probability score with the same certification and 9–14 for conformal prediction sets; drift-based routing changes little. A predicted-label χ2 test detects 95–99% of class-prior shifts that confidence- and embedding-based detectors miss. In continual adaptation S HIFT -J EV reaches 70.5% accuracy on Qwen3-0.6B, above replay, LwF, 1arXiv:2610.34329v1 [cs.LG] 25 Oct 2026 SHIFT -J EV : S HIFT -A WARE ABSTENTION AND ADAPTATION A P REPRINT

1 Introduction 

Many software systems choose among a small set of predefined options: they route a support ticket, identify the intent of a customer message, check a document against a rule or grade a model response. General chat LLMs can perform these tasks, but their free-form answers must be parsed, they are slow to generate and they carry no usable confidence. Decision models such as Jev [1] instead return a probability distribution over the candidate options in a single call, which downstream code consumes directly. Such models have recently been used as cheap first-stage judges [2, 3], as detectors of alignment failures [4], for social-science annotation [5] and for coding administrative records [6]. Li and Wagle [7] showed that an ordinary instruction-tuned LLM already behaves as a decision model (LLM-as-Jev): listing the options with bracketed numbers, pre-filling “ Best answer: [” and scoring each option identifier through a prefix-free trie yields calibrated decisions without training, and a tree-factorized loss with KL anchors fine-tunes the model without damaging its chat behaviour. These evaluations use test data that resembles the calibration data. Deployment does not. A routing model meets new products and phrasing ( domain shift ), changing frequencies of the correct options ( class-prior shift ), requests that no offered option covers ( emerging classes ), and option lists that are reordered, reworded, extended or missing the right answer ( candidate-set shift ). Because software acts on the returned probabilities automatically, a model that is confidently wrong under shift is more harmful than one that fails visibly. LLM judges are over-confident [8], conformal guarantees for LLMs fail under dataset shift unless re-verified [9, 10], risk controlled over a population can exceed its budget within groups [11, 12], and sequential fine-tuning can collapse coverage before it lowers accuracy [13]. No existing work studies how LLM decision models behave under these shifts or how to keep their errors within a budget. We address three research questions. RQ1 : how do the accuracy and calibration of frozen and fine-tuned LLM decision models degrade under the four shift types? RQ2 : can abstention keep the realized error rate of answered inputs below a target α under shift, and which signals and threshold rules achieve this? RQ3 :when labelled data from a new regime arrive, can the model adapt without forgetting earlier regimes while declining updates that do not help? We answer them with S HIFT -J EV (Fig. 1), which adds three modules to the LLM-as-Jev interface. Module A monitors each decision and the decision stream. Module B abstains when a learned risk score exceeds a threshold certified by Learn-then-Test; drift distances are used to route inputs to a threshold but never enter the score. Module C adapts the LoRA adapter to a new regime and keeps the update only when it measurably helps. This paper makes three contributions. First, it introduces a shift benchmark for LLM decision models with one in-distribution and ten shifted test environments covering all four shift types, seven external benchmarks at the base paper’s sample sizes, stream protocols at three severities and a four-stage continual protocol that includes a no-shift control stage. Second, it proposes a monitoring, certified-abstention and gain-gated adaptation procedure and isolates which of its parts matter: the risk score and the data used for certification matter, drift-based routing does not, label-shift monitoring is essential, and the accuracy gains of adaptation come mainly from replay and severity-scaled anchoring, with the gate acting chiefly as a safeguard against 2SHIFT -J EV : S HIFT -A WARE ABSTENTION AND ADAPTATION A P REPRINT

Third, it reports a comparison with six abstention rules, five continual-learning baselines and a range of ablations over two Qwen3 sizes with three seeds each and a second model family, using statistics that do not treat repeated evaluations of the same items as independent, and it states where S HIFT -J EV does not help.

2 Related Work 

Decision models and LLM judges. Jev-style decision models [1] return calibrated probabilities over typed options and are ranked on JevBench [14]. Li and Wagle [7] extract the same behaviour from open LLMs with an architecture-preserving answer interface, a training-free trie-scoring recipe and a tree-factorized listwise fine-tuning loss with KL anchors on legal-token mass, illegal-token distribution and context positions. Work on LLM-as-a-judge [15] has shown that judgment distributions carry more information than greedy verdicts [16], that judges are over-confident [8], biased [17] and benefit from distribution-matching training [18] or reasoning [19], and that reported judge accuracies require bias correction [20] and reproducible protocols [21]. Confidence routing between a cheap decision model and an expensive judge is effective in-distribution [2, 22], but Rao and Callison-Burch [3] find that decision models and LLM judges err on the same items, which limits cascades, and Li et al. [2] report that routing weakens on style-adversarial inputs. Multiple-choice selection by LLMs is sensitive to option order and identifiers [23–25], which motivates our candidate-set shifts and rotation-consistency signal. None of these works studies decision models under distribution shift or provides risk guarantees. 3SHIFT -J EV : S HIFT -A WARE ABSTENTION AND ADAPTATION A P REPRINT 

Uncertainty and calibration of LLMs. Temperature scaling [26] remains a strong post-hoc calibrator for pre-trained transformers [27]. LLM uncertainty can be read from token probabilities [28], elicited verbally [29] or estimated from semantic dispersion [30]; for decision models the categorical output makes probability-based scores natural. UniCR [31] fuses heterogeneous evidence into a calibrated correctness probability and enforces an error budget by refusal, and robust-satisficing aggregation [32] stabilizes multi-LLM decisions under shift. Calibration is known to deteriorate under dataset shift [33]. 

Selective prediction and conformal risk control. Selective classification trades coverage for risk [34, 35], with the maximum softmax probability as the classical score [36]. Conformal prediction [37, 38] and its risk-controlling extensions—RCPS [39], conformal risk control [40] and Learn-then-Test (LTT) [41]—give finite-sample guarantees under exchangeability, and Mondrian (group-conditional) variants give per-group guarantees [37]. For LLMs these tools have been applied to multiple-choice QA [42], generation [43] and factuality [44]. Very recent work studies when conformal risk control can certify structured LLM outputs and shows that guarantees fail under dataset shift unless re-verified [9], proposes similarity-weighted calibration for domain shift [10], and enforces risk per group hierarchy [11] or per argument role [12]. Beyond exchangeability, weighted [45], adaptive [46] and label-shift-aware [47] conformal methods exist, and online PAC-Bayes certificates have been proposed for forecasting under shift [48]. S HIFT -J EV is closest to group-conditional risk control: it certifies one LTT threshold per known regime, routes inputs to regimes with an option-invariant drift monitor, and falls back to a conservative threshold for unknown inputs and after stream alarms. 

Shift and out-of-distribution detection. Dataset-shift detection compares feature or prediction distributions with two-sample tests [49]; BBSE [50] detects and corrects label shift from a black-box predictor’s confusion matrix. Embedding-based OOD scores include Mahalanobis [51], relative Mahalanobis [52], deep k-NN [53] and energy [54]; sequential change detection uses CUSUM [55]. Concept drift in streams [56] and zero-day settings where test data come from unseen distributions [57] motivate continuous monitoring, and LLM-mediated continuous adaptation has been proposed for anomaly detection streams [58]. WILDS [59] benchmarks in-the-wild shifts but not candidate-set shift, which is specific to decision models. 

Continual learning for LLMs. Classical remedies for catastrophic forgetting are regularization (EWC [60]), distillation (LwF [61]) and replay [62–64]; see Wang et al. [65] and, for LLMs, Shi et al. [66]. LLMs forget during continual fine-tuning [67], although LoRA forgets less than full fine-tuning [68]. Recent work schedules replay by parameter change [69] and shows that conformal coverage collapses before accuracy under sequential fine-tuning, fixable by per-task recalibration [13]—an observation that our per-regime recertification (B6) also exploits. The KL anchors of LLM-as-Jev [7] act as a stability regularizer; we extend them to previous-stage anchors and couple their strength to measured shift severity. 3.1 Problem setting

r(x) ≤ τ . On a data distribution D its coverage is the fraction of answered inputs and its selective risk is the error rate among them,

S HIFT -J EV aims to keep RDt (τ ) ≤ α with high probability at the highest possible coverage, and to update the model only when the update improves accuracy on the new regime without degrading earlier ones. 

3.2 Decision model 

We follow Li and Wagle [7]. Options are listed as [1] , . . . , [K] in the user turn and the assistant turn is pre-filled with “ Best answer: [”. The score of option k is the log-probability of its identifier suffix 

ck = “k] ”, 

sk = log pθ(ck | x), P (k | x) = softmax k(sk/T ), (2) where T is a temperature fitted on ID validation data. Because the suffixes form a prefix-free trie, all options are scored in one forward pass: we pack the trie nodes after the prompt with a tree-shaped attention mask so that each node attends only to the prompt and its own ancestors, which gives exactly the same scores as scoring each option separately. For fine-tuning we use the base paper’s tree-factorized cross-entropy over the legal identifier tokens, plus KL anchors that tie the next-token distribution at the reply start and after the answer to the frozen model, which preserves normal text generation. Adapters are LoRA [70] modules with rank 16 on all linear layers. 

3.3 Module A: monitoring 

Module A computes four groups of signals for every decision without extra forward passes, except for the consistency check. Uncertainty is described by the normalized entropy, the maximum probability, the margin between the two most likely options and the number of options. Format is the log of the probability mass that the model places on legal identifier tokens at the trie root; low mass indicates that the model would rather produce something other than an option. Consistency compares the decision distribution with the one obtained after cyclically rotating the options, using the total-variation distance and agreement of the two argmax decisions; because it doubles scoring cost, we use it only for the 0.6B model. Input drift is the deep 

k-NN distance [53] between the input’s state embedding and a reference pool of ID inputs. The embedding is the mean final-layer hidden state over the tokens of the state text only, so it does not change when the options change. Module A also monitors the stream of decisions with three detectors: CUSUM [55] on standardized per-decision signals, a windowed Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and a black-box label-shift test in the spirit of BBSE [50], which compares the histogram of predicted labels in a 50-item window with its reference frequencies by a χ2 test. Every alarm level is set by simulation so that at most 5% of no-shift streams raise a false alarm. 5SHIFT -J EV : S HIFT -A WARE ABSTENTION AND ADAPTATION A P REPRINT 

3.4 Module B: certified, regime-conditional abstention 

The risk score r(x) is a logistic regression on the uncertainty, format and consistency signals, trained to predict errors on ID calibration data and on two proxy shifts (SciQ and Emotion) that are disjoint from all test shifts. Each calibration set is split once into two halves: the risk model is fitted, and candidate thresholds are proposed, on the first half only, and thresholds are certified on the second half only. Temperature scaling and the drift reference use separate ID validation data. Drift distances are deliberately excluded from r(x). A risk model fitted on two proxy shifts learns that “far from the reference data” means “likely wrong”, which holds for some shifts, such as intent sets with many options, and fails for others, such as an easy emerging-class set or knowledge questions that resemble ID data but are answered wrongly with high confidence. Drift instead tells the system which population an input comes from, so S HIFT -J EV uses it for routing. Each known regime g has its own reference pool and threshold 

τg. An input is assigned to the regime whose normalized k-NN distance is smallest, provided this distance is below the regime’s 95th percentile; otherwise it is unknown . Unknown inputs, and all inputs after a stream alarm, use the conservative threshold τshift , the smaller of the thresholds certified on ID and on proxy-shift data, so a detected shift can only make the model more cautious. An earlier design that placed drift inside 

r(x) is kept as an ablation. Thresholds are certified by fixed-sequence Learn-then-Test (LTT) [41]. Candidate thresholds are proposed on the first calibration half, ordered from strict to permissive, and tested in that order on the second half: a threshold passes if the one-sided Clopper–Pearson [71] upper bound on its selective risk, at confidence 

1 − δ with δ = 0 .1, is below α. Testing stops at the first failure and the last passing threshold is used, or the model abstains on everything if none passes. Because the risk model and the candidate thresholds depend only on the first half, the second half is used for a single test, and if calibration and test data of a regime are exchangeable the procedure guarantees RDg (τg) ≤ α with probability at least 1 − δ (Appendix B). No such guarantee is possible for unseen shifts, so for them we report empirical violation rates. 

3.5 Module C: gain-gated continual adaptation 

When a labelled batch from a new regime arrives, Module C first measures the shift. A binomial test checks whether more new inputs than expected lie beyond the reference 95th-percentile drift distance, and a Fisher exact test checks whether accuracy on the new data is lower than on the reference data. Their effect sizes give a severity ˆs ∈ [0 , 1] .The adapter is then trained for one epoch on the new data, with replay of buffered examples from earlier regimes. The buffer keeps, per regime, correctly classified examples with the smallest decision margin together with random examples. Two terms protect earlier behaviour. Tree-local distillation keeps the previous model’s option distributions on buffered examples [61, 72], and the LLM-as-Jev KL anchors are computed against the previous-stage model instead of the original one. Severity sets the stability–plasticity balance: the anchor weight decreases as λKL = λ0e−κˆs with κ = 1 .5, while the distillation weight increases with ˆs, so that larger shifts allow more change on new inputs and more protection of old decisions. The update is then judged by an expected-gain gate , which asks whether the update makes the model better rather than whether the model has become worse. Halfway through training, a probe compares the checkpoint with the current model on held-out new-regime items. If no shift was detected and the checkpoint wins no more items than it loses, training stops. Otherwise the half-trained and fully trained candidates are each accepted only if a one-sided sign test on paired held-out correctness against the current model gives p < 0.10 

and their accuracy on buffered old-regime items drops by at most 5 points. The accepted candidate with the best combined new- and old-regime accuracy is deployed. If neither is accepted, the update is rolled back. In both cases the regime joins the replay buffer and the reference pools and receives its own certified 6SHIFT -J EV : S HIFT -A WARE ABSTENTION AND ADAPTATION A P REPRINT

3.6 Compared systems 

We compare six systems. B1 is the frozen LLM-as-Jev model and B2 is B1 fine-tuned on an ID mixture. B3 

adds temperature scaling [26] to B2. B4 adds an uncertainty-only risk score with a single LTT threshold certified on pooled ID and proxy data. B5 is the full S HIFT -J EV reliability layer with routing and per-regime thresholds, and B6 is B5 after continual adaptation and per-regime recertification. The abstention baselines are the max-probability (MSP) score [36] and the energy score [54], both with the same pooled LTT certification as B4, and split-conformal LAC prediction sets [37], calibrated on all ID and proxy calibration data, that answer only when the set contains a single option. LAC guarantees marginal coverage of the prediction set rather than selective risk. The continual-learning baselines are naive sequential fine-tuning, EWC [60], sequential fine-tuning with the base paper’s KL anchors to the original model (Seq-FT+KL), experience replay [62] and LwF [61]. All baselines start from the same B2 adapter and use the same data, steps and LoRA capacity.

5.2 RQ1: decision models under shift 

Domain shift to intent sets with many options is the most damaging shift (Tables 4 and 5, Fig. 3). Frozen Qwen3-0.6B falls from 73.3% ID accuracy to 18.4% on Banking77 and 27.6% on Twitter-Fin while remaining confident (ECE 0.47 and 0.58), and Qwen3-4B falls from 91.1% to 55.4% and 60.2%. Class-prior shift does not reduce accuracy at either scale (AG News is easier than the ID average), and emerging classes cost 4–5 points because “none of the above” is often chosen correctly. Candidate changes that leave the task unchanged still hurt the small model: with an added near-duplicate distractor, Qwen3-0.6B reaches 59.3% against 73.3% on ID data, while Qwen3-4B reaches 89.7% against 91.1%. Fine-tuning with KL anchors and temperature scaling (B3) reduces the shift-averaged ECE from 0.289 to 0.087 for Qwen3-0.6B and from 0.182 to 0.078 for Qwen3-4B, and raises the 0.6B model’s shift accuracy from 57.5% to 64.2%. A plausible reason is that one threshold on the maximum probability does not transfer across tasks with very different numbers of options, whereas the learned score also uses the margin and the number of options. Split-conformal LAC sets answer the most inputs (53.6% and 73.8%), but at realized risks of 24.5% and 12.1%, and violate 14 and 9 tests, which is expected because they control marginal coverage rather than selective risk. Adding the format and consistency signals to the learned score does not reduce violations under a pooled threshold (8 and 3 tests at α = 0 .10 ). Drift-based routing has little effect (Table 8). 5.4 Shift detection in decision streams 

Domain shift and emerging classes are easy to detect (Table 10, Fig. 6): CUSUM on the drift distance or on the S HIFT -J EV risk score detects 97–100% of full-severity streams within 2–13 items. Class-prior shift is invisible to all confidence- and embedding-based signals (0–1% detection), because each input still looks like an ID input and the model remains equally confident. Only the predicted-label χ2 test detects it, in 95% (Qwen3-0.6B) and 99% (Qwen3-4B) of full-severity streams and 80% and 86% at 50% severity, after about 36 items. It is also the best detector of candidate perturbations (70% and 64%). 5.6 Scale and model family 

Training-free Qwen3-4B-Instruct reaches a six-task external macro accuracy of 75.76, against 78.58 for the base paper’s different Qwen3.5-4B checkpoint, and fine-tuning leaves it unchanged (75.71) while halving its ECE (0.220 to 0.105; Table 18, Fig. 10). For the frozen Phi-4-mini (one run, Table 19), the MSP score with a pooled threshold has no violations at α = 0 .10 and four in total. The S HIFT -J EV layer has three in total but certifies no threshold at α = 0 .05 , has a higher realized risk at α = 0 .10 than the uncertainty score (7.9% against 5.2%), a worse AURC (0.160 against 0.129) and lower abstention on removed-answer items (14% against 25%). Without fine-tuning and without the consistency signal, the format signal of a different model family adds noise to the risk model. On the frozen Qwen models the S HIFT -J EV layer certifies no threshold at α ≤ 0.10 , whereas the MSP score with a pooled threshold answers 24.4% (0.6B) and 52.6% (4B) of shifted inputs at α = 0 .10 , with 7 and 0 violations. The learned risk models are therefore most useful on top of the fine-tuned and temperature-scaled model (B3), and a plain MSP score remains a reasonable choice for an unadapted backbone.

6 Discussion 

6.1 Implications for deploying decision models 

Under shift, both the risk score and the data used to certify its threshold determine whether selective risk stays within budget. All scores ranked errors almost identically, yet with the same pooled certification the MSP score violated α = 0 .10 three times as often as a learned uncertainty score on Qwen3-0.6B, and split-conformal prediction sets, which guarantee marginal coverage rather than selective risk, violated it in a third to a half of the tests. This agrees with reports that LLM risk guarantees must be re-verified for each deployment [9] and enforced per group [11, 12]. No rule eliminated violations at a useful coverage: at α = 0 .10 the best rules still violated 2–3 of 27 tests. Evaluations of decision models should therefore report realized selective risk and coverage under shift, not only calibration error, and should report coverage together with violation counts, since a rule that abstains on everything has no violations. 20 SHIFT -J EV : S HIFT -A WARE ABSTENTION AND ADAPTATION A P REPRINT 

Drift tells the system which population an input belongs to, but in our experiments routing inputs to population-specific thresholds added little, because the proxy-shift threshold was rarely stricter than the ID threshold. A single conservative threshold, the stricter of those certified on ID and on proxy-shift data, gave the fewest violations; this post-hoc finding needs confirmation on further shifts. Drift is more useful for monitoring the stream, and label-shift monitoring should run by default: class-prior shift changes neither the inputs nor the model’s confidence, so every confidence- and embedding-based detector missed it, while a χ2 test on predicted labels, which needs nothing beyond the decisions themselves, detected 95–99% of shifted streams. For continual adaptation, replay of boundary examples and severity-scaled anchoring to the previous model produced most of the accuracy gain on Qwen3-0.6B. The expected-gain gate added about one point on average, but it rejected every no-shift update, turned forgetting negative and, unlike a gate that tests for degradation, did not skip a regime on which the model could improve. In practice, we recommend fine-tuning with KL anchors followed by temperature scaling, which lowers calibration error under shift two- to three-fold; certifying a learned uncertainty score with LTT on ID and proxy-shift data separately and applying the stricter threshold; running a predicted-label χ2 test next to a drift CUSUM; and gating updates on held-out gain with held-out and recertification sets larger than the 80 and 160 items used here when the backbone is strong. 

6.2 Limitations and threats to validity 

Several results are negative or weaker than the design intended. First, at α = 0 .05 the ID-only certification of B5 failed in five of six Qwen runs and the model abstained on everything. A 400-item test half is too small to certify a 5% risk unless the answered inputs are almost error-free, and the fixed-sequence order, which tests the strictest candidate first and stops at the first failure, discarded passing thresholds in one run. Larger calibration sets, or an ordering of candidates chosen on the first calibration half to maximize the chance of passing, would address this without affecting validity; we did not evaluate these changes. Second, routing by drift had little effect, as discussed above. Third, on Qwen3-4B the gate rejected a Twitter-Fin gain of 2.5–7.5 points that 80 paired items cannot confirm at p < 0.10 ; larger held-out sets or a sequential test would recover such gains. Fourth, abstention on items whose correct option was removed varied between 14% and 72% across seeds on Qwen3-4B, so a dedicated “none of the above” mechanism may be needed. Fifth, for the frozen Phi-4-mini the additional signals increased realized risk, so feature-rich risk models should be validated for each backbone. The statistical evidence is limited by three seeds per backbone and by evaluation items that are shared across seeds. We therefore avoided pooling seeds as independent pairs, but seed-level tests have little power, and violation counts over (environment, seed) tests are descriptive. The routing analysis in Table 8 re-certifies thresholds offline from the stored calibration splits of the same runs and is post hoc. The 4B comparisons with the base paper use a different checkpoint, and our fine-tuning mixture deliberately differs from the base paper’s so that the test shifts remain unseen. Class-prior and candidate-set shifts are synthetic, although parameterized by severity, and JevBench [14] was not available in our environment. The LTT and sign-test guarantees hold within a regime under exchangeability; for unseen shifts our violation counts are empirical. The present design was reached after earlier runs on the same benchmark exposed two failures, a gate that tested for degradation and drift features inside the risk score. All numbers reported here come from new runs with new seeds for the 4B model, new adapters and larger shift test sets, and the earlier designs are reported as ablations. Finally, the study covers two model families and models of up to 4B parameters on single-GPU budgets. 21 SHIFT -J EV : S HIFT -A WARE ABSTENTION AND ADAPTATION A P REPRINT 

7 Conclusion 

LLMs can serve as decision models, but deployment shifts their domain, label priors, label space and candidate sets. We evaluated such models on a benchmark of ten shifted environments and proposed S HIFT -J EV , which adds risk scoring with certified abstention, stream monitoring and gain-gated continual adaptation to the LLM-as-Jev interface. Fine-tuning with KL anchors and temperature scaling reduced calibration error under shift two- to three-fold. With the same certification, a learned uncertainty score kept selective risk within budget far more often than a max-probability score or conformal prediction sets, while drift-based routing added little and a single conservative threshold did best. A predicted-label χ2 test detected class-prior shifts that all other detectors missed. On Qwen3-0.6B, S HIFT -J EV was the most accurate continual learner in every seed, with negative forgetting and correct accept and reject decisions, and most of its gain came from replay and severity-scaled anchoring. On Qwen3-4B its gate was too conservative for the small remaining gains. Future work will repair the LTT ladder at strict risk levels, study sequential gain tests, larger per-regime calibration sets, label-shift-aware threshold correction [47], more model families and naturally occurring shifts in production decision streams.

A Implementation Details 

Answer interface and scoring. We use each model’s chat template with thinking disabled (Qwen3). The user message lists the state followed by “ [1] o1 . . . [K] oK ” and the instruction to answer with the bracketed identifier only, and the assistant turn is pre-filled with “ Best answer: [”. Identifier suffixes are taken from the tokenization of the full string, so multi-digit identifiers and merged bracket tokens are handled exactly, and the prefix-free property is checked for every item. For packed scoring, all trie nodes are appended after the prompt in one sequence; a token of a node attends to the whole prompt, to its ancestors and causally to itself, and position ids continue along each root-to-node path. When a model is loaded, packed and unpacked scores are compared on four items, and the packed path is used only if the maximum probability difference is below 10 −4 (fp32) or 0.03 (fp16); the observed difference was at most 2 × 10 −5.

Fine-tuning objective. The B2 loss is the sum of the cross-entropy over legal child tokens at each node of the correct option’s trie path and three KL anchors with weight 1: on the probability mass of legal identifier tokens, on the distribution of illegal tokens, and on the full next-token distribution at the reply start and after the answer. Anchors are computed against the frozen model on correct-path and sampled off-path nodes with top-64 plus tail truncation. 

Precision and hardware. On T4 GPUs Qwen3-0.6B runs in fp32 because fp16 overflows in its attention, and the 4B and Phi models run in fp16 with fp32 LoRA weights. Each part of the study runs two lanes in parallel on the two T4 GPUs of one Kaggle session with a shared time budget, and results are merged afterwards. 

Monitor and detectors. The drift model standardizes the state embeddings of 400 ID validation items, projects them to 64 principal components and uses a cosine k-NN index with k = 10 ; Mahalanobis [51] and relative-Mahalanobis [52] distances with a Ledoit–Wolf covariance are computed for ablations. CUSUM uses a drift allowance of 0.5 on signals standardized by the reference pool, with the alarm level at the 95th percentile of no-shift maxima over 200 simulated streams. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test uses windows of 25 items with step 5, and the χ2 test uses windows of 50 items over the predicted-category histogram with an “unseen” bin and add-0.5 smoothing. 

Risk model and certification. Features are standardized and the logistic regression uses C = 1 . Calibration sets are split 50/50 with a fixed seed. Candidate thresholds are the largest thresholds whose empirical selective risk on the first half is at most cα for c ∈ { 0.2, 0.35 , 0.5, 0.65 , 0.8, 0.9, 1.0, 1.1}. The Clopper–Pearson bound is the 1 − δ quantile of Beta( e + 1 , n − e) for e errors among n answered points. Split-conformal LAC uses the non-conformity score 1 − P (y | x) on pooled calibration data and the ⌈(n + 1)(1 − α)⌉/n empirical quantile. 

Module C. Shift measurement uses 250 incoming items and a reference pool of 200 ID validation items, extended by 100 items per adapted regime; a shift is validated if either test gives p < 0.01 with an effect of at least 5 points. The buffer stores 60 items per regime (half lowest-margin correct, half random) and replay adds 30% of the new-data count. The gate uses 80 held-out new-regime items and the buffer’s validation items (40 ID and 20 per adapted regime). EWC uses an online diagonal Fisher estimate over 100 examples with decay 0.9 and weight 50, and LwF uses tree-local distillation to the previous-stage model on new data with weight 1. 29 SHIFT -J EV : S HIFT -A WARE ABSTENTION AND ADAPTATION A P REPRINT

Proposition 1 (Per-regime validity) . If the calibration points of regime g and a new test point from g are exchangeable, and the risk model and the candidate thresholds depend only on the first calibration half, the fixed-sequence procedure of Section 3.4 returns τg with Pr[ RDg (τg) ≤ α] ≥ 1 − δ.Proof sketch. For each candidate threshold, the null hypothesis that its selective risk exceeds α is rejected only if the Clopper–Pearson bound, a valid level-δ test given the number of answered points, falls below α.The order of the hypotheses is fixed before the second half is seen and testing stops at the first non-rejection, so the family-wise error rate is at most δ without multiplicity correction [41]. A violation requires a false rejection, which has probability at most δ.

Proposition 2 (Controlled false acceptance) . If a candidate update is no more likely to be right where the current model is wrong than the reverse, the gate accepts it with probability at most γ = 0 .10 ; with two candidates, the probability of accepting any such update is at most 2γ.Proof sketch. Given the number of discordant held-out pairs, the candidate’s number of wins is stochastically no larger than a Binomial (n, 12 ) variable under the null hypothesis, so the one-sided exact sign test has level 

γ. A union bound covers the two candidates, and the retention condition can only remove acceptances.
