Abstract 

In eXplainable AI, counterfactual explanations have gained significant traction by offering users direct, intuitive, and actionable insights into model decisions. We introduce CounterFlow, a novel framework based on Conditional Flow Matching that generates visual counterfactual explanations by directly steering a source image toward a user-specified target class. By leveraging target-conditioned vector fields, CounterFlow identifies highly realistic semantic modifications while re-quiring substantially lower computational overhead than traditional GAN- or diffusion-based ar-chitectures. Empirical evaluations on standard benchmarks demonstrate that CounterFlow achieves state-of-the-art performance in both visual realism and target class switching. By working directly in the raw pixel space, CounterFlow offers a more practical solution for real-world deployment. 

# 1 Introduction 

Context. On the one hand, in generative modeling, diffusion models currently dominate the landscape for high-quality image, video, and audio synthesis (Rombach et al., 2022; Ho et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023a). However, they are also well-known for their slow training times and their stochastic sampling paths. To address these limitations, Lipman et al. (2023); Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden (2023); Liu et al. (2023b) introduced Conditional Flow Matching (CFM). CFM is a generative modeling frame-work based on Continuous Normalizing Flows (CNFs) that uses standard regression objectives for training. Moreover, instead of relying on diffusion processes, CFM generates examples by integrating the CNF’s ODE along deterministic trajectories. On the other hand, the generative models’ ability to generate complex data distributions and thus to produce realistic examples is particularly appealing for eXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI, Dwivedi et al., 2023), where one aims to produce counter-factual explanations (Wachter et al., 2017), i.e. , examples (i) close to a given input, (ii) plausible, and 

(iii) that change the model’s prediction. Unlike adversarial examples that rely on imperceptible noise to fool the classifier, counterfactual examples expose meaningful changes, helping users to trust the model while enabling developers to debug it. Our work leverages CFM to create counterfactual explanations: rather than generating images from random noise, our approach transports an input sample toward a counterfactual conditioned on a user-defined target class, while jointly optimizing for plausibility and proximity; see Figure 1. 

Related Work. Prior work on image counterfactual explanations falls into two categories: instance-based and generative approaches. Instance-based methods (see e.g. , Goyal et al., 2019; Keane and Smyth, 2020) select examples from the training set using similarity metrics to modify the input sample

We focus instead on generative approaches, a more recent line of work (Khorram and Fuxin, 2022; Jeanneret et al., 2024; Weng et al., 2024; Augustin et al., 2022; Cao et al., 2025) that produces more plausible examples, which we group by generative backbone: GANs, diffusion models, or CFM. • GAN. C3LT (Khorram and Fuxin, 2022) encodes the input (with a VAE encoder) into a latent space, in which it learns two cycle-consistent transformations per pair of classes (y, y ′) before decoding with the generator of a GAN. It thus requires one model set per pair, impractical for many classes, and its fidelity is bounded by the VAE’s reconstruction quality. • Diffusion. DiME (Jeanneret et al., 2024) generates a counterfactual by corrupting the input and denoising it through the backward diffusion process (an inner loop of several steps); at each outer step, the classifier gradient computed on this denoised estimate refines the corrupted instance, and the process repeats until it converges to a valid counterfactual. FastDiME (Weng et al., 2024) instead computes this gradient directly from the model’s one-step denoised estimate, bypassing the inner loop entirely. ACE (Jeanneret et al., 2023) takes a different route: it builds an adversarial attack against the classifier and lets the diffusion model’s own denoising process project this attack back onto the man-ifold of realistic images, turning an imperceptible perturbation into a semantically meaningful one. DVCE (Augustin et al., 2022) starts the reverse diffusion from a partially noised version of the input, rather than pure noise, and steers the process by projecting the gradient of an adversarially robust classifier onto a cone around the target classifier’s own gradient direction whenever the two disagree, avoiding non-semantic changes. More recently, MaskDiME (Guo et al., 2026) improves on DiME’s efficiency and spatial precision with a training-free, adaptive dual-mask mechanism guided by clas-sifier gradients that restricts updates to a localized region rather than the whole image. Regardless of the specific guidance scheme, diffusion-guidance methods must reapply their guidance at every one of many denoising steps, making inference slow: DiME’s inner diffusion loop scales quadratically with the number of diffusion steps, and even FastDiME’s one-step approximation, despite yielding a roughly 20 × empirical speedup, only reduces this to a linear scaling and remains markedly slower than a single forward pass. ACE and DVCE compound this same per-step cost further, respectively adding an adversarial attack and a robust-classifier gradient computation at every step. • CFM. LeapFactual (Cao et al., 2025) builds on Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) to generate coun-terfactuals. It learns a class-conditional generative flow from a Gaussian prior to the latent space of a pretrained VAE, and repurposes this flow for counterfactual generation through a lift-and-land 

2procedure. Given an input, its VAE encoding is first lifted toward the prior under the input class by solving the learned ODE backward, and then landed back into the latent space under the target class by solving the ODE forward. This “leap” must be repeated a dataset-dependent number of times, with each leap requiring a VAE decoding and a classifier query. Our work also builds on CFM, but uses it in a fundamentally different way. We directly learn a transport from an input image to its counterfactual. This avoids (i) the need for multiple costly leaps, (ii) the intermediate traversal through a prior space, and (iii) VAE encoding and decoding, whose reconstruction fidelity can limit the quality of the generated counterfactuals. 

Our contributions. To address the drawbacks of the previous methods, our contributions are as follows: • We introduce CounterFlow, the first CFM framework that directly conditions the vector field on both the source and target class, enabling a single model to transport examples between any pair of classes in their original pixel representation. Moreover, CounterFlow generates a counterfactual by integrating a single (learned) ODE from source to target, with no additional VAE decoding or iterative optimization at inference. • To enable the learning of the transport map, we introduce two complementary contributions: a latent-space nearest-neighbor pairing strategy and an auxiliary classification loss. The pairing algorithm leverages the embedding space of a pretrained VAE to associate each source example with a seman-tically similar target example, thereby reducing the transport distance that the flow must learn. The auxiliary classification loss further guides the flow toward transformations that induce the desired change in the classifier’s prediction. • We provide an empirical evaluation covering conditional validity, plausibility, and computational efficiency, a combination rarely reported together in prior work, demonstrating that CounterFlow matches or improves on state-of-the-art generation quality at a fraction of the computational cost of GAN- or diffusion-based alternatives.

Once vθ is trained, generating a counterfactual no longer requires any pairing, VAE encoding, or mini-batch construction. CounterFlow requires only an input sample x0 (with the prediction y0 = f (x0))and a desired target class y1. The source class is obtained using the pretrained classifier, while the target class is either specified by the user or sampled uniformly among the remaining classes. # 5 Experiments 

We now turn to an empirical evaluation of CounterFlow against state-of-the-art counterfactual gener-ation methods. Note that our implementation is based on the torchcfm library (Tong et al., 2024), which provides the Conditional Flow Matching framework used to train CounterFlow. The source code to reproduce the experiments will be made publicly available at this link. 

5.1 Setting 

Datasets. We evaluate CounterFlow on two standard image classification benchmarks: MNIST and 

CIFAR-10 , MNIST consists of 70 ,000 grayscale images of handwritten digits belonging to 10 classes, with a spatial resolution of 28 ×28 pixels. CIFAR-10 contains 60 ,000 color images of size 32 ×32 pixels, evenly distributed among 10 object categories. These two datasets provide complementary evaluation settings, ranging from a simple handwritten digit classification task to a natural image classification benchmark. For all experiments, we use the standard training and test splits provided with each dataset. Section A.4 discusses why we do not extend the comparison to larger benchmarks such as ImageNet. 

CounterFlow’s inference. For all quantitative evaluations, CounterFlow is integrated via the Euler solver with 40 steps. This fixed-step solver provides a more deterministic evaluation protocol and allows a fair comparison of the computational cost across methods. For qualitative visualizations, we instead use the Dormand–Prince solver (Dormand and Prince, 1980) ( dopri5 ) to obtain smoother trajectories and higher-quality visual results. 

Methods. We compare CounterFlow 2 against three recent counterfactual generation methods: C3LT (Khor-ram and Fuxin, 2022), FastDiME (Weng et al., 2024), and LeapFactual (Cao et al., 2025), where we use the official implementations. Among diffusion-guidance methods, we retain only FastDiME as a representative baseline: DiME and ACE are prohibitively expensive to run at scale due to their inner-loop or per-step attack computations; DVCE requires an adversarially robust classifier, a constraint that our method does not rely on and that we therefore do not impose in our experiments; and MaskDiME’s masking mechanism restricts edits to a localized region, which conflicts with our evaluation setting where the change may need to affect the whole image. Beyond this choice, neither of the two remain-ing baselines, namely LeapFactual and FastDiME, is applicable to both datasets. LeapFactual relies on

Models to be explained. To ensure a fair comparison, all methods rely on the same pretrained image classifier for a given dataset. Metrics. We evaluate the counterfactual explanations along four axes: effectiveness (reaching f (x1) = 

y1), proximity to the source (a distribution-level counterpart of the distance d(x1, x0) minimized per example in Equation (1), measured in a semantic feature space rather than in a pixel space), realism 

(the plausibility constraint qy1 (x1) ≥ ε, also from Equation (1)), and computational efficiency . We operationalize the evaluation of these axes through the following five metrics. • Conditional Validity measures the proportion (in %) of generated counterfactuals x1 that are classified as the desired target class y1, i.e. , whether we have f (x1) = y1.• FID t computes the Fr´ echet Inception Distance between the generated counterfactuals and real samples from the target class, quantifying realism : lower values indicate that the generated sam-ples better match the distribution of the desired class. • FID s computes the Fr´ echet Inception Distance between the generated counterfactuals and the original source class, measuring proximity to the source : lower values indicate that the counter-factuals remain closer to the original data distribution, suggesting that fewer semantic changes were applied to reach the target class. • FLOPs / sample measures the computational cost required to generate a single counterfactual ex-planation. It includes the complete inference pipeline, i.e. , all neural network evaluations required by the ODE solver. This metric provides an implementation-independent estimate of inference complexity. • Model Size reports the total number of trainable parameters of each method, allowing comparison of their memory footprint and model complexity. Following the standard definition of the Fr´ echet Inception Distance (FID, Heusel et al., 2017), feature statistics for FID t and FID s are extracted from pretrained image classification networks. For CIFAR-10, we use the pretrained Inception-V3 network available in torchvision . Since ImageNet-pretrained models are not well suited for handwritten digit recognition, for MNIST we instead compute features using the pretrained convolutional classifier employed throughout our experiments. FID is then com-puted between the Gaussian statistics of the extracted features from the generated counterfactuals and those of the corresponding source or target class, i.e. , FID t on the target class and FID s on the source class. Note that, since these statistics are estimated from the subset of test examples belonging to a single class, rather than from the full test set as in standard, unconditional FID computations, the re-ported values are higher than those typically observed in unconditional image generation. Table 1a reports the quantitative comparison between CounterFlow and representative counterfactual generation methods on MNIST and CIFAR-10; a radar-plot visualization is provided in Figure 3 in the appendix. 

MNIST. CounterFlow reaches the highest conditional validity ( 99 .6% ) and the lowest target FID while remaining close to the source, confirming that the learned conditional transport reliably reaches the target class with a single ODE integration. LeapFactual stays closer to the source but has the highest target FID, reflecting a more conservative transformation. CIFAR-10. Improving both FIDs at once is the signature of a near-minimal transport: counterfactuals that fall within the target class without drifting away from the source image. more expensive per sample, and has the worst FID on both axes; with so few samples switching class, these scores reflect generic realism rather than class transfer. 9Overall, CounterFlow achieves state-of-the-art counterfactual validity and the best realism/proximity trade-off on CIFAR-10, while substantially reducing inference complexity compared with prior flow-and diffusion-based approaches. 

Ablation. Table 1b shows that replacing latent-space pairing with random pairing consistently lowers conditional validity and raises both target and source FID on both datasets, mostly through the source FID on CIFAR-10. Pairing semantically similar examples yields simpler transport trajectories that are easier for the conditional velocity field to approximate, whereas random pairing often associates visually unrelated samples, leading to harder-to-learn trajectories and lower-quality counterfactuals. Style Preservation. Figure 1 shows five source/counterfactual pairs per dataset. On MNIST, Coun-terFlow preserves writer-specific style, such as stroke thickness and slant – e.g. , the 4→5 transition retains the source’s thin strokes – and keeps the digit’s orientation, rather than collapsing to a generic class prototype. On CIFAR10, the same near-minimal-path behavior appears at the scene level: the background stays untouched and only the foreground object changes, often preserving its color palette and orientation while altering just the class-relevant structure. 

Trajectories. In most examples the color flips early, showing that CounterFlow reaches the target class within a small fraction of the path and spends the remaining steps refining the sample within that class rather than driving the decision itself, i.e. , most of the trajectory makes the class change plausible rather than induces it. Intermediate frames stay visually close to both endpoints rather than passing through a degraded intermediate state, confirming at a finer granularity that CounterFlow produces minimal, semantically meaningful edits rather than an unconstrained image-to-image mapping. # 6 Discussion 

Existing image counterfactual generation methods often require heavy generative backbones, including diffusion models or multiple class-specific generative networks, making their deployment computation-ally demanding. CounterFlow demonstrates that Conditional Flow Matching provides a significantly simpler alternative. By learning a single target-conditioned transport field and requiring only one ODE integration at inference, our method achieves competitive or superior counterfactual quality while sub-stantially reducing computational complexity. We believe this constitutes an important step toward making counterfactual explanations practical for interactive decision-support systems, where explana-tions must be generated quickly and consistently. Despite these encouraging results, several limitations remain. First, our experiments are restricted to relatively low-resolution image datasets. Evaluating CounterFlow on larger-scale benchmarks such as ImageNet or high-resolution generative datasets would provide further evidence of its scalability. Second, our latent-space pairing strategy currently relies on nearest-neighbor retrieval using a pretrained encoder, which introduces an additional preprocessing step. Finally, while Conditional Flow Matching substantially reduces inference complexity compared to diffusion models, solving an ODE remains more expensive than a single feed-forward network. 10 7 Conclusion 

We presented CounterFlow, a Conditional Flow Matching framework for visual counterfactual expla-nation generation. Unlike previous approaches relying on GANs or diffusion models, CounterFlow directly learns a target-conditioned transport field between image classes in pixel space. Experiments on MNIST and CIFAR-10 demonstrate that the proposed method achieves state-of-the-art counterfac-tual validity while remaining competitive in image realism and substantially reducing computational requirements. Beyond counterfactual explanations, CounterFlow illustrates that target-conditioned flow matching can efficiently learn semantic transformations directly between complex data distributions, without relying on an intermediate noise distribution. Future work will investigate its application to larger-scale datasets and other data transformation tasks, including domain adaptation, semantic image editing, and robust model analysis.

Explained classifier. A counterfactual method should not depend on the specific classifier it explains. Table 5 therefore keeps CounterFlow unchanged and only substitutes f , comparing the RepVGG-A2 used throughout the paper with a deliberately mis-specified variant, namely the same network eval-uated without its input normalization, which drops its accuracy to 86 .3% , and with an adversarially robust PreActResNet-18 (Gowal et al., 2021). The mis-specified classifier is explained as well as the original one: validity stays at 100% and both FID scores remain within two points of the default, in-dicating that CounterFlow does not require the explained model to be accurate, only that it defines an exploitable decision boundary. The robust classifier behaves differently: validity drops to 83 .5% , while FID t reaches the lowest value we observe ( 55 .72 ). This is consistent with what adversarial training is designed to achieve, namely flattening the decision boundary and suppressing the small non-robust directions that a gradient-based guidance term can exploit, so that LCla pushes less strongly and a frac-tion of the samples never crosses the boundary. The counterfactuals that do cross must therefore carry a genuine, large-scale semantic change, which is what brings them closer to the target distribution; note that the 16 .5% of samples that remain in the source class can only inflate FID t, so this value is, if any-thing, pessimistic. Explaining a robust model is thus harder for CounterFlow, but yields more plausible counterfactuals, which is coherent with diffusion-based methods that explicitly require an adversarially robust classifier (Augustin et al., 2022). We restrict our experiments to MNIST and CIFAR-10, the largest settings in which all compared meth-ods can be run with their official implementations, the same classifier, and the same evaluation protocol. For instance, extending this comparison to ImageNet raises the following issues. 

Baselines. C3LT requires a separate model for each pair of classes, i.e. , 499 ,500 models for the 1,000 

classes of ImageNet. Hence, its ImageNet experiments are qualitative, restricted to five pairs of classes, and use latent codes sampled from a pretrained GAN rather than real images (Khorram and Fuxin, 2022). LeapFactual relies on a latent space in which inputs can be faithfully encoded, and each of its leaps requires decoding the latent code and querying the classifier; its only large-scale experiment is a binary task on images generated by a pretrained GAN (Cao et al., 2025). Diffusion-based methods do apply to ImageNet, but at a high cost: on CIFAR-10, FastDiME already requires about 900 × more FLOPs per counterfactual than CounterFlow (Table 1a), and ACE is evaluated on only three pairs of ImageNet classes (Jeanneret et al., 2023). 

Evaluation. Our FID s and FID t are computed against the test images of a single class, whereas Ima-geNet provides only 50 validation images per class, which makes such estimates unreliable. Moreover, the number of ordered source-target pairs grows from 90 to 999 ,000 . As noted by Jeanneret et al. (2023), counterfactual explanation methods still lack an appropriate testbed on such complex datasets. 

CounterFlow. In principle, CounterFlow applies to ImageNet by adapting its U-Net architecture. How-ever, unlike the baselines, which build on generative models pretrained at a considerable computational cost, CounterFlow would have to be trained from scratch in pixel space, which exceeds our computa-tional budget. Moreover, the number of source–target contexts (y0, y 1) grows quadratically with the number of classes, so that each context would be seen less often during training.
