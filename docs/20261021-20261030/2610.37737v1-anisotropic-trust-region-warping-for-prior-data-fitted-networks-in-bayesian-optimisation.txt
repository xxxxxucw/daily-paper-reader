Abstract
Prior-Data Fitted Networks (PFNs) replace the hyperparameter refitting of Gaussian Process (GP)
surrogates in Bayesian optimisation with a single forward pass, at the cost of a prior that is fixed at
meta-training time. We show empirically that a PFN’s predictive log-likelihood falls increasingly
behind that of a refitted GP as the objective function’s lengthscale grows beyond the mode of the
PFN’s meta-training prior, and that this deficit carries over to optimisation performance. To correct
the mismatch without retraining the network, we introduce Anisotropic Trust Region (ATR) warp-
ing. Per-dimension lengthscales are estimated by kernel target alignment, and an affine rescaling of
the input space maps the objective onto the lengthscale the PFN expects, with a trust region around
the anchor determining which observations are retained, leaving the PFN’s O(d · N 2) inference
cost unchanged. On functions drawn from a Mat´ern-3/2 kernel, ATR warping improves optimi-
sation performance of the PFN at both extremes of the lengthscale range. Our implementation is
publicly available at https://github.com/youngers2006/Trust-Region-Warping-For-PFNs.
1. Introduction
Bayesian Optimisation (BO) [6] has traditionally relied on Gaussian Processes (GPs) as surrogate
models due to their rigorous uncertainty quantification and exact posterior inference. However,
GPs impose a severe computational bottleneck, refitting the covariance matrix requires O(N3) [15]
complexity, limiting their scalability for optimisation tasks.
Prior-Data Fitted Networks (PFNs) have emerged as a highly scalable alternative to GPs [10].
By training Transformers to approximate Bayesian posteriors, PFNs perform inference entirely via
in-context learning [9], reducing the cost of refitting to O(1) and bounding inference to O(d · N2).
Despite these computational advantages, this research highlights an issue in PFNs, namely poor
generalisation to objective functions with lengthscales above the mode of its meta-training prior.
Because PFNs are amortised surrogates, their predictive accuracy is bound to the static prior they
were trained on. Through stress testing via prior-objective mismatch, we demonstrate that when
evaluating functions with lengthscales well above the mode of the PFN’s training prior, the model’s
predictive log-likelihood and downstream optimisation performance diverge from that of a GP.
To solve this degradation without sacrificing scalability, we introduce Anisotropic Trust Re-
gion (ATR) Warping. Rather than expanding the PFN’s training distribution, ATR warps the input
space to match the PFN’s native prior. By optimising a target lengthscale vector using Kernel Tar-
get Alignment and constraining the search space, we map mismatched functions back towards the

2. Background and Motivation
PFNs amortise Bayesian inference via meta training, replacing the hyperparameter fitting done in
GPs with a forward pass [9]. Amortisation limits the model’s ability to fit the target functions that
lie outside of the meta-training prior. A PFN is optimal only under the prior it was meta-trained
on [12], and its in-context predictions are not known to generalise beyond that prior’s support [11].
To demonstrate this effect we tested the model fitting capability of the PFN against the GP
across a range of lengthscales. We draw functions from a Mat´ern-3/2 kernel, using random Fourier
feature sampling [14], of unit amplitude with lengthscale, l, swept over [0.021, 2.0], and measure the
predictive log-likelihood of the objective function averaged over test locations, given 20·d observa-
tions, reporting the median across function draws. As a reference we fit a GP with the same kernel
and ARD, refitted by marginal likelihood at every step under a deliberately uninformative smoothed
box prior which includes the lengthscales tested. A reference is necessary because absolute log-
likelihood rises with l for both models. Longer lengthscales mean more effective data per correlation
length, so the task itself becomes easier, and only the relative comparison isolates the fixed prior.

A GP addresses lengthscale mismatch by fitting the lengthscale. A PFN cannot, but the same
correction can be applied in the input space: rescaling the domain by a factor s divides the function’s
lengthscale by s, so a suitable choice of s maps a mismatched function onto one that the prior
expects. Snoek et al. [16] warped the inputs in this way so that a stationary GP could fit non-
stationary objectives, and LABCAT [17] rescales its trust regions using the lengthscales of a local
GP, but both rely on a surrogate that is refitted as data arrives. TuneTables [5] and GIT-BO [18]
do adapt a frozen PFN, though through learned context embeddings and a gradient-informed search
subspace respectively, rather than through the inputs, and we are not aware of work that warps
the inputs to match a fixed PFN’s prior. Without refitting, the warp needs its own estimate of the
lengthscale, so prior mismatch becomes an estimation problem. Section 3 describes our estimator
and the warp built from it, and Section 4 evaluates the result.
3. Anisotropic Trust Region Warping
Aim: Estimate the objective function’s lengthscale on [0, 1]d and use it to construct an anisotropic
trust region, T , whose affine warp maps the lengthscale to the PFN prior’s mode, in O(d · N2) [9].

trust region is therefore what makes the warping admissible for lengthscales below the mode. When
lj > ltarget the objective is smoother than expected, sj > 1, and the region of side sj extends
beyond the domain, so all data is retained (Lj = 1). Warping that region to [0, 1] places the real
domain in [0, uj], the remainder cannot be queried.
Trust Region Placement: The region is centred on xopt with side lengths Lj, and is translated
back inside the domain rather than truncated where it would overflow [0, 1], so its width is preserved.
Restarts: After τ consecutive iterations without improvement, as in TuRBO, a point is drawn
uniformly from the domain and becomes the new anchor. Restarts are necessary at smaller length-
scales, where rough functions have many basins, to prevent getting stuck in local optima.
Bounding Against Context Starvation: Highly rough functions can shrink the region so far
that it discards past evaluations and leaves the surrogate with no context. To prevent this, the floor
is set from the k observations nearest xopt in the L∞(Chebyshev) norm, with axis j scaled by
lj/ltarget so that the neighbourhood reflects the anisotropy the warp will apply. Writing dk,j for
their largest deviation from xopt in dimension j, we set smin,j = 2dk,j, the axis-aligned bounding
box of those k points, which ensures the region contains at least k samples.

4. Experiments and Results
To demonstrate the effectiveness of ATR warping we tested it against the base PFN on functions
drawn from a Mat´ern-3/2 kernel across 15 lengthscales in [0.021, 1.5], at d ∈{2, 5, 10}, running
50 iterations with 21 repeats per configuration. Draws are isotropic so that one lengthscale indexes
the sweep; anisotropic draws gave the same picture. Figure 3 shows the 2D results at both extremes
and at the prior mode; Appendix B has the full set and Appendix D the setup.
ATR warping is beneficial at extreme lengthscales, though the improvement is more modest at
the lower end. For lengthscales in distribution the estimated ratio lj/ltarget is close to one, so the
trust region covers most of the domain, and ATR-PFN behaves much like the PFN. What improve-
ment remains comes from the trust region and restarts rather than warping, which is near identity
here. The same pattern holds at 5D and 10D (Figures 8 and 9), though, as Figure 1 would predict,
with smaller margins. An ablation in Appendix C, supplying the true lengthscale in place of the
KTA estimate, shows that estimator variance does not drive the improvement. At a larger budget
of 150 iterations the advantage for smaller lengthscales widens substantially (Figure 10), we do not
separate the contributions of the warping and the trust region at short lengthscales.
5. Discussion and Future Work
We have shown that a fixed PFN’s predictive accuracy and downstream optimisation performance
degrade as the objective’s lengthscale grows beyond the mode of its meta-training prior, and that
ATR warping substantially improves the PFN at mismatched lengthscales, without retraining the
network or raising its O(d · N2) inference complexity. The ablation in Appendix C, which supplies
the true lengthscale in place of an estimate, suggests the improvement is not driven by estimator
variance. Above the prior mode Lj = 1, so the region spans the domain and restarts cannot move
it, neither localisation mechanism is active there, and the gain that remains is attributable to the
warping alone.
KTA’s estimates are monotone but shrunk toward the centre of the range (Appendix E), and have
high variance on sparse data, which carries through to the run-to-run variance of the optimisation; a
better low-sample estimator would reduce both. High alignment is also only a sufficient, not a neces-
sary, sign of a good kernel [13]. Shrinking the region to correct a rough function discards evaluations
outside it, so the k-nearest-neighbour floor keeps at least k points in context, at the cost of capping

the k nearest evaluations are far apart, the floor keeps the region large and the warp has little effect.
We isolated a single axis of prior mismatch, and how the method behaves when several are mis-
specified at once remains to be tested on standard benchmarks. ATR leaves the network untouched,
so it composes with corrections for other axes, such as HEBO’s Kumaraswamy input warping [2].

The results in Figure 7 use a budget of 50 iterations, at which the separation between PFN and
ATR-PFN at the shortest lengthscale is modest. To check whether this reflects the budget rather
than the method, we repeated the smaller lengthscale configurations in 2D with a budget of 150
iterations to allow sufficient information for learning at these lengthscales. The advantage widens
over the longer horizon, consistent with the k-nearest-neighbour floor loosening as N grows and
permitting a tighter trust region. We do not separate the contributions of the warp and the trust
region here.
Appendix C. Ablation Study
We ran an ablation study to separate the effect of the warping from the estimation of the lengthscale
(Figure 11), in 2D as it was the most representative dimension, 50 iterations being enough to gather
sufficient information. Our results show that the method’s improvement is not driven by estimator
variance. The trust region and restart mechanisms are retained in this condition as they are integral
to the method, so it isolates the estimator rather than separating the warping from localisation.
The residual at l = ltarget, where warping is identity, indicates that the trust region and restart
mechanism also contribute to the performance improvement; above ltarget the reverse holds, since
Lj = 1 retains all data and the full domain, leaving the improvement there attributable to the
warping alone.

Appendix D. Experiment Details
D.1. Gaussian Process Setup
The reference surrogate used is a Gaussian Process with a Mat´ern-3/2 ARD kernel, to match the
PFN [10], in d dimensions, scaled by an outputscale with a Gamma(2.0, 0.15) prior [10]. Length-
scales are given a smoothed box prior over [0.015, 2.5] with σ = 0.01, chosen to be uninformative
over the tested range whilst remaining correctly specified. As our tested objective functions are
noiseless, the GP’s noise is constrained to [10−5, 10−3] and initialised at 10−4.

D.2. Prior-Data Fitted Network Setup
As the PFN surrogate we use the model conditioned on the HEBO+ prior from PFNs4BO [10]
without any finetuning. Its meta-training prior places a Gamma(α = 1.211, β = 1.521) prior on
the lengthscale, whose mode, (α−1)/β = 0.1385, is the value used as ltarget throughout this paper.
Unlike the HEBO surrogate that inspired it, this checkpoint was trained without input warping in its
prior (hebo warping is disabled in the released training configuration).

We used the following parameters for the Kernel Target Alignment optimisation process, shown in
the table below. We found k = 4 to be sufficient for our context budget (50), but this value will
likely need to be reduced for smaller context sets and raised for larger context sets.

Two details of the procedure are worth stating. The alignment is fit to all observations gathered
so far, not only those inside the current region, so the estimate does not depend on the region it
is used to construct. Targets are standardised before the alignment is computed, and the retained
subset is standardised again after filtering, so that restricting the region does not present the PFN
with an objective whose amplitude is inconsistent with its prior.
Cortes et al. [1] centre both kernel matrices; we centre only Ky. Centring Kx sharpened the
alignment’s dependence on l but raised the variance of the estimate at our sample sizes, producing
over-aggressive warping which hurt optimisation performance, so we retain the uncentred spatial
kernel.
D.4. Regression Comparison Experiment
Regression performance was assessed by drawing a function per repeat, conditioning each surrogate
on 20 · d uniformly sampled observations, and evaluating the predictive log-likelihood of the true
function from the surrogate fit. We report the median across 21 repeats over 17 lengthscales in
[0.021, 2.0]. This process was repeated for d ∈{1, . . . , 10}.
D.5. Optimisation Experiment
Optimisation performance was assessed by drawing a function per repeat, conditioning each surro-
gate on 5 · d uniformly sampled initial points. Optimisation was then run for 50 iterations, with 21
repeats over 15 lengthscales in [0.021, 1.5] at d ∈{2, 5, 10}, using expected improvement (EI) [8]
maximised over 100000 Sobol sampled candidates where the set is common to all surrogates at
each step. We report the median regret across the 21 draws, normalised by the range of the objec-
tive function, so that results are comparable across function draws and lengthscales.

Appendix E. KTA Estimation Accuracy
ATR warping depends on the lengthscale estimate being close enough to the true lengthscale for
warping to move the objective function towards the prior. We assessed this in 2D over 21 repeats,
taking the estimate at iteration 20 of the optimisation loop and averaging the 2 per-axis values, since
the objectives were isotropic in this case.
Shown in Figure 12, the estimate is monotone in the true lengthscale but shrunk toward the
centre of the tested range, crossing the identity near l = 0.1, close to the PFN’s prior mode. At
both extremes the estimate causes an underestimation of the true warping factor, so the correction
is partial. The estimator does not cause over-warping anywhere in the tested range, so the objective
moves towards the prior rather than past it.
