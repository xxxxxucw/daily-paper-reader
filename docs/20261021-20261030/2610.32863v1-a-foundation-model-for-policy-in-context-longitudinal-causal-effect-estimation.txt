## ABSTRACT 

Longitudinal causal effect estimation is complicated by time-varying confound-ing and treatment-confounder feedback loops. Existing methods generally require data-specific nuisance-model fitting, and many are tailored to prespecified treat-ment sequences or prediction horizons, leading to time-consuming computation at deployment. We introduce Policy In-Context Longitudinal causal Effect estima-tion with Prior-data Fitted Networks (PICLE-PFN ), the first foundation model for longitudinal causal effect estimation that supports dynamic treatment poli-cies. PICLE-PFN represents target policies through in-context history-decision demonstrations, providing a unified interface for a broad class of treatment poli-cies. Pretrained over synthetic data sampled from longitudinal structural causal models, PICLE-PFN amortizes causal effect estimation across datasets, policies, and horizons. Given a new observational dataset and target policy, it directly pre-dicts conditional average potential outcomes (CAPOs) in one forward pass with-out task-specific training or hyperparameter tuning. Across synthetic and semi-synthetic longitudinal benchmarks, PICLE-PFN achieves competitive RMSE and substantially improved uncertainty calibration with a fraction of deployment run-time, compared to state-of-the-art conventional estimators trained and tuned sep-arately for each dataset. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Clinical care often involves a sequence of treatment decisions that evolve with a patient’s changing condition (Murphy, 2003; Chakraborty & Moodie, 2013). Estimating the effects of these time-varying treatments is therefore essential for guiding effective care. Doing so from observational data is particularly challenging: covariates can simultaneously be consequences of earlier treatments and determinants of later treatment decisions and outcomes, creating treatment–confounder feedback that complicates causal effect estimation (Robins, 1986; Hern´ an & Robins, 2010). Existing methods decompose the longitudinal counterfactual target into a sequence of local nuisance regressions, which are fitted to each target dataset and combined through a causal estimation proce-dure (Robins, 1986; 2000; Bang & Robins, 2005). This decomposition introduces two practical chal-lenges. First, estimation errors can propagate and accumulate over the intervention horizon (Chen et al., 2026a). Second, existing methods face a trade-off between flexibility across causal queries and estimation variance. Some estimators fit nuisance models that can be reused across many poli-cies and horizons, but this flexibility can come at the cost of high variance, e.g., through products of inverse propensities or by modeling the full longitudinal data-generating process (Li et al., 2021; Frauen et al., 2025). Other estimators reduce variance by fitting only the outcome regressions needed for a particular policy and horizon, but changing the query then requires retraining and potentially new hyperparameter tuning (Hess et al., 2026; Shirakawa et al., 2024). Moreover, many existing methods (Lim, 2018; Bica et al., 2020; Melnychuk et al., 2022; Seedat et al., 2022) are designed

Yet clinically relevant counterfactual questions may involve treatment regimes that adapt decisions to time-varying patient characteristics. There is a need for an estimator that supports both static and dynamic policies across different horizons, without retraining for each new target. To address these challenges, we explore an alternative approach based on in-context learning (ICL). Recent tabular foundation models demonstrate that pretraining on synthetic tasks can produce mod-els that adapt in context to new datasets and prediction problems without parameter updates (Holl-mann et al., 2023; 2025; QU et al., 2025; 2026). This paradigm offers two opportunities for longi-tudinal causal estimation. First, in synthetic data, we can intervene on a target policy and directly generate its corresponding multi-step potential outcome. This provides ground-truth supervision for the final causal estimand, avoiding the need to learn and compose a sequence of local nuisance models. Second, diverse pretraining priors can support adaptation to different datasets and causal queries. The remaining gap is representing target policies, including dynamic ones, as context for ICL, and directly learning conditional average potential outcome (CAPO) across different horizons. To meet these gaps, we introduce PICLE-PFN, the first foundation model for longitudinal CAPO estimation supporting both static and dynamic policies. Our contributions are three-fold: (i) Pol-icy representation and ICL formulation. We show that a target policy’s decisions on factual histories across the entire observed dataset preserve the policy information needed for CAPO identification. This motivates an ICL formulation that combines observational data with policy demonstrations, providing a unified interface for static and dynamic policies over multi-step inter-vention windows. (ii) Architecture and amortized estimation. We design a novel transformer based on Prior-data Fitted Network (PFN). Direct pretraining on synthetic multi-step CAPO targets avoids full generative modeling and explicit nuisance-model composition at deployment. Pretrain-ing across diverse datasets, policies, and horizons enables flexible causal queries in a single for-ward pass without task-specific optimization. (iii) Empirical performance. PICLE-PFN achieves the lowest RMSE in six of seven benchmark settings, with up to 12.2% lower RMSE than the strongest baseline, while being about 100 × to 5000 × faster at deployment than a comparably ac-curate method. ## 2 RELATED WORK 

Tabular Foundation Model for Time Series and Causal Inference Recent work adopts the PFN approach for time-series forecasting. They either repurpose a pretrained non-longitudinal tabular model by treating time as additional features (Hoo et al., 2026) or explicit model temporal struc-ture (Dooley et al., 2023; Taga et al., 2025; Potapczynski et al., 2026). Our model follows the latter approach, building an architecture to respect the temporal ordering of trajectories. A separate line of work applies PFN to causal inference (Balazadeh et al., 2025; Robertson et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026). Yet they only focus on the static, single-treatment-assignment setting. In contrast, we con-sider the longitudinal setting with sequential treatment assignments and time-varying confounders 1.

Longitudinal Causal Effect Estimation. Longitudinal causal estimators address time-varying confounding through modeling treatment, outcome, or both. Treatment-based methods use inverse probability weighting or balanced representations (Robins, 2000; Bica et al., 2020; Seedat et al.,

2Preprint 2022; Lim, 2018; Melnychuk et al., 2022), but can suffer from unstable weights under limited over-lap (Cole & Hern´ an, 2008; Petersen et al., 2012). Outcome-based methods use the G-formula, either by simulating covariate trajectories (Li et al., 2021) or recursively regressing outcomes backward in time (McGrath et al., 2020; Frauen et al., 2023; Oprescu et al., 2025; Hess et al., 2026). Dou-bly robust methods combine treatment and outcome models to reduce sensitivity to misspecification (Van der Laan et al., 2011; Frauen et al., 2025; Shirakawa et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2026a;b). Despite these advances, existing methods show limited flexibility to handle various causal queries. Many focus on prespecified treatment sequences that do not adapt to patients’ evolving status. Some require retraining or potentially retuning when changing the target policy or horizon. Recent DR-based methods also commonly assume a single-dimension treatment, with extensions to multidi-mensional treatment spaces requiring nontrivial changes to their models and objectives. In contrast, PICLE-PFN provides all these capabilities within a single pretrained model (Table 1).

Supervision for Multi-step Counterfactual Targets. The formulation above specifies the informa-tion available at inference time, but not how to supervise the estimator. Although the target CAPO is identified, it is not directly observed as a multi-step training label. For one-step outcome regression, the observed outcome provides a direct supervision signal: regressing Yt+1 on (Ht, A t) estimates the conditional mean needed to evaluate a target policy. Over multiple steps, however, the observed endpoint reflects the subsequent treatments assigned under the behavior policy, rather than the tar-get policy. It therefore does not generally provide a valid regression label for the multi-step CAPO under a target policy. Conventional G-computation methods bridge this gap by fitting local nuisance models and composing their predictions across time (Li et al., 2021; Shirakawa et al., 2024). Our ICL estimator bypasses explicit nuisance-model fitting and directly maps observational and policy contexts to CAPO, requiring a different source of supervision. We therefore pretrain on synthetic datasets, from which we can generate both the observed data and corresponding CAPO targets through simulation under the target policy. The combination of ICL and synthetic supervision makes the PFN paradigm a natural fit.

We generate synthetic training tasks by sampling longitudinal SCMs and target policies. Each SCM varies in its dimensions, graph skeleton, and transition mechanisms, capturing nonlinear dynamics, heterogeneous treatment effects, and diverse behavioural policies. We construct the graph by sam-pling parent variables and their time lags while enforcing acyclicity. Target policies include both static treatment sequences and dynamic rules. Figure 1: PICLE-PFN Architecture . Feature pooling encodes longitudinal covariates and treat-ments into embeddings processed by a shared backbone with causal temporal attention and row-attention. We parametrize Fθ using a PFN-style transformer (Hollmann et al., 2025; QU et al., 2025) with a shared observed-data backbone and window-specific readout tokens . Each trajectory has one read-out token per intervention window (Figure 1). Each transformer layer first updates the observed-data representations through causal attention across time steps and row attention across trajectories, then updates the readout tokens. Each readout cross-attends to its own trajectory under a window-specific mask: for window (s, e ), query readouts access only the factual history Hs, while context readouts access the factual trajectory through the target endpoint, including Ye+1 . The context readouts ad-ditionally incorporate target-policy demonstrations over s:e. Readout tokens then attend across tra-jectories, using only context readouts as keys and values, to enable in-context learning. Finally, the query readouts predict CAPO. Readout tokens share model parameters across windows, producing predictions for all admissible windows in a single forward pass. Full details appear in Appendix B.1.

This is the desired behavior: the model widens its intervals to reflect the growing uncertainty of longer horizons, and thereby maintains coverage. In contrast, IGC-Net’s intervals do not widen with longer horizons, and even narrow in one setting, suggesting its substantial overconfidence across horizons. These results show that PICLE-PFN expresses predictive uncertainty more consistently with its observed CAPO estimation errors.

None of these produces an alternating treatment sequence. On 

mimic 1t with τ = 4 , we evaluate two unseen policies, applied regardless of how the patient re-sponds: treat at every other step, (1 , 0, 1, 0) , and treat at the first and last steps only, (1 , 0, 0, 1) . The result suggests that in-context conditioning on the target policy can generalize beyond the policy families represented in the prior. 

## 6 CONCLUSION , L IMITATIONS , AND FUTURE WORK 

We reformulate longitudinal CAPO estimation as an ICL task by representing target policies through decisions on factual histories across the observed dataset. We introduce PICLE-PFN, the first foun-dation model to longitudinal CAPO estimation supporting both static and dynamic policies. On syn-thetic and semi-synthetic benchmarks, PICLE-PFN matches or outperforms baselines at a fraction of their deployment runtime. We further evaluate its ability to predict factual outcomes in real-world 9Preprint data. Further analyses demonstrate strong uncertainty calibration, greater resilience to small samples and unobserved confounders, and generalization to policies unseen during pretraining. Several limitations motivate future work. (i) Computational complexity. Quadratic costs in sample size N and trajectory length T (Appendix F) limit scalability. Advances in scalable tabular founda-tion models (QU et al., 2026; Grinsztajn et al., 2026) may inform more efficient architectures. (ii) 

Scope of pretraining. Our model considers binary treatments and discrete-time trajectories without right censoring. Extending it to broader treatment spaces, continuous-time series, and censored out-comes would expand its applicability. (iii) Identifying assumptions and prior dependence. Causal identification requires assumptions such as sequential ignorability; empirical robustness to certain violations does not establish theoretical guarantees under unobserved confounding. Accuracy may also deteriorate when deployment settings differ from the pretraining distribution. Characterizing this sensitivity and developing guarantees under prior misspecification remain important directions. 10 Preprint

## B IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

B.1 MODEL ARCHITECTURE 

The model operates on two kinds of tokens: observed data tokens , which represent the factual tra-jectories, and readout tokens , which combine the factual representations with target policy demon-strations to predict potential outcomes. Both are processed by the same stack of eight transformer layers. Each layer has two parts: an observed data backbone that updates the observed data tokens, followed by a readout update in which the readout tokens attend to the backbone’s output at that layer. The backbone never attends to readout tokens, so its representations are independent of the target policy and intervention window. We first describe the architecture for a single intervention window (s, e ), and then explain how the same construction is extended to all admissible windows in parallel. 

Feature Pooling and Tokenization. Each trajectory is represented by 2T pooled embeddings corresponding to the interleaved sequence L1, A 1, . . . , L T , A T . To encode each L or A vector, we first embed its scalar features and add learned column-identity embeddings. A feature-attention layer models interactions among features using self-attention, followed by attention pooling with learned query tokens to aggregate them into a single embedding (Lee et al., 2019; QU et al., 2025; Grinsztajn et al., 2026). Baseline covariates B are encoded in the same way, and their pooled embedding is added to every Lt. Each sequence position also receives a position embedding over the 2T positions plus an L/A type embedding. The readout tokens Rs:e are initialized with three components: (i) Start- and end-index embeddings; (ii) The initial target policy demonstration Aπs 

> s

; (iii) Outcome: factual outcome Ye+1 for context trajectories, and a null value for query trajectories. 15 Preprint

We compare against nine baselines. CRN (Bica et al., 2020), RMSN (Lim, 2018), TE-CDE (Seedat et al., 2022), G-Net (Li et al., 2021), and CT (Melnychuk et al., 2022) are taken from the CT codebase (Melnychuk et al., 2022); D3-Net (Chen et al., 2026a), DeepLTMLE (Shirakawa et al., 2024), and DeepACE (Frauen et al., 2023) from the D3-Net codebase (Chen et al., 2026a); and 

IGC-Net (Hess et al., 2026) from the authors’ public implementation. 20 Preprint There is one major change in our implementation of IGC-Net. As published, it estimates the effect of a single static treatment sequence shared by every unit. Neither the paper nor the implementation considers dynamic target policies. However, we noticed that it can be readily extended to support such a setting by modifying a step in data preprocessing. Specifically, we extend IGC-Net to our setting by replacing the hardcoded treatment sequence with the per-trajectory treatment decision of the target policy π, leaving the architecture and training objective untouched. This extension goes beyond the published scope of IGC-Net and is made in its favour: it is what allows IGC-Net to be run on every setting, including those with a history-dependent target policy. G-Net supports dynamic treatment regimes in principle: its Monte Carlo rollout simulates future covariates, on which a regime could be evaluated. The released implementation, however, fixes the full treatment sequence before inference and never re-evaluates a policy on the simulated state, so supporting history-dependent regimes would require non-trivial changes to its inference path. We therefore do not run G-Net in this setting. This omission does not affect our conclusions: under static treatment sequences on the same data (Table S.2, mimic 1t ), G-Net already underperforms the leading baselines by a wide margin at every horizon. CRN, RMSN, TE-CDE, G-Net, and CT are encoder–decoder models that are trained once per task 

on the full trajectory window ( T = 15 steps) and then evaluated at every horizon τ ∈ { 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 }.D3-Net, DeepLTMLE, DeepACE, and IGC-Net are based on ICE G-computation, which requires horizon-specific architectures, so a separate model is trained for every (setting, horizon) cell. Not every baseline runs on every setting. D3-Net, DeepLTMLE, and DeepACE are hardwired to a single binary treatment (their propensity head, inverse propensity ratio, and the debiasing objec-tive all assume single-dimensional treatment), so they are evaluated only on the single-treatment MIMIC settings. On the settings with a history-dependent target policy, only IGC-Net, D3-Net, and DeepLTMLE are run: the encoder–decoder models condition on a fixed treatment sequence and cannot take a target policy that depends on the evolving state. Cells that a method cannot serve are left blank in the results tables. D.3 HYPERPARAMETER TUNING AND EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

For every baseline, we tune its hyperparameters with Optuna (Akiba et al., 2019) for 2 hours on one A100 GPU, using one held-out tuning task drawn from the same generator as, and disjoint from, the evaluation tasks. The selection criterion is held-out factual RMSE: counterfactual labels are never used for model or hyperparameter selection. Hyperparameters are tuned once per setting for the encoder–decoder models, and once per (setting, horizon) cell for ICE G-computation-based methods. For instance, IGC-Net, uses 5 × 2 = 10 hours of tuning per setting. The search spaces of every baseline are listed in Table S.4. The selected hyperparameters are then fixed, and a fresh model is trained on each of the 20 evalua-tion tasks. Every task contains 2000 trajectories, split into 1000 training trajectories and 1000 test trajectories. The baseline is trained on the training half, and scored on the Monte-Carlo simulated CAPO on the test half. This is the same split our model uses: the training half is its in-context set, and the test half its query set, so every method sees identical data. D.4 RUNTIME MEASUREMENT 

We additionally compare wall-clock deployment cost, using a dedicated single-GPU timing bench-mark that reuses each model’s already-tuned HPs (so HP tuning time itself is excluded). PICLE-PFN’s cost is inference-only: forward passes of the single fixed checkpoint over a task’s query tra-jectories, with no gradient update. We repeated five forward passes and report the mean. Baselines’ cost is training plus inference: each train/inference call is likewise repeated 5 times and averaged. The architectures of CRN, TE-CDE, CT, RMSN, G-Net are not horizon-specific, so they require one training and 5 inferences, one for each target horizon, matching how they are evaluated. Their runtime is divided over five to represent the average runtime for each target horizon. D.5 REAL -WORLD MIMIC PREDICTION 

We follow the cohort construction and data extraction procedures of Hess et al. (2026), to which we refer readers for additional details, and define a factual outcome prediction task as follows. 

Cohorts and Data We extract each patient’s hourly trajectory over the first 11 hours. Missing values are imputed by forward- and backward-filling within each trajectory, and patients with any remaining missing outcome or covariate values are excluded. We then sample ten cohorts of n =3000 patients each, without replacement within each cohort. Each cohort is split by row order into 22 Preprint 1800 training, 600 validation, and 600 test patients. All cohorts and splits are generated once and shared across methods. We consider two treatments: vasopressor use and mechanical ventilation. The outcome is hourly mean diastolic blood pressure (DBP, mmHg). Time-varying covariates comprise DBP and the 25 vital signs and laboratory measurements selected by Hess et al. (2026) ( dL = 26 ). Static covariates comprise gender, ethnicity, and age. 

Prediction task. We evaluate multi-step prediction of factual outcomes under the behavior policy. ## E COMPARISON WITH CAUSAL LONG PFN 

Concurrent work, CausalLongPFN (Zare et al., 2026), also uses a PFN to estimate CAPO. However, their method only supports static treatment sequences, whereas PICLE-PFN supports both static and dynamic treatment policies. More importantly, their approach, in general, do not recover the multi-step CAPO. For a prediction horizon greater than one, CausalLongPFN recursively applies a one-step out-come predictor. At each step from the start to the end time step, it inserts the predicted outcome mean into the next outcome history while leaving future time-varying covariates masked. This plug-in rollout does not, in general, identify the multi-step CAPO. To see this, consider a simpli-fied setting where the only time-varying confounder is the intermediate outcome itself, yielding the trajectory O = ( B, A 1, Y 2, A 2, Y 3). Support a target treatment sequence is (a1, a 2). At the second step, CausalLongPFN’s one-step predictor models the conditional distribution of Y3 given 

(B, A 1, Y 2, A 2). Suppose the one-step predictor perfectly recovers the true conditional mean: 

g(b, a 1, y 2, a 2) = E[Y3 | B = b, A 1 = a1, Y 2 = y2, A 2 = a2].

The critical flaw in their one-step mean rollout approach is that, it inserts the first-step predicted mean by2 = E[Y2 | B = b, A 1 = a1] and returns g(b, a 1, by2, a 2). In general, 

E[g(b, a 1, Y 2, a 2) | B = b, A 1 = a1]̸ = g(b, a 1, E[Y2 | B = b, A 1 = a1], a 2).

Thus, even with exact one-step conditional means, mean plug-in rollout does not generally recover the multi-step CAPO. Additionally, the released CausalLongPFN checkpoint supports at most 10 time-varying covariates and a maximum prediction horizon of five. Our MIMIC benchmark contains 27 time-varying covari-ates and is therefore incompatible with its input interface. Consequently, among our benchmarks, we can evaluate CausalLongPFN only on cancer and restrict the comparison to τmax = 5 . This benchmark is less sensitive to the flaw in their one-step mean rollout approach, because it only has one time-varying confounder, which is the outcome itself in the intermediate step. Table S.6 shows that CausalLongPFN achieves lower RMSE at τ ∈ { 1, 2}, whereas PICLE-PFN achieves lower RMSE at longer horizons. This pattern is consistent with the limitation of mean plug-in rollout discussed above. CausalLongPFN also requires substantially longer inference time at every evaluated horizon. Two things of its method contribute to this cost. First, multi-step prediction requires multiple forward pass because CausalLongPFN needs sequential one-step model evaluations, whereas PICLE-PFN estimates all supported horizons in a single forward pass. Second, its context tokens attend to the query token, making their representations query-dependent. Consequently, the full context set is re-encoded for each query trajectory at every rollout step, rather than being encoded once and reused across queries.
