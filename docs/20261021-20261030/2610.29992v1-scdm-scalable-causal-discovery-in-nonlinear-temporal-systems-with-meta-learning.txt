# Abstract 

Causal discovery from nonlinear multivariate time series is challenging in high-dimensional systems, where the number of candidate directed relations grows quadratically with the number of variables. Existing methods are often limited by target-wise model fitting, repeated conditional testing, or expensive graph-search procedures. We propose SCDM, a shared-parameter meta-learning framework for scalable temporal causal discovery. SCDM treats each target variable as a task while learning a shared temporal predictor and a shared causal-strength matrix across tasks. Each meta-episode updates only a subset of target-variable tasks, allowing the model to accumulate structural evidence across the full system without exhaustive target-wise optimization. After training, a post-training graph readout converts the learned predictor into continuous directed causal scores for threshold-independent evaluation. We also provide a finite-error recovery principle showing that, under fully observed delayed-system assumptions and a positive risk-gap condition, thresholding SCDM scores recovers the graph when statistical, meta-optimization, and readout errors are below the population margin. Experiments on controlled synthetic, neuroimaging-inspired, high-dimensional, and real-world temporal benchmarks demonstrate competitive causal recovery and improved scalability in large temporal systems.

# 1 Introduction 

Causal discovery from multivariate time series seeks directed dependencies in systems such as brain activity, financial markets, and traffic networks. Temporal ordering supports predictive causal analysis through the Granger framework [Granger, 1969, Lütkepohl, 2005]. For d observed variables, however, the candidate cross-variable edge space contains d(d − 1) ordered pairs, so nonlinear prediction, target-wise fitting, and repeated conditional tests become costly in high-dimensional systems [Runge et al., 2019, Runge, 2020]. Existing methods address parts of this challenge through structured sparsity, temporal convolutions, self-explaining predictors, perturbation-based scores, or coarse-to-fine discovery [Tank et al., 2021, Nauta et al., 2019, Marcinkeviˇ cs and Vogt, 2021, Bi et al., 2025, Cheng et al., 2024]. Many of them still require target-specific optimization or repeated graph evaluation, which becomes limiting when the edge space contains millions of directed pairs. The question is therefore how to share nonlinear prediction across targets while keeping target-specific structural evidence. We propose Scalable Causal Discovery with Meta-Learning (SCDM), a shared-parameter framework for nonlinear temporal causal discovery. Inspired by gradient-based meta-learning [Finn et al., 2017,

Nichol et al., 2018, Yang et al., 2022], SCDM treats the prediction of each target variable as a related task in the same dynamical system. It learns a shared nonlinear temporal predictor and a shared causal-strength matrix. Each meta-episode samples a small batch of target-variable tasks, performs temporary support adaptation, and updates the shared parameters through query losses. Repeated over episodes, this procedure accumulates structural evidence across variables while keeping each update tied to the sampled task batch rather than the full set of targets. This target-task subsampled meta-training is the main scalability mechanism of SCDM. SCDM separates training from graph extraction. After meta-training, a post-training perturbation-style readout, related to recent perturbation-based dynamic causal scoring [Bi et al., 2025], converts the trained predictor into continuous directed source–target scores. This readout is a graph-extraction interface rather than the main training contribution. We also provide a finite-error recovery principle for the fully observed delayed temporal setting: if statistical, meta-optimization, and readout errors are smaller than the population risk-gap margin, thresholding the learned scores separates causal parents from non-parents. We evaluate SCDM on controlled nonlinear benchmarks with known ground-truth graphs, The Virtual Brain (TVB)-inspired neuroimaging simulations [Sanz Leon et al., 2013], a high-dimensional voxel-level functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) benchmark, and the real-world LargeST traffic benchmark [Liu et al., 2023]. On controlled benchmarks, SCDM is competitive with strong baselines rather than uniformly best. Its main advantage appears at large scale: it gives a better runtime–accuracy tradeoff on 1,000 variables and finishes on 3,000 variables with far less time and memory. Two ablations separate the roles of the two training components: target-task subsampling lowers training cost without hurting recovery, and the support/query meta-objective yields shared parameters that adapt better to held-out targets than pooled multi-task training. The main contributions are: • We formulate nonlinear temporal causal discovery as a shared-parameter episodic meta-learning problem. Each target variable defines one prediction task, and all tasks share one temporal predictor and one causal-strength matrix, so no separate model is fitted for each target. • We separate two training components with different roles. Target-task subsampling sets how many target tasks each update touches ( B instead of d), which controls training cost. The support/query objective trains the shared parameters to perform well after a few target-specific steps, which pooled multi-task training does not do. We test each component with its own ablation. • We give a finite-error recovery result for fully observed delayed temporal systems, and evaluate SCDM on controlled, neuroimaging-inspired, voxel-level ( d = 1000 ), and real-world ( d = 3000 ) benchmarks, together with stress tests for collinear sources and withheld variables. 

# 2 Related Work 

Temporal causal discovery. Temporal causal discovery exploits lagged dependencies to infer directed relations in multivariate time series. Classical Granger causality defines directed influence by whether one variable’s history improves another variable’s prediction [Granger, 1969], with VAR mod-els providing a standard linear implementation [Lütkepohl, 2005]. VARLiNGAM further combines vector autoregression with non-Gaussianity assumptions for structural identification [Hyvärinen et al., 2010]. Constraint-based methods such as PCMCI and PCMCI+ use conditional independence and momentary conditional independence tests for autocorrelated time series [Runge et al., 2019, Runge, 2020], with related work studying time-series-specific assumptions and conditional independence structures [Entner and Hoyer, 2010, Peters et al., 2014, Malinsky and Spirtes, 2018]. These methods provide important foundations, but their linear, component-wise, or repeated-testing structures can become costly in nonlinear high-dimensional systems where the candidate edge space scales as 

d(d − 1) . SCDM keeps the predictive interpretation of temporal causality while replacing repeated target-wise fitting or testing with a shared meta-learned temporal predictor and causal-strength matrix. 

Neural temporal causal discovery. Neural methods improve nonlinear modeling capacity by re-placing linear predictors with expressive sequence models. Neural Granger causality uses component-2wise predictors with structured sparsity [Tank et al., 2021]; GVAR uses self-explaining neural networks for nonlinear and time-varying Granger effects [Marcinkeviˇ cs and Vogt, 2021]; and TCDF combines temporal convolutions, attention-based interpretation, and causal validation [Nauta et al., 2019]. More general neural causal discovery methods use differentiable graph learning, continuous optimization, and score-based discovery for nonlinear systems [Zheng et al., 2018, Kalainathan et al., 2018, Ng et al., 2022, Zhang et al., 2024, 2025]. Recent perturbation-based methods such as UnCLe estimate dynamic causal effects through uncoupled temporal modeling and perturbation-based causal scoring [Bi et al., 2025], related to occlusion-style attribution under input removal or perturbation [Zeiler and Fergus, 2014, Ribeiro et al., 2016, Shrikumar et al., 2017]. SCDM is related to these methods through nonlinear prediction and continuous graph scoring, but uses perturbation-style scoring only as a post-training readout; its main scalability mechanism is shared episodic meta-training across target-variable tasks. 

Causal inference scalability and meta-learning. Existing scalable strategies include sparsity regu-larization and fast sparse solvers [Ren et al., 2020, Ren and Qian, 2024], variable-wise decomposition, screening [Ren et al., 2017, Ren, 2017], and coarse-to-fine search; for example, neural Granger methods use structured sparsity [Tank et al., 2021], while CUTS+ targets high-dimensional irregular time series through coarse-to-fine discovery and message-passing prediction modules [Cheng et al., 2024]. SCDM instead formulates target-variable prediction as a family of related tasks. Inspired by MAML and first-order meta-learning [Finn et al., 2017, Nichol et al., 2018], and related meta-learning applications in few-shot learning, optimization, and structured prediction [Vinyals et al., 2016, Ravi and Larochelle, 2017, Yao et al., 2019], SCDM samples a subset of target-variable tasks per episode and updates a shared temporal predictor together with a shared causal-strength matrix through query losses. This support/query formulation is used not for few-shot classification, but as a scalable mechanism for accumulating structural evidence across variables without updating all target tasks at every iteration.

4.2 Post-training Graph Score Readout 

After meta-training, SCDM converts the learned shared predictor and causal-strength parameterization into directed source–target scores through a post-training graph readout. This readout is not part of the optimization objective; it is the graph-extraction interface for the trained meta-predictor. We use two readouts. The source-isolated readout below measures how much each candidate source alone helps the trained predictor. The structural readout uses |Wj,i | directly; it needs no extra model evaluation, which makes it the only affordable choice when d ≥ 1000 . Each main-text recovery table states its readout, and Appendix B.3 compares both on the same trained models. The source-isolated readout is defined as follows. We analyze the fully observed non-latent setting to characterize when the scores produced by SCDM recover the delayed causal graph. The analysis follows the standard risk-gap view of temporal causal discovery and is stated under delayed causal sufficiency, correct lag specification, and sufficient regularity of the time-series process. Under these conditions, thresholding the learned scores recovers the graph whenever the statistical, meta-optimization, and readout errors are smaller than the population separation margin. Theorem 4.1 separates identifiability from finite-error estimation. The margin ∆min belongs to the delayed data-generating process, while SCDM enters through the meta-optimization and readout residuals. Task-subsampled meta-training is the estimation procedure, and correct recovery still requires these residuals to be small. The theorem gives no comparative guarantee between meta-training and other training objectives; Section 5.5 compares them empirically. The proof and statistical-rate instantiation are given in Appendix A. Causal sufficiency is the assumption most likely to fail in practice. If an unobserved variable drives both j and i, the history of j carries information about that hidden driver, so removing j raises the risk of target i even when j / ∈ Pa( i). Then S⋆ji > 0 for a non-edge, the margin condition fails, and the theorem gives no guarantee for that pair. When the structural readout |Wj,i | is used, ϵreadout is the gap between |Wj,i | and the ablation score, which we do not bound; for that readout the theorem states a condition rather than a guarantee.

Baselines. We compare six temporal discovery methods. UnCLe uses uncoupled temporal mod-eling and perturbation-based scoring [Bi et al., 2025]; PCMCI uses conditional-independence test-ing [Runge et al., 2019]; TCDF combines temporal convolution, attention, and validation [Nauta et al., 2019]. The 1,000-variable study compares against UnCLe and CUTS+, which output continuous scores and save intermediate checkpoints and can therefore be evaluated at matched wall-clock budgets (Appendix B.3). 

Evaluation metrics. For datasets with known ground-truth graphs, we evaluate causal discovery using AUROC and AUPRC as threshold-independent metrics. We additionally report F1 and SHD for thresholded graphs in the appendix. Unless otherwise stated, self-edges are excluded from graph-level evaluation because they correspond to autoregressive self-dependencies rather than cross-variable causal discovery. For real-world LargeST experiments, where a definitive causal graph is unavailable, we report runtime, peak memory, forecasting error, and qualitative learned-graph visualizations rather than causal AUROC or AUPRC. 

Runtime and implementation protocol. Runtime is measured as wall-clock time under a fixed hardware and software environment. Unless otherwise stated, runtime includes model training and causal score extraction, but excludes dataset preprocessing and figure generation. For high-dimensional experiments, we additionally record peak GPU memory when available. A method is marked as OOM if it fails under the specified memory budget, and as timeout if it does not complete within the fixed wall-clock limit. Detailed hyperparameters, compute resources, thresholding rules, and runtime measurement details are provided in Appendix B.1 and Appendix B.3.

5.5 Ablation Study 

We run two ablations, one for each training component described in Section 4.1. 

Target-task subsampling. All variants use the same full method, dataset, optimizer, number of outer updates, and graph-score evaluation; only the number of target tasks B sampled per meta-episode changes. We report training time only, because the post-training readout is identical across variants and does not measure the cost of meta-training. A smaller per-update cost could in principle be offset by needing more updates, so we fix the number of outer updates and compare total training time. Table 4 shows that B = 32 cuts total training time by 3.1× relative to all-target updates (B = 100 ) and also gives the best recovery. 

Support/query meta-objective. Subsampling could also be combined with ordinary multi-task training, so the ablation above does not show what the support/query objective adds. We hold out a subset of target variables from training and train three variants with the same architecture, data, initialization, and budget: full SCDM, an episodic variant without inner adaptation ( K = 0 ), and pooled multi-task training on sampled targets (Joint MTL). All three then adapt to each held-out target with the same test-time protocol (Appendix B.3). The shared matrix W also matters for recovery: removing it lowers AUPRC from 0.728 to 0.390 (Appendix B.3).

# 6 Conclusion 

We introduced SCDM, a shared-parameter meta-learning framework for scalable causal discovery in nonlinear temporal systems. By treating target-variable prediction as a collection of related tasks, SCDM learns a shared temporal predictor and causal-strength matrix while updating only a subset of target tasks per meta-episode. Ablations show that target-task subsampling lowers training cost without hurting recovery and that the support/query objective gives shared parameters that adapt better than pooled multi-task training. SCDM is competitive on controlled benchmarks and gives a better runtime–accuracy tradeoff at 1,000 and 3,000 variables. SCDM remains an observational method with clear limits. Its recovery result assumes no hidden common causes, a correct lag order, and no instantaneous effects; a hidden driver of several observed variables can create spurious edges, and our latent-variable experiments (Appendix C) are a controlled diagnostic rather than a general solution. Individual edge scores can also move to correlated non-parents when sources are nearly redundant (Appendix B.3). This condition should be interpreted as a sufficient recovery regime rather than a sharp sample-complexity characterization. It shows that graph recovery is controlled jointly by temporal sample size, function-class complexity, graph dimension through a union bound over d2 candidate edges, and the quality of meta-optimization and perturbation readout. The theorem is stated in terms of an ideal ablation risk-gap target because this quantity has a direct population interpretation: it measures how much optimal prediction risk increases when the source history of variable j is removed. The implemented method avoids retraining a separate restricted predictor for every ordered pair (j, i ). Instead, after training a shared temporal predictor, it computes a post-training perturbation score that measures how the target prediction changes when source-specific historical information is removed or neutralized. This distinction is important in high dimensions. Explicitly fitting all restricted models would require 

O(d2) model refits, which is incompatible with voxel-level or traffic-scale systems. The perturbation readout is therefore used as a scalable approximation to the same ablation principle. The residual 

ϵreadout explicitly records this approximation gap. In the infinite-data, well-specified, and fully optimized limit, if the perturbation operation faithfully removes the same source-specific information as the ideal ablation operator, then ϵreadout → 0. In finite samples, the residual depends on predictor quality, optimization quality, score normalization, and the fidelity of the perturbation operator. Finally, the identifiability component of the theorem comes from the delayed causal system and the population risk margin, not from meta-learning itself. Meta-learning enters through the finite-error term ϵmeta (N, B, K ). In the proposed framework, each target variable defines a task, and all tasks share the same temporal predictor and causal-strength parameterization. The support/query split encourages the shared parameters to adapt efficiently to target-specific prediction tasks, while the outer update aggregates evidence across a mini-batch of target variables. Compared with fitting independent target-specific models, this shared episodic training can reduce adaptation error and optimization error in finite samples. The theory captures this effect through the condition that ϵmeta 

becomes small enough relative to the population margin ∆min .Thus, the theorem does not claim that meta-learning alone identifies causal structure. Rather, under standard delayed-system identifiability conditions, meta-learning provides a scalable optimization mechanism for approaching the population risk-gap score in high-dimensional temporal systems.

Meta-learning. SCDM uses first-order MAML with K = 3 inner gradient steps per task. The inner learning rate is αθ = 0 .01 for the predictor and αW = 0 .001 for W ; the smaller rate keeps a single task from moving the shared graph too far during adaptation. The outer optimizer is Adam with learning rate β = 0 .001 and weight decay 10 −5, and gradients are clipped to unit ℓ2 norm before each outer update.

Threshold selection and evaluation. The binary adjacency matrix is obtained by sweeping 200 evenly-spaced candidate thresholds between the minimum and maximum score on the validation split and selecting the threshold that maximizes the F1 score on the validation set. B.2 Dataset Overview and Evaluation Protocols 

This subsection gives a compact overview of the benchmark suite and evaluation protocol. Detailed simulation construction choices, including graph-generation rules, dynamics, observation models, and recoverability diagnostics, are provided separately in Appendix D. 

Benchmark overview. NC8 is a low-dimensional nonlinear temporal benchmark with eight ob-served variables and a known lagged directed graph. It is used to verify causal recovery in a small controlled setting and to support the masked-NC8 latent diagnostic. The simulated fMRI benchmark is a TVB-inspired, atlas-grounded BOLD-like simulation over 100 Schaefer cortical ROIs. It com-bines Yeo-network organization, delayed nonlinear ROI interactions, and HRF-based observation effects while retaining a known directed ground-truth graph. The simulated EEG benchmark is a source-space EEG-like simulation over the same Schaefer-100 ROI scale. It uses network-modular directed source interactions, EEG-like oscillatory components, and a simplified 64-channel source-to-sensor projection for qualitative visualization. The voxel-level fMRI benchmark scales the controlled setting to 1000 observed variables and nearly one million candidate directed pairs, preserving syn-thetic ground truth for runtime–accuracy evaluation. LargeST-GLA is a real-world traffic benchmark without complete directed causal ground truth; it is therefore used for scalability, forecasting, and qualitative structural analysis rather than causal AUROC/AUPRC.

When threshold-dependent metrics such as F1 and SHD are reported, the threshold is selected on the validation split and applied once to the test split. Readout ablation. Collinearity stress test. The source-isolated score measures predictive contribution, so it can fail when two sources carry nearly the same information. We build NC8-Collinear, which adds a common driver X0 with edges X0 → X1 and X0 → X2, a true edge X1 → X3, and no edge X2 → X3.

X2 is therefore a correlated non-parent that can stand in for X1. We vary the driver strength a

At a = 0 the edges from X0 carry no signal but remain in the ground truth, which explains the lower AUPRC at that level. Conclusions about individual edges should therefore be treated with care when observed sources are nearly redundant.

C.4 Interpretation and Limitations 

The latent experiments should be interpreted as a controlled extension study rather than the central contribution of the paper. The main SCDM framework targets fully observed scalable temporal causal discovery, and the main empirical claims are based on non-latent causal recovery, high-dimensional scalability, and real-world LargeST evaluation. The latent-augmented setting introduces explicit hidden nodes, but the learned latent coordinates are not identifiable in an absolute sense. They may be permuted, rescaled, or transformed without changing the observed prediction objective. Therefore, we do not claim recovery of the exact latent trajectories. Instead, we evaluate whether the model can assign useful structural scores to latent-to-observed influence under a controlled masking protocol where the withheld variables and full graph are known for evaluation. These results provide supplementary evidence that the shared-parameter meta-learning framework can be extended beyond the fully observed setting. The masked NC8 experiment provides an interpretable proof of concept for hidden-to-observed recovery, while the masked simulated EEG and fMRI experiments evaluate observed-block recovery in higher-dimensional temporal systems. However, unrestricted latent causal discovery under arbitrary hidden confounding, instantaneous effects, or misspecified latent dimension remains outside the scope of this work.

D.3 Simulated EEG Benchmark 

The simulated EEG benchmark is an atlas-grounded source-space EEG-like simulation with known directed ground truth. Each variable corresponds to a cortical source ROI from the Schaefer-100 parcellation, and the directed graph follows a network-modular prior based on the Yeo-7 func-tional systems. D.4 High-dimensional Voxel-level fMRI Benchmark 

The voxel-level fMRI benchmark is designed to evaluate causal discovery in a substantially larger controlled system than the ROI-level simulated fMRI benchmark. While the ROI-level benchmark contains 100 Schaefer cortical ROIs, the voxel-level benchmark contains d = 1000 observed variables arranged on a 10 × 10 × 10 spatial grid. This yields d(d − 1) = 999 ,000 candidate directed off-diagonal relations, allowing us to stress-test scalability in a regime where exhaustive target-wise modeling becomes expensive. Unlike LargeST, the voxel-level fMRI benchmark includes a complete synthetic directed ground-truth graph, so it supports AUROC and AUPRC evaluation in addition to runtime and enrichment analysis. The benchmark should be interpreted as a controlled high-dimensional BOLD-like temporal simula-tion rather than a full biophysical simulator of empirical voxel-level fMRI acquisition. The generated variables are organized into spatial regions, with local lagged dependencies between neighboring voxels and additional long-range region-to-region influences. The simulation also includes latent regional drivers and nonlinear lagged dynamics, followed by fMRI-like observation effects such as hemodynamic response kernels, downsampling, and observation noise. Stability is controlled through global inflow scaling rather than an explicitly named spectral-radius rescaling step. The purpose of this benchmark is therefore to preserve a known directed temporal graph while increasing the dimensionality to a voxel-scale setting. For the high-dimensional results reported in this paper, we use the voxel1000_v2 version of the benchmark. This version contains T = 800 time points and a sparse directed observed graph with

D.5 Recoverability and Evaluation Scope 

The simulation datasets are intended to support controlled causal recovery evaluation with known directed ground truth. They should not be interpreted as fully realistic biomedical simulators. Instead, they provide structured temporal systems that combine recoverable directed dependencies, domain-inspired organization, and controlled access to ground-truth graphs. The low-dimensional NC8 benchmark validates whether a method can recover directed temporal dependencies in a small nonlinear system. The simulated fMRI and simulated EEG benchmarks evaluate recovery in medium-dimensional neuroimaging-inspired systems with atlas-based organiza-tion and known directed graphs. The voxel-level fMRI benchmark increases the number of variables to stress-test runtime, memory, and high-dimensional score extraction. Together, these simulations separate causal recovery evaluation, scalability analysis, and real-world qualitative assessment, while avoiding unsupported claims about exact biological realism. 

# E Broader Impacts 

SCDM is a scalable causal discovery framework for nonlinear temporal systems. By reducing the cost of learning directed dependency scores in high-dimensional time series, the method may support more efficient scientific analysis and decision-making in domains such as neuroscience, traffic systems, finance, climate, and engineering monitoring. In particular, scalable temporal causal discovery can help researchers explore structured dynamic relations in settings where exhaustive target-wise modeling or repeated conditional testing is computationally expensive. However, the outputs of SCDM should be interpreted with caution. The method is based on observa-tional temporal data and relies on assumptions such as delayed causal influence, sufficient observation of relevant variables, correct lag specification, and informative predictive risk gaps. If these as-sumptions are violated, learned directed scores may reflect predictive association rather than true 36 causal mechanisms. Misuse of such scores in high-stakes settings, such as clinical decision-making, public policy, infrastructure control, or financial intervention, could lead to misleading conclusions or harmful actions. Therefore, SCDM should be used as a tool for hypothesis generation and structural analysis, and its findings should be validated with domain knowledge, controlled interventions, or external evidence whenever possible. 37 NeurIPS Paper Checklist
