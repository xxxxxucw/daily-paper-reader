## Abstract 

Residual-based causal discovery assumes that each variable is generated from its parents and an independent noise source. It estimates this noise and tests its independence from candidate causes. For identifiable functions such as additive-noise models (ANMs) and the broader class of location-scale noise models (LSNMs), this prin-ciple identifies the causal direction, as only the causal model admits this independence. Existing LSNM methods rely on parametric assumptions to obtain standardized residuals, risking model mis-specification. In contrast, the RESIT-framework allows for nonparametric estimation of residuals to iteratively identify sink nodes, but was pro-posed for ANMs. To close this gap, we propose LOSCAR , an extension of RESIT to LSNMs that estimates the conditional mean and scale via quan-tile regression, enabling pretrained tabular foun-dation models as plug-in estimators. We com-pare it with a feasible generalized least squares (FGLS) variant, which is consistent given suitable basis functions. Across bivariate and multivariate causal-discovery benchmarks, in-context quantile regression substantially outperforms FGLS with flexible function classes, particularly for multi-variate causes.

## 2. Residual-Based Causal Discovery 

Below we describe the framework for residual-based causal discovery. First we review LSNMs, which include ANMs as a special case, and their identification results. Then, we discuss the basis of bivariate causal discovery via residual-based independence testing and how these ideas extend to the multivariate setting.

2.2. Cause-Effect Inference by Residual-Based Independence Testing 

We start by describing the bivariate setting in which we call the two involved random variables X and Y . If the data has been generated according to an LSNM encoding the graph 

X → Y , i.e., Y := f (X) + g(X)NY , it holds that, by assumption, (Y − f (X)) /g (X) = NY is independent of X.If the LSNM is identifiable, such an independence relation does not exist for the anti-causal direction, i.e., we cannot find functions h and k such that Y is independent of NX =(X −h(Y )) /k (Y ). for ANMs. As in ANMs g(·) ≡ 1, RESIT approximates the noise terms by the residuals obtained through standard regression for both hypothetical causal models and decides for the model which does not reject the null hypothesis of independence between noise and hypothesized cause. If independence is rejected in both causal directions, the direction with the higher p-value is selected in cases where a decision is forced. 1

In LSNMs, the noise is typically extracted through estimat-ing f and g. When the noise follows a standard Gaussian distribution, Immer et al. In this paper, we build upon the result of Sun & Schulte (2023), who show that consistently estimating mean and standard deviation is sufficient. 2.3. Multivariate Causal Discovery 

To infer DAGs from an iid sample of a multivariate ran-dom vector X, we adapt the RESIT (Peters et al., 2014) algorithm to LSNMs as shown in Algorithm 1. We call the resulting algorithm L OSCAR (short for Location-Scale RESIT). Similar adaptations to LSNMs have been explored by Bodik & Chavez-Demoulin (2025); Khan et al. (2026). In summary, akin to the original RESIT method, Algorithm 1 consists of two phases. Phase 1 learns a causal ordering π

in which the parent set for each node k consists of all nodes that appear prior to k in the causal ordering. The causal ordering is defined as a bijective mapping π : {1, . . . , d } → {1, . . . , d } satisfying 

π(i) < π (j) if j ∈ De G

refers to the descendants of a node i. We start with an empty ordering π and a candidate set S =

{1, . . . , d }. Then, we iteratively identify sink nodes, which we add to π and remove from S until S is empty. At each step, we estimate the standardized residuals for each node 

k ∈ S by obtaining the conditional mean and standard deviation of X(k) | { X(i)}i∈S\{ k} based on one of the estimators described in Section 3. Note that both the mean and the standard deviation can be non-constant and are modeled through {X(i)}i∈S\{ k}. We omit this dependence in the notation of Algorithm 1 to avoid clutter. The sink node is then determined as the node for which the estimated 

p-value between standardized residuals and corresponding parent set {X(i)}i∈S\{ k} is largest. Phase 2 of the algorithm prunes superfluous edges for each node k by iteratively checking if removing a member of the current parent set will induce a dependence between the estimated standardized residuals and the nodes appearing before k in the ordering π.

## 3. Estimating Methods 

In this section, we describe three approaches for estimat-ing the conditional mean and scale that we employ within LOSCAR to extract residuals in LSNMs. We propose two methods that estimate the scale from a pretrained in-context quantile regression model (Section 3.1). Specifically, we use TabPFN (Hollmann et al., 2023), a tabular foundation model, to obtain estimates of the conditional mean and con-ditional quantile function. The first two approaches then recover the scale from the estimated conditional quantile function in different ways. The third approach (Section 3.2) jointly estimates the mean and variance functions via fea-sible generalized least squares, which is consistent under suitable modeling assumptions.

## 4. Related Work 

DAG Identifiability Identifiability conditions are well established for linear non-Gaussian models (Shimizu et al., 2006, LiNGAM), causal additive models (B¨ uhlmann et al., 2014, CAM), (nonlinear) ANMs (Hoyer et al., 2008), post nonlinear additive noise models (Zhang & Hyv¨ arinen, 2009, PNL), and LSNMs (Immer et al., 2023; Strobl & Lasko, 2022). Both PNL models and LSNMs include ANMs as a special case. We review LSNMs in Section 2.1. 

Quantile-Based Causal Discovery Methods that ap-proach causal discovery through quantile regression are score-based. QCCD (Tagasovska et al., 2020) employs quantile regression to derive a minimum description length (MDL) based score in line with other approaches building upon MDL-based approximations (Budhathoki & Vreeken, 2016; Marx & Vreeken, 2017) of the algorithmic Markov condition (Janzing & Sch¨ olkopf, 2010). Recent work de-rives a score based on quantile partial effects (QPE) to learn the causal order (Chen et al., 2026), but their estimation method relies on a range of sensitive hyperparameters such as the assumed QPE model or transformation functions. Here, we use in-context quantile regression for estimating conditional mean and standard deviation based on pretrained tabular foundation models which does not require further tuning. 

Causal Discovery with LSNMs Besides the work dis-cussing identifiability of LSNMs, Lin et al. (2024) derive a method for symmetric noise leveraging skewness for iden-tification. The code is, however, not publicly available. Further, HOST (Duong & Nguyen, 2023) iteratively iden-tifies source nodes assuming Gaussianity. ROCHE (Tran et al., 2024) accounts for heavy-tailed noise by modeling a t-distribution, and S KEW D (Klippert & Marx, 2026) im-plicitly models skewness by assuming a skew-normal. We include the latter three as baselines. ROCHE, however, can only be applied to the bivariate setting. 

Amortized Causal Discovery Through simulating data from causal networks during training, amortized causal dis-covery (Lorch et al., 2022) frames causal discovery as a supervised learning task (Petersen et al., 2023). By embed-ding causal assumptions into data generation and building upon the predictive capabilities of transformer models, more recent approaches show promising results for DAG learn-ing and data imputation (Thompson et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026) and learning causal graphs in the presence of confounding and non-additive models such as CDFM (Qiao et al., 2026) and F OUND CAUSE (Bl¨ obaum et al., 2026). Unlike these models, our method does not re-quire extensive training but can leverage any pretrained tab-ular foundation model that can perform quantile regression. Further, L OSCAR directly exploits identification results for LSNMs.

## 6. Conclusion 

We introduced L OSCAR , a residual-based causal discovery method for location-scale noise models with nonparamet-ric in-context quantile regression using tabular foundation models. We proposed two variants that differ in how the con-ditional scale is estimated for residual extraction and com-pared them with a consistent FGLS-based variant. Across our experiments, the quantile-based variants consistently outperform the FGLS variant and achieve state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance on established bivariate benchmarks, while remaining on par with SOTA baselines in low- to medium-dimensional settings. 

Limitations and Future Work The scalability of LOSCAR is limited by the cost of repeated model fitting and the difficulty of conditional independence testing with large conditioning sets. Moreover, its greedy search may propagate errors across successive steps. To improve ro-bustness to such errors, it would be interesting to extend LOSCAR with beam-search, as explored for RESIT by Ong et al. (2026), in future work.

A.4. Hyperparameter Configuration L OSCAR 

We implemented our method in Python, extending the implementation of RESIT provided through the lingam pack-age (Ikeuchi et al., 2023) 4 which also provides an efficient implementation of the HSIC independence criterion. For all experiments, we fix the significance threshold α used in Phase 2 of L OSCAR to 0.01 .We use TabPFN-3 (Grinsztajn et al., 2026) with model checkpoint tabpfn-v3-regressor-v3 default.ckpt as default in our experiments. Conditional mean and quantile levels are obtained through a single fit and predict sequence where the fitting step provides the data as context to the model. Quantile levels are obtained dependent on the instantiation of L OSCAR . L OSCAR estimates the scale based on the quantile difference (cf. Section 3.1.1) with τ1 = 0 .05 and τ2 = 1 − τ1

using the quantiles of the standard normal distribution for the normalization term. L OSCAR -GL selects quantiles to compute the variance integral stated in Section 3.1.2 using the Gauss-Legendre quadrature (Golub & Welsch, 1969) adjusted to the interval (0,1). This procedure deterministically selects m quantiles at which the quantile function is evaluated to compute the integral. By default we set m = 10 . We ablate both parameter choices in Appendix B.1.2. Further, for RESIT-T AB PFN we only compute the conditional mean based on TabPFN in the RESIT algorithm. For both L OSCAR -FGLS and RESIT-OLS, the mean parameters were estimated using ridge regression with a penalty of λ = 0 .0001 . For each covariate, we used an independent cubic spline basis with 8 features and linear extrapolation beyond the boundary knots. For L OSCAR -FGLS, the variance parameters were estimated using ridge regression with λ = 0 .01 , with an independent cubic spline basis of 6 features per covariate and the same linear extrapolation scheme. 

Compute and Runtime: All experiments were performed single-threaded on an NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Server Edition GPU node. Variants that did not use TabPFN were deployed on CPU (single-threaded, AMD EPYC 9655 Processor). Running a single pair of the bivariate benchmark took approximately 1 to 3 seconds. For the multivariate synthetic experiments, the runtime for d = 6 and d = 12 for a single DAG were 17 seconds and ∼ 2 minutes wall-clock time, respectively, for L OSCAR , whereas L OSCAR -GL was faster for d = 12 , with L OSCAR -FGLS being fastest, taking 

∼ 3 seconds for d = 12 . Details for the multivariate synthetic datasets are provided in Appendix B.2.4. As the real-world datasets contained more edges (especially Light Tunnel, which contained 20 nodes, 39 edges, and 10 000 data points) LOSCAR took longest to compute on these, taking ∼ 14 minutes on Sachs and ∼ 85 minutes on Light Tunnel, respectively. LOSCAR -GL terminated after ∼ 110 minutes on the Light Tunnel dataset.
