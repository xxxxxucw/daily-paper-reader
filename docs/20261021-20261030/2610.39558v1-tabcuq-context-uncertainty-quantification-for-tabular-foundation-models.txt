Abstract
Tabular foundation models use in-context learning to estimate conditional expected outcomes
and average treatment effects for specific groups of individuals. Using these estimates to guide
scientific decisions requires reliable uncertainty quantification. The challenge is to account
simultaneously for two sources of uncertainty: limited-context bias and context variation arising
from the small labeled sample supplied at inference. Predictive spread does not directly quantify
both sources of uncertainty, and we observe that the ordinary bootstrap may substantially
overestimate context variation, whereas subsampling may underestimate it.
We introduce
TabCUQ, a perturbation-based framework that injects noise into observed outcomes and applies
the foundation model to the perturbed contexts. TabCUQ estimates context variation using
wild-bootstrap dispersion and accounts for limited-context bias using the maximum deviation
between perturbed and original estimates. For a kernel-regression analogue whose estimation error
admits an exact decomposition into these two components, we show that our framework provides
asymptotically valid pointwise coverage. In simulations, TabCUQ substantially improves coverage,
with average interval widths typically about twice those of an oracle bias-aware benchmark.
Across seven real-world tasks, it also improves coverage of reference functions learned from much
larger samples.

Introduction
Scientific decisions often depend on estimates drawn from small tabular datasets. Tabular foundation
models offer a promising approach to prediction in these settings. Conventional methods, such as
generalised additive models, random forests and neural networks trained from scratch, are typically
fitted separately to each dataset [Hastie et al., 2009]. The foundation models studied here are neural
networks trained in advance on many synthetic and real datasets. This training allows the same
model to be reused for prediction tasks on new datasets. For a new task, the user supplies a small
labeled dataset containing examples of features and their observed outcomes. The model takes
these examples as part of its input and uses them to predict the outcome of a new observation
from its features, without updating its pretrained parameters. This process is known as in-context
learning. Built on this idea, tabular foundation models, including TabPFN [Hollmann et al., 2025],
TabICL [Qu et al., 2025] and TabFM [Kong and Das, 2026], have demonstrated strong predictive

estimates obtained from a small labeled dataset support a scientific conclusion?
We focus on two quantities that inform scientific decisions: the conditional mean and the
conditional average treatment effect. A chemist may need to determine whether the mean reaction
yield under specified experimental conditions exceeds a threshold for further development. Time
and material costs limit the number of experiments available to estimate this quantity [Shields
et al., 2021, Hickman et al., 2022]. The average outcome across repeated experiments under those
conditions is a conditional mean. A clinical researcher may instead ask whether a treatment shortens
average recovery time for patients with a particular clinical profile. Their average recovery time
under treatment minus their average recovery time without treatment is the conditional average
treatment effect (CATE); a negative value indicates faster recovery.
We use conditional-mean estimation to explain how a foundation model produces an estimate.
As illustrated in Figure 1, the foundation model takes two inputs: context and query. The context is
the labeled dataset containing observed features and outcomes, and the query specifies the feature
values at which the mean is to be estimated. In the chemistry example, the context contains
completed experiments and their measured yields, while the query specifies the reaction conditions
of interest. The model processes the context and query without updating its pretrained weights, a
procedure called in-context learning. We use its point prediction as an estimator of the conditional
mean. Although the prediction rule is fixed, the estimate depends on the labeled observations
supplied as context.

We distinguish two components of context uncertainty: context variation and limited-context
bias. A fresh context of the same size sampled from the target population can produce a different
estimate. Each context consists of newly sampled observations, so both features and outcomes vary.
We refer to this sampling variation as context variation. To understand the second component,
2


imagine repeatedly drawing contexts of the same size and averaging the resulting estimates at a
fixed query. Even after the sampling fluctuations are averaged out, the average estimate may still
lie above or below the true conditional mean at that query. We refer to this systematic gap as
limited-context bias.
In our experiments, TabPFN frequently exhibits such bias with small contexts,
analogous to the regularization bias commonly encountered in classic machine learning methods. In
statistical terminology, these are the sampling variability and bias of the estimator induced by the
pretrained prediction rule. The same distinction applies to a CATE estimator at a fixed clinical
profile. Panel A of figure 2 illustrates both components. Both components matter when scientific
decisions depend on an estimated mean or treatment effect. Sampling fluctuations or bias can
make an estimate appear favorable even when the true mean yield is inadequate or the treatment
benefit is negligible. A point estimate alone therefore does not establish whether the data support
the scientific conclusion. We seek confidence intervals for the conditional mean or CATE. A valid
confidence-interval procedure covers the fixed target with probability at least the stated confidence
level over repeated contexts of the same size. Such intervals help assess whether the data support a
mean yield above the development threshold or a treatment effect below zero, taking estimation
uncertainty into account.
Constructing such confidence intervals requires estimating context variation and accounting
for limited-context bias. Bootstrap and subsampling provide standard tools for approximating
sampling distributions under appropriate conditions and are natural candidates for estimating context
variation. Applying classical resampling methods to pretrained tabular foundation models raises
the question of whether they accurately capture variation across the labeled contexts supplied at
inference, while the pretrained network parameters remain fixed. Nevertheless, accurately estimating
context variation is only part of the inferential task. In nonparametric regression, estimation bias
that is non-negligible relative to the estimator’s standard deviation can impair confidence-interval
coverage, even when that standard deviation is accurately estimated [Wasserman, 2006, Section 5.7].
Pretraining does not itself guarantee negligible bias at the available context size. Through extensive
simulation and real-world studies, we observe that the Tabular foundation models tend to suffer
from an estimation bias with the limited aomunt of context. We therefore develop TabCUQ, a
perturbation-based confidence-interval construction that accounts for both context variation and
limited-context bias.
1.1
Main results and contributions
The paper makes two main contributions. First, we find empirically that wild-bootstrap perturbations
effectively estimate the scale of context variation for the pretrained tabular prediction rules evaluated
here. Interestingly, we observe that the ordinary bootstrap produces standard-error estimates
substantially larger than the true standard deviations, whereas the evaluated subsampling procedure
produces smaller estimates. These findings motivate our use of wild-bootstrap perturbations in
the uncertainty quantification of the tabular foundation model. Second, we introduce TabCUQ, a
perturbation-based confidence-interval construction for conditional means and CATEs that combines
an estimate of context variation with a characterization of limited-context bias. In a kernel-regression
setting, we prove that the proposed bias cushion bounds estimation bias and establish coverage for
the resulting interval under the stated assumptions.
TabCUQ generates perturbed contexts by applying wild-bootstrap perturbations to the observed
outcomes, while holding the features and context size fixed. We then apply the tabular foundation
model to each perturbed context to obtain the corresponding perturbed estimates. We use the
empirical standard deviation of these estimates to estimate the scale of context variation. To
characterize limited-context bias, we define a bias cushion as the largest absolute displacement of the

1.2
Relation to existing work
TabCUQ builds on classical resampling methods for inference about fixed population quantities.
The nonparametric bootstrap and subsampling approximate sampling distributions by recomputing
estimators on resampled observations [Efron, 1979, Politis et al., 1999]. Classical wild-bootstrap
methods perturb regression residuals with covariates held fixed to accommodate heteroskedastic
errors [Wu, 1986]. TabCUQ applies outcome perturbations to pretrained in-context prediction rules,
using the resulting estimates to quantify context variation and construct a margin for limited-context
bias. Its use of a bias cushion also connects the construction to bias-aware methods that calibrate
confidence intervals to accommodate estimation bias [Armstrong and Kolesár, 2016].
For pretrained models, posterior methods offer another approach to uncertainty quantification.
PFNs were introduced to approximate Bayesian posterior predictive distributions under a specified
prior over tasks [Müller et al., 2022]. Predictive intervals from these distributions concern future
responses and include outcome noise. Posterior methods can also target uncertainty about conditional
means and other functionals. The approximate martingale posterior method of Nagler and Rügamer
[2025] uses PFN-initialised predictive distributions and copula updates to quantify uncertainty
about conditional means, quantiles and other summaries at a fixed query. The authors motivate
learning-rate choices that widen credible intervals to accommodate nonparametric bias. TabMGP
[Ng et al., 2026] constructs martingale posteriors for parameters defined as minimisers of an expected
loss. Fortini et al. [2026] use a predictive central limit theorem to obtain Gaussian approximations to
posterior uncertainty about conditional event probabilities under a limiting predictive distribution.
These approaches provide posterior uncertainty for a range of targets using pretrained models. In
contrast, our proposal assesses the uncertainty from the frequentist perspective, which has distinct
properties and different application scenarios from posterior credibility. Our method constructs
intervals by perturbing the observed outcomes and repeatedly evaluating the original point estimator.
We assess their coverage at a fixed query over repeated draws of the labeled context.
Conformal prediction constructs prediction sets for future responses [Shafer and Vovk, 2008],
whereas TabCUQ constructs confidence intervals for conditional means and CATEs. Under ex-
changeability, standard split conformal prediction guarantees marginal coverage [Lei et al., 2018];
coverage at a particular query can still fall below the nominal level [Barber et al., 2021]. For
tabular foundation models, C-USIM [Park et al., 2026] uses held-out observations to calibrate
highest predictive density regions and studies the gap between conditional and marginal coverage.
In causal inference, Lei and Candès [2021] develop conformal methods for counterfactual outcomes
and individual treatment effects. An individual treatment effect is the difference between a person’s
two potential outcomes; CATE is the conditional expectation of this difference given the features.
These are different inferential targets: even valid coverage for outcomes or individual effects at a
fixed query does not by itself establish confidence-interval coverage for their conditional means.

2
Uncertainty Quantification for Tabular Foundation Model
Uncertainty quantification is essential when predictions from tabular foundation models inform
decisions based on small- to medium-sized datasets. A positive estimate of a conditional mean or
treatment effect does not, by itself, establish that the underlying quantity is positive. The estimated
sign may reflect limited-context bias or context variation. We first review tabular foundation models
in Section 2.1 and then discuss the need for uncertainty quantification and the challenges involved
in the remainder of this section.
2.1
Basics of Tabular Foundation Models
We review prior-data fitted networks (PFNs), which provide the methodological foundation for
TabPFN [Müller et al., 2022]. PFNs are pretrained on synthetic datasets and subsequently applied
to new datasets at prediction time. Let ϕ index a data-generating rule, with prior density π(ϕ).
To construct each synthetic dataset, the simulator first samples ϕ ∼π and then generates labeled
observations independently from the sampled rule, conditional on ϕ. The observations are randomly
divided into a labeled context and held-out queries. For each query, the network receives the context
together with the query features and predicts the corresponding outcome label. The query labels
are withheld from the network inputs and used only to compute the training loss.
Let D denote the labeled context and (Xtest, Ytest) a held-out query, where Xtest and Ytest are
its features and outcome label, respectively. A PFN with parameters θ outputs a predictive density
qθ(y | x, D) for the outcome label of a query with features x, given context D. The population
pretraining objective is
L(θ) = Eϕ∼πED,(Xtest,Ytest)|ϕ [−log qθ(Ytest | Xtest, D)] .
(1)
In practice, we sample synthetic datasets using the procedure described above and approximate the
expectation in (1) by an empirical average of the losses on the sampled synthetic datasets. This
empirical loss is used to update the network parameters θ, with new synthetic datasets sampled
throughout training.
We follow Müller et al. [2022] and connect the pretraining objective in (1) to the Bayesian
posterior predictive density. Let pπ(y | x, D) denote the Bayesian posterior predictive density of the
query outcome with the prior π, admitting the following expression

network class, finite pretraining data, and imperfect optimization.
Conventional Bayesian methods typically obtain the predictive density by approximating this
posterior distribution pπ(ϕ | x, D) for each context and then applying (2) to learn the posterior
outcome distribution. In contrast, PFNs are based on directly using qθ(y | x, D) to approximate
the posterior predictive density pπ(y | x, D) through pretraining on simulated datasets, without
explicitly constructing the posterior over ϕ or evaluating the integral in (2) at prediction time.

2.3
Challenges of Uncertainty Quantification for Tabular Foundation Models
To support scientific conclusions about conditional means and CATEs, we construct confidence
intervals by bounding the estimation error. We focus on the conditional mean to illustrate the
main idea and the same construction applies to CATE. A confidence interval provides a range of
plausible values for the average outcome at x in regression. For a fixed query x and significance
level α ∈(0, 1), we seek a data-dependent bound Rα(Dn; x) satisfying
P{|bµ(x) −µ(x)| ≤Rα(Dn; x)} ≥1 −α.
Using this bound as the radius gives the 1 −α confidence interval
Cα(Dn; x) = [bµ(x) −Rα(Dn; x), bµ(x) + Rα(Dn; x)] .
Its width indicates estimation precision, and an interval entirely above or below a scientifically
relevant benchmark supports the corresponding conclusion about µ(x). One of the most commonly
used confidence interval is the Wald interval [Wasserman, 2004], which uses an estimated standard
error multiplied by a normal critical value as its radius:

We next discuss possible reasons why ordinary bootstrap and subsampling misestimate context
variation in our experiments. Ordinary bootstrap reuses the observed data, with some observations
repeated and others omitted.
TabPFN may respond differently to these repetitions than to
new observations, so the bootstrap variation may not match the variation we want to estimate.
Subsampling uses smaller contexts. We rescale its standard-error estimates to account for the
smaller size, but the adjusted estimates still underestimate context variation in our experiments. For
9


Wald intervals, overestimating the standard error produces wider intervals, whereas underestimating
it can lead to insufficient coverage even when the point estimator’s bias is small.
Accurately estimating context variation does not, however, address limited-context bias. When
this bias is large relative to the standard error, a Wald interval may fail to attain nominal coverage
even if the standard error is accurately estimated. Since the population target is unknown, the bias
cannot be determined directly from the observed labeled context. In the next section, we therefore
seek an interval construction that accounts for both context variation and limited-context bias.

Method

empirical bias cushion for limited-context bias. We design the particular perturbation or resampling
method in the following Section 3.1. We develop the confidence interval construction for a conditional
mean in Section 3.2, then extend it to a conditional average treatment effect in Section 3.3.

For average treatment effects, semiparametric methods based on influence functions allow valid
inference with flexible estimates of nuisance functions under suitable conditions [Hines et al., 2022].
With continuously distributed covariates, however, a conditional mean or CATE at a fixed query is
generally not pathwise differentiable in an unrestricted nonparametric model. The usual regular
root-n theory for average treatment effects therefore does not apply directly. Although orthogonality
can reduce sensitivity to nuisance-estimation errors, it does not by itself eliminate the bias introduced
by estimating the conditional effect. For the tabular foundation models considered here, reliable
inference must account for both context variation, arising from the randomness of the finite labeled
sample supplied to the model, and limited-context bias, the systematic difference between the
estimator’s expectation across sampled contexts and the target.
In our experiments, we use a T-learner: the pretrained model estimates the conditional mean
outcome separately under treatment and control, and their difference estimates CATE. Other
learners could also be considered.
For example, the DR-learner combines estimated outcome
regressions and treatment probabilities to construct pseudo-outcomes, which are then regressed on
the covariates to estimate CATE [Kennedy, 2023]. Such an approach could reduce the contribution
of nuisance-estimation errors, although the final regression step may still introduce approximation or
regularization bias. Here, we focus on uncertainty quantification for the T-learner, using TabCUQ
to estimate context variation and provide an empirical bias cushion. Extending this construction
to other learners would require accounting for their additional estimation steps and assessing the
resulting interval coverage.

5.1
Experimental design and benchmarks
We evaluate the coverage and width of nominal 95% confidence intervals for conditional means
µ(x) and conditional average treatment effects τ(x) in simulations where the target values are
known. The primary experiments use TabPFN 2.6 with eight ensemble members. Each Monte
Carlo repetition generates an independent labeled context, including both features and outcomes,
while the queries, pretrained model, and model seed remain fixed. Table 1 summarizes the main
data-generating processes. For conditional means, we consider univariate and multivariate regression
models with different mean functions, outcome noise distributions, and covariate structures. For
CATEs, we consider nine designs that vary the baseline response, treatment-effect function, outcome
noise, and treatment assignment.
We compare TabCUQ with confidence intervals based solely on the wild-bootstrap standard
error (referred to as Wild bootstrap). Both methods use the same point estimate and estimated
standard error, so this comparison isolates the contribution of the empirical bias cushion. For CATE
estimation, we use a T-learner with separate contexts for the two treatment arms. We also consider
an oracle bias-aware (OBA) benchmark that uses the known target value and independent datasets
generated from the true data-generating distribution to estimate the bias and sampling standard
deviation of the original estimator. These quantities are used to construct benchmark intervals
that provide a reference for interval width. The OBA method relies on information available in our
simulations but unavailable in practice.

Increasing the context size reduces interval width but does not necessarily resolve Wild

The perturbation budget moderately affects the empirical bias cushion.
Because the
cushion is defined through the maximum absolute displacement among perturbed estimates, adding
more perturbations cannot decrease this maximum when the draws are nested. In the four original
univariate designs, increasing the number of perturbations from 100 to 500 increases the average
wild-bootstrap displacement cushion by 8.5%–14.4%. Thus, the perturbation budget moderately
affects the interval being constructed rather than merely the numerical precision of its computation.
Additional results are reported in Appendix E.

Real-World Studies
6.1
Data sets and study design
We evaluate TabCUQ on seven real-world regression tasks spanning socioeconomic, environmental,
behavioural, protein, and single-cell data. These tasks represent different prediction settings. Census
income has a weak predictive signal and a heavy-tailed outcome, whereas tree-cover prediction has
a stronger but highly nonlinear relationship between the covariates and outcome. Job Corps and
Jester Jokes provide moderate- and high-dimensional regression settings. The two protein-stability
tasks evaluate generalization to mutations on proteins largely absent from the training pool, with
close sequence homologues removed. CITE-seq examines uncertainty quantification using 2,000
gene-expression features. We consider the task of CATE inference on the Jester Jokes dataset while
implementing conditional-mean inference on all the other datasets. Detailed dataset descriptions
and preprocessing procedures are provided in Appendix I.

Unlike in simulations, the population conditional mean or CATE is unknown in real data. We
therefore evaluate confidence intervals relative to a fixed, data-derived reference function. For each
dataset, we reserve an evaluation set and fit the reference function using the remaining observations.
The reference model is selected by five-fold cross-validation from a collection of regression methods,
including random forests, XGBoost, LightGBM, multilayer perceptrons, ridge regression, and a
constant predictor. A simple Cell-mean predictor is additionally included for the Census dataset with
discrete covariates only. The reference function is fitted using a substantially larger sample than the
contexts supplied to the foundation model and is treated as fixed during evaluation. Consequently,
the reported coverage concerns the estimated reference function rather than the unknown population
conditional-mean or CATE functions.
We repeatedly sample contexts of size nctx ∈{200, 300, 500, 1000} without replacement from the
reference pool. For each sampled context, we obtain a point estimate and confidence interval at
the same fixed evaluation queries. The reference function and evaluation queries remain unchanged
across repetitions. We measure reference coverage by the proportion of intervals containing the
large-sample reference function and summarize the interval widths across the repeated contexts.
The main analyzes use 200 held-out evaluation points for all tasks except protein-stability prediction,
while the two protein tasks use the evaluation sets of 461 and 571 mutations prespecified by the data
providers. For each sampled context, we generate 100 wild-bootstrap perturbations of the observed
outcomes for TabCUQ. Additional details on the setting and evaluation strategy are reported in
Appendix J.

6.2
Results
TabCUQ substantially improves reference coverage with interval widths comparable
in scale to the oracle benchmark.
At nctx = 1000, Wild bootstrap intervals achieve average
reference coverage between 0.614 and 0.842 across the seven tasks, whereas intervals with the
wild-bootstrap displacement cushion achieve coverage between 0.966 and 1.000. The mean pointwise
TabCUQ/OBA width ratios range from 1.62 to 2.27, indicating that the coverage improvements are
achieved with interval widths of the same order as those of the oracle bias-aware benchmark. The
improvement is observed in various inferential tasks. For example, coverage increases from 0.649 to
0.997 for Census income and from 0.682 to 0.973 for tree-cover prediction. Similar improvements
occur in the biological application examples with higher dimensional predictors. In specific, the
coverage increases from 0.779 to 0.997 for protein ∆∆G, from 0.658 to 0.967 for protein ∆Tm, and
from 0.614 to 0.966 for CITE-seq. These results show that the improvement is not confined to
weak-signal problems, as substantial gains are also observed in nonlinear regression and molecular
prediction tasks. Figure 9 presents pointwise coverage and relative-width comparisons for six of the
tasks, with complete results for all seven reported in Appendix J.
Increasing the context size does not consistently resolve Wild bootstrap undercoverage.
In the context-size sensitivity experiments, Wild bootstrap reference coverage decreases from 0.828
to 0.587 for Census and from 0.855 to 0.761 for Jester as nctx increases from 100 to 2000. Tree cover
shows a different pattern, with coverage increasing from 0.503 to 0.689, although it remains below
the nominal level. Undercoverage also persists in the protein-stability tasks, where Wild bootstrap
coverage ranges from 0.779 to 0.812 for ∆∆G and from 0.658 to 0.689 for ∆Tm across context sizes
of 100–1000. In a separate CITE-seq context-size analysis using 500 principal components, Wild
bootstrap coverage increases from 0.703 to 0.760 but remains below nominal. This analysis uses
a different feature representation and reference function from the main CITE-seq experiment. In
contrast, TabCUQ intervals maintain reference coverage close to or above 0.95 across most context
sizes and datasets. The contrasting trends across datasets indicate that increasing the number of
context observations does not necessarily lead to better calibrated intervals. The complete results
are provided in Appendix J.
Favorable average reference coverage can still conceal substantial differences across
individual queries.
This is particularly evident in the main CITE-seq experiment using 1,000
gene-expression features. Although the TabCUQ intervals achieve average reference coverage of
0.966, approximately 10% of evaluation points have pointwise coverage below 0.90. The Job Corps
and Jester analyses also show considerable variation in interval width across queries. These results
suggest that the improvement in reference coverage is not uniform across evaluation points, and
that favorable dataset-level averages should not be interpreted as evidence of adequate coverage at
every query.
