ABSTRACT
Tabular foundation models (TFMs) are being applied to Web data
streams such as network intrusion detection, where they adapt
by updating the labelled rows of their context without retraining.
When a past concept returns after leaving the context, a TFM faces
a recall-or-relearn decision between restoring stored rows of the con-
cept and relearning it from new labels. We propose MICE (Mixture
of In-Context Experts), a training-free learner that cuts the stream
into segments at detected changes. It merges a closed segment into
a stored context when the TFM’s estimated additional error on the
segment is within a tolerance, under a capacity limit on stored con-
texts. A fast level weights the stored contexts and first-in-first-out
(FIFO) windows by the log-loss of their predictions on the last la-
belled batch. We show that, with pure stored contexts and a margin
condition, this fast level gains over relearning, in expected accuracy,
the normalised positive area between the stored context’s accuracy
and the TFM’s learning curve, up to a first-batch term. A slow level
mixes the fast level’s prediction with that of the FIFO window of
the last labelled rows and bounds the discounted half-Brier regret
of MICE against this window at every time. Learning curves mea-
sured beforehand predict the ordering of the observed recall gains.
MICE outperforms reset, which empties the context at detected
drifts, on grid streams with random boundary phases. Merging
keeps fewer experts than pools that store every segment, and with
fixed segmentation a time-matched snapshot pool matches MICE
on the shifted grid. On real streams, MICE has the highest mean
accuracy of all policies we compare.

Existing stream policies either keep rows without grouping them
by concept or reuse trained models. FIFO windows already make
TFMs competitive with adaptive tree ensembles [27], and a prior
trained on drifting causal models improves temporal extrapolation
[18]. Reset, which empties the context when the drift detection
method (DDM) fires [11], discards outdated rows but rebuilds the
context even when a concept has appeared before. The large tabular
model (LTM) baseline of Lourenço et al. [27] pairs a short-term FIFO
buffer with a class-balanced long-term memory, without grouping
rows by concept. Recurring-concept learners instead reuse trained
models [9, 10, 16, 24]. For a frozen TFM, a stored context can serve
as an expert directly. The challenge is to construct useful contexts
from an unsegmented stream and decide when their predictions
deserve more weight than those of recent data.
Consider a fast level that weights stored contexts and recent
windows by their loss on the last labelled batch. Under pure-context
and margin conditions, its expected accuracy gain over a learner
that resets at concept boundaries equals the area between stored-
context accuracy and the relearning curve, up to a first-batch term.
The area is large when relearning is slow and vanishes when one
batch already reaches stored-context accuracy. Learning curves
measured beforehand therefore predict which concepts offer the
most benefit from recall. The online method needs no learning curve
at run time, because it selects contexts from their observed losses.
MICE (Mixture of In-Context Experts) maintains stored con-
texts alongside FIFO windows and combines their predictions using
exponential weights [25]. Memory construction closes segments at
detected changes and merges them when their recall regret, the
extra 0-1 error that the TFM makes on a segment when conditioned
on a stored context instead of on the segment itself, is small. This
preserves reusable concepts while limiting the pool. Fast selection
weights the experts by the log-loss of their predictions on the last
labelled batch. When one expert leads every labelled batch of a
single-concept block by a log-loss margin, the regret of the fast
level over the block is at most one batch of clipped loss plus a term
exponentially small in the margin. Slow aggregation mixes the fast
level’s prediction with the prediction of FIFO, weighted by their
discounted half-Brier losses. It keeps discounted half-Brier regret
against FIFO within 2 ln 2 at every time, for any sequence of la-
bels and expert predictions, which provides a loss guarantee when
following the pool does not yield a clear advantage.
We summarise our contributions as follows.
• We formulate the recall-or-relearn decision for TFMs on streams
with recurring concepts and measure its three aspects with a
context stress test.
• We prove that, under pure-context and margin conditions, the
expected accuracy gain of the fast level over relearning is a
normalised learning-curve area up to a first-batch term, which
lets learning curves measured before deployment predict where
recall pays.
• We propose MICE, a training-free mixture of in-context experts
whose fast level selects stored contexts from recent losses and
whose slow level keeps discounted half-Brier regret against FIFO
at most 2 ln 2 at every time.
• We show that, with detected segmentation, MICE is above reset
on 70 of 72 grid streams with random boundary phases. On 29 real
streams, where concepts are unobserved and experts often have
similar losses, it has the highest mean accuracy of all policies
and is above FIFO on 24.

RELATED WORK
Recurring-concept methods reuse trained models, whose knowl-
edge of past concepts lives in weights that cost training to obtain.
Drift detection and adaptation are surveyed by Gama et al. [12]
and Lu et al. [28], and Cabrera Martin et al. [7] treat drift, forget-
ting and adaptation together. Gonçalves and de Barros [16] select a
stored model by statistical tests. Gama and Kosina [10] learn a meta-
model of each model’s domain. Weighted ensembles reweight their
members as the stream changes [9, 24], and density-harmonized
gradient descent learns imbalanced streams online from actively
queried labels [20]. Dynamic TMoE adds and prunes trained ex-
perts at detected regime shifts [46], and drift-aware collaborative
assistance combines trained experts across heterogeneous streams
[41]. Online learning with experts tracks the best expert through
tracking and adaptive regret [17], whatever the experts are, and
MICE uses this view for contexts.

Prior-data fitted networks (PFNs) approximate posterior predic-
tion from a labelled context [31, 32], and TabPFN [19], TabICL [35]
and TabICLv2 [34] are TFMs of this kind with fixed weights. On
streams, Drift-Resilient TabPFN [18] retrains the prior to extrapo-
late temporal trends. The LTM baseline of Lourenço et al. [27], a
TFM with a short-term FIFO buffer and a class-balanced long-term
memory, outperforms incremental ensembles. LTM does not re-
call a returning concept as a whole, because its two buffers do
not group rows by concept.
Recalling a stored context for a new interval needs a criterion for
whether one context can serve another concept. The H-divergence
[3] and the discrepancy distance [29] measure how well a source
serves a target through the hypotheses it induces. Decision-based
divergences [44] define a distance between distributions by the
decisions that their difference affects. These divergences do not
say when recalling a stored context beats relearning from the rows
of the current concept, because they compare distributions for a
fixed hypothesis class. Our analysis uses learning curves to pre-
dict when recall pays, and MICE estimates the cost of reuse from
the TFM’s prediction errors.

The context stress test runs three tests with frozen TabPFN v2,
which Fig. 3 shows and App. D specifies. The revisit test shows that
FIFO forgets a returning concept. In the five batches after the return,
FIFO reaches 0.17 to 0.51 accuracy on the Sine [11], STAGGER
[36] and Agrawal [1] streams, whereas an oracle context of the
concept’s past rows reaches at least 0.99. The splice test shows that
mixing concepts in a stored context can remove its gain. Moving
the boundaries of eight grid streams 200 rows into fixed 500-row
segments lowers the gain of stored contexts over reset from +3.09 to
−3.67 points, and realigning the segments on the same rows returns
it to +3.01. The curve test shows that relearning speed differs across
concepts. Concepts with 5 centroids are learned from 50 rows,
concepts with 100 centroids need more than 1000, and concepts
of the standard generators about 200. Because 200 rows are two
batches, resetting the context at a detected drift already recovers in
the revisit test. FIFO’s failure on recurrence therefore establishes
forgetting, whereas the learning curve determines whether stored
memory adds value beyond reset.

METHOD: A MIXTURE OF IN-CONTEXT
EXPERTS
MICE uses contexts as experts of one frozen TFM. A fast level
weights stored contexts and FIFO windows by the log-loss of their
predictions on the last labelled batch. A slow level mixes the fast
level’s prediction with that of FIFO and bounds the cost of follow-
ing the fast level. Sec. 4.1 quantifies the gain of a stored context.
Segmentation and merging construct reusable contexts that hold
mostly one concept. Fig. 4 gives the overview, Alg. 1 in App. B the
per-batch procedure, and Tab. 2 the notation. Formal statements
are in App. A and proofs in App. F.

4.5
Timing and Cost
When the labels of the previous batch arrive, every loss scores a
prediction issued before they arrived, and the contexts are updated
only afterwards. No expert is therefore scored on rows it already
holds, and the slow level is scored on the predictions actually is-
sued. At most one segment closes per batch, and App. B gives the
initialisation. The cost is one TFM call per expert and batch, at
most 15. Closing a segment adds two cross-fitted calls and one call
per stored expert, at most 14 calls, which brings a closing batch
to at most 29 calls. The change test reuses the predictions of the
300-row window and makes no call.
In summary, stored contexts provide the gain of recall, and the
slow level bounds what following them can cost.
5
EXPERIMENTS
The evaluation follows the gain predicted by Eq. (1) from ideal
contexts to online use. RQ1: does the area identity describe re-
call gains under its assumptions, and predict their ordering for
the full method? RQ2: can detected segmentation recover gains
when boundaries are unknown? RQ3: how does slow aggrega-
tion affect prediction loss and accuracy on real streams? RQ4:
which gains come from the pool and its organisation, and what
resources do they require?

5.4
RQ3: The Cost of Following Memory on
Real Streams
Real-stream concepts and learning curves are unobserved, and the
experts often have similar prediction losses. On the grid the leading
expert holds weight 1.000 from the second batch of a recurring
block. On the real streams the median margin, the gap in mean
log-loss between the leading and the next-best expert on a labelled
batch, is about 0.01 nats, the regime the slow level is built for. With
detected segmentation, MICE is never more than 0.23 points below
FIFO across the 29 real streams and is above it on 24 (+0.58 on
average). With fixed segmentation, the slow level limits the largest
shortfall of MICE to 0.25 points, against 2.3 for MICE w/o slow
level. In comparison, reset loses up to 9.6 points to FIFO and 1.8 on
average, and the window ensemble up to 1.4, as Tab. 1 reports and
App. H gives per stream. The largest gains occur on Rialto (+4.7,
a daily cycle) and the INSECTS streams (+0.2 to +1.9). In Tab. 1,
the trained baselines have lower mean accuracy on these streams.
Beyond accuracy, MICE has the highest F1 and a lower Brier score
than every baseline on the real streams. LTM has the highest mean
AUC, with its advantage concentrated on the PokerHand segments,
where its class-balanced buffer keeps rare classes.
The confirmation segments of Airlines, CoverType and Poker-
Hand, run with fixed segmentation, reproduce the real-stream result.
MICE is never more than 0.01 points below FIFO on any of the six
segments and is not below reset. It is more accurate than all four
in-context baselines on five and has the highest mean (85.3 against
85.1 for FIFO). The slow level turns the −2.0 and −1.9 points of
MICE w/o slow level on the two PokerHand segments into +0.4 and
+0.5. The one-sided 95% block-bootstrap bound of the difference to
FIFO is above −0.3 points on 28 of the 29 real streams. On Spam,
with 61 batches, non-inferiority is undecided.
On the nine independent confirmation segments of Bitcoin Heist
and CIC-IDS2017, detected segmentation keeps the accuracy of
fixed segmentation, from which the mean of MICE differs by less
than 0.01 points. On every segment the one-sided 95% bootstrap
lower bound on its accuracy difference to FIFO is at least −0.25
points, and its observed accuracy is never more than 0.18 points
below the better of FIFO and reset. It is above its pool-free replay
on eight of nine segments. Mean accuracies over the nine seg-
ments differ by less than 0.4 points across the in-context policies,
at about 98.5%, and MICE is the most accurate of the in-context
policies on four of the nine segments.

5.5
RQ4: Memory Organisation and Resource
Tradeoffs
Removing stored contexts lowers accuracy on all 24 grid streams
(by 5.6 points on average) and on 28 of the 29 real streams (by
0.35) in this fixed-segmentation ablation. Without them, the two
levels mix only the three FIFO windows.
Merging keeps fewer experts than two pools that never merge
and share the windows and two-level rule of MICE with a pool
size of 12. An archive keeps every closed segment as its own ex-
pert, and a snapshot pool stores the latest 1000 rows at every close.
With fixed segments, MICE is above the archive on the aligned
grid by +0.30 points and within 0.1 points of both pools on the
real streams, with fewer experts (8.4 against 14.2 per batch) and
TFM calls (9.7 against 14.0).
Capped to the time per batch of MICE, both fixed-segmentation
pools are less accurate than MICE, by 6.7 and 7.5 points on the
aligned grid and 0.35 and 0.27 on nine real streams. On the shifted
grid, the time-matched snapshot pool is level with MICE, and the
uncapped archive is ahead of it, as Fig. 7a shows.
With detected segments on the fresh grid streams, MICE keeps
the pool at a third of the archive’s experts and is 0.11 points more ac-
curate. On the grid confirmation streams it exceeds a snapshot pool
of equal capacity by 2.14 points and is level with it only on 2 000-row
blocks, as Fig. 7b shows. On 144 pairs of grid segments the recall
regret separates same from different concepts with AUROC 1.00.
Runtime and peak GPU memory grow with the rows that a
policy conditions on, and MICE is compared with FIFO windows
of up to 13 400 rows. MICE with fixed segments takes 1.07 s per
batch on three real prefixes on one GPU, against 0.22 s for FIFO
with 1000 rows, 0.42 s with 3000 rows and 1.91 s with 13 400, as
plotted in App. H. Peak GPU memory is 0.8 to 26.4 GB for FIFO and
1.0 GB for MICE. A 3000-row window, about 2.5 times cheaper, is
more accurate than fixed-segmentation MICE on 21 of the 29 real
streams. MICE is ahead by +0.31 points on average because of the
sharply drifting streams, four INSECTS streams by +3.3 to +5.5 and
Rialto by +6.9, as Fig. 9 details per stream. These comparisons cover
complete policies at different context budgets, whereas Thm. 4.2
compares MICE with FIFO at 𝑀rows.

CONCLUSION
A stored context lets a frozen TFM reuse a past concept without re-
training. Under pure-context and margin conditions, its value is the
relearning it saves, a normalised learning-curve area up to a first-
batch term. MICE builds contexts at detected changes and weights
them by their last-batch log-loss. Its slow level keeps discounted
half-Brier regret against FIFO at most 2 ln 2 at every time. Learning
curves predict the ordering of recall gains on the grid. Detected seg-
mentation recovers these gains under unknown boundary phases.
On 29 real streams, MICE has the highest mean accuracy of all
policies and is above FIFO on 24. Learning curves measured before
deployment can therefore indicate whether stored contexts will pay.

C
REGISTRATION AND DEVELOPMENT
Hypotheses, data and decision rules were written down before each
test, every test stream was run once, and all constants were set on
synthetic development streams. All reported numbers are replays
of cached expert predictions with weights that use earlier labels
only, which makes them equal to those of an online run.
The real streams were opened in five stages, of which the first
three developed the slow level and the fourth was registered for
fixed segmentation. The main text calls the third held-out set the
held-out segments and the fourth held-out set the confirmation
segments. The second held-out set consisted of all streams of the
public collection with at least 5000 rows, at most ten classes and
no earlier use. No method had loaded the nine independent con-
firmation segments of Bitcoin Heist and CIC-IDS2017 before their
test. Detected segmentation was registered and tested separately.
MICE w/o slow level met non-inferiority to reset on the first two
held-out sets but was the most accurate method on only one of six
streams of the second, which motivated the slow level. From then
on these streams count as development data. On the third held-out
set the registered slow step was 10. The step 𝜂2 = 1
2 of Thm. 4.2
was added before any two-level result was computed.
Each registered test below gives its criterion and then its out-
come.
• Grid, seeds 10 and 11 (MICE w/o slow level): gain over reset on
all eight streams with 30 or 100 centroids, Spearman correlation
of predicted and observed gain at least 0.8. Held (0.94).
• Grid, seeds 20 and 21: within 0.5 points of MICE w/o slow level
and above reset on the same eight streams. Held.
• Oracle test of Fig. 5b: measured area within 1.0 point of its pre-
diction on at least five of six cells, Spearman at least 0.8. Held.
• Confirmation segments, the fourth held-out set (six segments,
fixed segmentation): never more than 0.5 points below the better
of FIFO and reset, more accurate than all in-context baselines on
four of six with the highest mean, never more than 0.3 points
below FIFO. Held. A second backbone seed held on these six
segments. On four segments of the third set it gave two of four
where three were registered. With a second backbone seed, MICE
is the most accurate policy on two of four held-out segments.
With TabICL as the backbone and no constant changed, MICE is
the most accurate policy on all six confirmation segments.
• Boundary tests (fixed segmentation): above reset on 10 of 12
misaligned streams and on 7 of 8 shifted streams, each with a
mean gain of one point. Both failed. The realigned test added
afterwards held.
• Detected segmentation (72 test streams, criteria C1–C5): gain over
reset of at least one point in each length regime with a positive
bootstrap bound (C1), recovery on the shifted grid (C2), no loss
on the aligned grid (C3) and on the real streams (C4), and fewer
calls than an archive (C5). All held. Purity: 88% of the closes held
at least 95% rows of one concept, short of the registered 90%.
• Pool controls: MICE above the archive by 0.5 points on the aligned
grid failed (+0.30). The real-stream criterion held.

CURVES
The grid generator fixes the input distribution and lets concepts
differ only in their classes. A stream has 𝑑= 10 features, four
nominal classes and three concepts. For 𝑚∈{5, 30, 100} centroids,
𝐶𝑗∼U([0, 1]10) i.i.d. and mixture weights 𝑤𝑗= 𝑢𝑗/Í
𝑖𝑢𝑖with
𝑢𝑗∼U(0, 1). Each concept 𝑘has its own class vector 𝜆𝑘(𝑗) ∼
U{0, 1, 2, 3}, drawn i.i.d. over centroids and independently across
concepts. A row of concept 𝑘draws 𝑗∼Categorical(𝑤), x =
𝐶𝑗+ N (0, 0.152𝐼10) and 𝑦= 𝜆𝑘(𝑗). Concepts appear in the order
1, 2, 3 in blocks of 𝐿∈{500, 2000} rows for three cycles. The input
distribution 𝑃𝑋is the same for all concepts because all of them
share 𝐶, 𝑤and the noise. One NumPy generator per data seed
draws 𝐶, 𝑤and 𝜆first and then the rows.
Because the class vectors are drawn independently, 𝜆𝑘is not a
permutation and two concepts may agree on a centroid. On our
seeds the weight of such centroids is 18% to 48% averaged over the
three pairs of concepts and 3% to 76% for single pairs. With five
centroids a concept can also lack some classes, with two to four
present. The generator applies no rejection or coverage constraint,
and class priors follow from 𝑤and 𝜆𝑘.
The data seeds are allocated as follows. Seeds 0 and 1 serve
development, including that of the change detector, and seeds 10
and 11 the registered test. Seeds 20 and 21 serve confirmation, 30
and 31 the misaligned schedules, and 40 and 41 the oracle test. Seeds
40 to 43 serve the test of detected segmentation and share seeds 40
and 41 with the oracle test. Seeds 50 to 53 serve its confirmation.
The shifted streams reuse seeds 20 and 21 and draw their prefix
from a separate generator. The streams of Fig. 6 prepend rows of
the first concept from a separate generator and also use blocks of
lengths drawn uniformly from 500 to 2 500 rows.
The learning curves that supply the recall predictions were
measured before the grid test, for data seeds 10 and 11. For
each 𝑚and concept, one block of 4000 rows of that concept
comes from the generator above. Rows 1–3000 form the con-
text pool and rows 3001–4000 the 1000 test rows, disjoint. For
𝑛∈{50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, 2000}, three contexts are drawn without
replacement from the pool (generator seeds 0, 1, 2). Contexts of
different sizes and repetitions therefore overlap, while the same
1000 test rows are used throughout. The frozen TabPFN v2 with
four estimators and random state equal to the data seed predicts the
test rows, whereas stream evaluation uses backbone random state 0.
The curve 𝑎(𝑛) is the mean accuracy over 2 seeds, 3 concepts
and 3 repetitions (18 measurements per 𝑛, not 18 independent
concept draws), interpolated linearly in log𝑛.
Predicted recall gains come from inserting the averaged curve
into the area term of Eq. (1), which measures the accuracy that
a stored expert saves over relearning. Visits 2 and 3 of a concept
recur and enter with weight 1
3 each. The first visit has no stored
expert and contributes zero. The registered predictions used 𝑀
stored rows on every recurrence, and Sec. 5.2 reports the form with
𝑛𝑐= min(𝑀, (𝑐−1)𝐿). The observed gain of a cell is the mean over
its two streams, and the Spearman correlations are over the six cells.
The curves of seeds 10 and 11 share randomness with the evalu-
ated streams of these seeds, because their data were generated by
re-initialising the generator of the same seed with one 4000-row
block per concept. The shared part is the concept definitions of
the evaluated seed-10/11 streams, the centroid draws of their first
block, and some bit-identical feature vectors (0 to 1887 per stream).
These identical rows arise in runs where the two draw sequences of
the same seed realign at a constant offset. The seed-20/21 streams,
by contrast, share nothing with the curves.
To remove the shared rows, a sensitivity analysis registered
after this overlap was found remeasured the curves on rows drawn,
given the same concept definitions, from one independent NumPy
generator per seed, centroid count and concept. Each generator is
seeded with (20 000+seed,𝑚,𝑘), and no redrawn row is in common
with any evaluated stream. These counts and checks are produced
by code/grid_overlap_check.py. The redraw changes the curves
by at most 0.015 at any 𝑛and the predictions by at most 0.6 points.
For 100 centroids and 𝐿= 500, the prediction is 9.9 instead of the
registered 10.4, and 8.9 instead of 9.2 with 𝑛𝑐. The rank correlations
are unchanged at 0.94 on seeds 10 and 11 and 0.83 on seeds 20 and 21.
