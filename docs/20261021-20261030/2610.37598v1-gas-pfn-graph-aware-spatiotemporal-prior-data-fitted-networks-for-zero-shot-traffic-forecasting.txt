GAS-PFN: Graph-Aware Spatiotemporal Prior-Data Fitted Networks for
Zero-Shot Traffic Forecasting
Joseph Natter,1 Long Vu,1 Yifan Zhang,2 Bobin Deng,1 Kun Suo,1 Rui Wu1
1Kennesaw State University
2Missouri State University
Abstract
Traffic forecasting requires modeling both complex tempo-
ral dynamics and the spatial dependencies imposed by road
topology. Supervised graph-based models achieve strong per-
formance, but are typically trained for a particular dataset and
sensor graph, making deployment to new or evolving networks
costly. Prior-Data Fitted Networks (PFNs) offer a zero-shot
alternative by learning in-context inference from synthetic
task priors, which provide an explicit mechanism for encod-
ing domain-specific assumptions. Existing forecasting PFNs,
however, do not explicitly represent the graph-structured de-
pendencies governing traffic dynamics.
To combine graph-aware traffic modeling with zero-shot adap-
tation, we introduce the Graph-Aware Spatiotemporal Prior-
Data Fitted Network (GAS-PFN). GAS-PFN incorporates
sampled road topology into its synthetic task prior through
random-walk diffusion, teaching the model to learn graph-
coupled dynamics during pretraining. Its co-designed infer-
ence architecture uses two mask-conditioned passes through
a shared Transformer backbone to separately model sensor-
specific temporal dynamics and spatiotemporal interactions
among neighboring sensors before integrating their represen-
tations for prediction. This design enables GAS-PFN to use
target-network structure and context without parameter up-
dates.
Across six benchmarks at the 90-minute horizon, GAS-PFN
reduces MSE by 34.0% on average relative to TimePFN, its
closest PFN-based predecessor. It also reduces MSE by 27.7%
on average relative to four recent industry-developed general-
purpose zero-shot forecasters. In this comparison, all models
receive 90 minutes of contiguous recent history, and any ad-
ditional context is drawn from each dataset’s chronological
training split. These results indicate that aligning PFN pri-
ors and inference architectures with graph structure improves
in-context learning for zero-shot traffic forecasting.
Introduction
Traffic conditions evolve over a road network, and congestion
on one road can affectconnected roads.Roadtopologyshapes
these interactions, but the patterns that actually emerge also
depend on changing factors such as travel demand, incidents,
weather, and human behavior, making them difficult to cap-
ture with a fully specified mechanistic model. Deep neural

models provide a flexible means of learning these nonlinear
spatiotemporal relationships directly from data, and graph
neural networks and Transformer-based models have conse-
quently achieved strong forecasting performance (Li et al.
2018; Wu et al. 2020; Jiang et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2023).
Despite their strong performance, these models are typ-
ically trained for a particular dataset and sensor graph, re-
quiring substantial data and computation when deployed to
a new road network or adapted to changes in an existing
one. Prior work reports that training a forecasting model on
approximately one third of California’s highway network re-
quired nearly eight GPU-days (Mallick et al. 2020; Prabowo
et al. 2024). Traffic-sensing deployments are also not static:
sensors may be added, removed, or relocated, while sensor
failures and communication interruptions can break the re-
cent observation history even when older measurements re-
main available (Zuo et al. 2023). These conditions motivate
forecasting methods that can adapt to a target network with-
out repeated parameter optimization or reliance on a long
uninterrupted recent history.
Recent time-series foundation models, including Chronos-
2, TimesFM-2.5, Moirai-2, and Lag-Llama, address the
need for forecasting without task-specific retraining and have
demonstrated strong zero-shot performance across heteroge-
neous datasets (Ansari et al. 2025; Das et al. 2024; Liu et al.
2025; Rasul et al. 2024). However, these models are primar-
ily designed as general-purpose sequence forecasters and do
not explicitly incorporate the relational structure of road net-
works. This leaves a conceptual gap between transferable
zero-shot forecasting and graph-aware traffic modeling.
Prior-Data Fitted Networks (PFNs) offer one emerging
route toward bridging this gap by framing zero-shot predic-
tion as in-context inference (Müller et al. 2022; Hollmann
et al. 2023). During pretraining, PFNs learn from synthetic
tasks sampled from a prior to approximate Bayesian poste-
rior predictive inference from context examples and a query.
Their synthetic task priors provide an explicit mechanism for
representing domain-specific assumptions, while inference-
time conditioning enables adaptation without gradient-based
optimization (i.e., training or fine-tuning). Recent work has
extended this framework to time-series forecasting (Taga,
Ildiz, and Oymak 2025); however, existing time-series PFNs
do not explicitly incorporate known graph topology into ei-
ther their synthetic task priors or inference architectures.

Building on this idea, this work introduces the Graph-
Aware Spatiotemporal Prior-Data Fitted Network (GAS-
PFN), which proposes PFN-style forecasting for graph-
structured traffic signals. GAS-PFN is pretrained on syn-
thetic tasks in which interactions among channels are gener-
ated by diffusion over sampled road-network topologies. This
exposes the model to spatially coupled temporal processes
rather than treating each series as independent or only implic-
itly related. At inference time, GAS-PFN performs two differ-
ently masked passes through a shared Transformer backbone.
A sensor-local pass restricts interactions to observations from
the same sensor, capturing its intrinsic temporal dynamics.
A neighborhood-aware pass allows interactions across both
time and neighboring sensors, capturing graph-conditioned
spatiotemporal dependencies. The resulting representations
are then fused for prediction. By conditioning on both recent
measurements and historical examples from the target net-
work, GAS-PFN adapts through in-context learning rather
than task-specific parameter updates.
Evaluation across six traffic forecasting benchmarks and
three prediction horizons shows that GAS-PFN achieves the
strongest overall zero-shot performance under the limited-
recent-history protocol. At the 90-minute horizon, it ob-
tains the best MAE and MSE on five of six datasets and
achieves lower average MSE than both TimePFN and four
recent general-purpose zero-shot forecasters. GAS-PFN also
outperforms supervised SCPT when target-road training cov-
erage is limited. These results indicate that encoding graph
structure in both the synthetic prior and inference architecture
improves zero-shot forecasting under limited recent context.
Contributions. The main contributions are as follows:
• A graph-aware synthetic task prior that uses road-network
topology and random-walk diffusion to expose a PFN to
graph-coupled spatiotemporal dynamics during pretrain-
ing.
• A co-designed inference architecture that separately mod-
els sensor-specific temporal dynamics and spatiotempo-
ral interactions among neighboring sensors, enabling the
model to exploit its graph-aware prior during zero-shot
prediction.
• A comprehensive evaluation across six traffic forecasting
benchmarks and three prediction horizons, in which GAS-
PFN achieves the best result in 27 of 36 MAE/MSE com-
parisons and outperforms supervised SCPT when target-
road training coverage is limited.
Related Work
Zero-Shot Time-Series Forecasting
Recent time-series foundation models have demonstrated
strong zero-shot forecasting performance across heteroge-
neous datasets. Chronos-2 uses group attention to support
univariate, multivariate, and covariate-informed forecast-
ing within a single pretrained model (Ansari et al. 2025).
TimesFM 2.5 builds on a patch-based decoder-only Trans-
former trained for zero-shot forecasting across domains, hori-
zons, and temporal resolutions (Das et al. 2024). Moirai 2.0
adopts a decoder-only architecture with quantile forecast-
ing and multi-token prediction to improve both forecasting
accuracy and inference efficiency (Liu et al. 2025). These
models support general-purpose zero-shot forecasting but do
not explicitly incorporate known relational structure between
channels, such as road-network topology. GAS-PFN instead
investigates how such domain structure can be encoded di-
rectly into zero-shot pretraining and inference.
Traffic Forecasting and Cross-Network
Generalization
Traffic forecasting methods commonly exploit spatial depen-
dencies among sensors through graph-based architectures.
DCRNN models traffic propagation as diffusion over a di-
rected graph and combines graph convolution with recur-
rent temporal modeling (Li et al. 2018). Graph WaveNet
introduces adaptive adjacency learning and dilated tempo-
ral convolutions to capture spatial relationships and long-
range temporal dependencies (Wu et al. 2019). More recent
Transformer-based methods include PDFormer, which in-
corporates propagation-delay-aware spatial attention (Jiang

et al. 2023), and STAEformer, which obtains strong per-
formance through learned spatiotemporal embeddings (Liu

These models achieve strong supervised performance but
are generally optimized for a particular dataset and sensor
graph. Graph-dependent components such as node embed-
dings, adjacency representations, and attention masks may
therefore be tied to the network observed during training, lim-
iting direct application to altered node sets or unseen road
networks. Transfer-oriented methods address this limitation
by learning representations intended to generalize beyond the
roads observed during training. Mallick et al. transfer a graph
neural traffic forecaster between highway networks (Mallick
et al. 2020), while SCPT contrastively pretrains a spatial
encoder to infer representations for roads excluded from su-
pervised training (Prabowo et al. 2024). These methods learn
representations that generalize to new roads, whereas GAS-
PFN adapts at inference time through context examples from
the target graph.
Prior-Data Fitted Networks
Prior-Data Fitted Networks (PFNs) learn to approximate
Bayesian posterior predictive inference by training on syn-
thetic tasks sampled from a predefined prior (Müller et al.
2022; Hollmann et al. 2023). At inference time, predictions
are produced by conditioning on observed context examples
rather than updating model parameters. ForecastPFN applied
this approach to zero-shot forecasting using a fully syn-
thetic time-series distribution (Dooley et al. 2023). LaT-PFN
combines PFN-based inference with a joint-embedding pre-
dictive architecture, performing in-context learning within a
prediction-oriented latent space (Verdenius, Zerio, and Wang
2024). TimePFN extends PFN forecasting to multivariate se-
ries through synthetic Gaussian-process tasks and an archi-
tecture that models temporal and cross-channel dependen-
cies (Taga, Ildiz, and Oymak 2025), making it the closest
PFN-based predecessor to GAS-PFN.
A distinguishing feature of PFNs is that the synthetic
task prior constitutes an explicit modeling choice through
which domain-specific assumptions can be introduced. Ex-
isting forecasting PFNs, however, do not encode graph topol-
ogy within their synthetic task priors and inference architec-
tures. GAS-PFN addresses this gap by co-designing graph-
structured synthetic tasks and graph-aware in-context infer-
ence for traffic forecasting.


Method
GAS-PFN combines a graph-aware synthetic task prior with
an inference architecture designed to exploitgraph-structured
context. Figure 1 summarizes the full pipeline. We first for-
malize the zero-shot PFN setting, then introduce a graph-
aware spatiotemporal prior that exposes the model to traffic-
like temporal dynamics and graph-coupled interactions, fol-
lowed by an architecture designed to learn these patterns
during pretraining and infer them from context at test time.

Spatiotemporal Prior
The central prior-design principle is to expose the PFN
to temporal processes whose channels interact over realis-
tic road-network structures. Each synthetic task combines
a compositional temporal process with a connected graph
sampled from a bank of real road networks, as illustrated in
figure 2.
The univariate temporal generator adapts the composi-
tional prior used by LaT-PFN, itself derived from Fore-
castPFN (Verdenius, Zerio, and Wang 2024; Dooley et al.
2023). We modify its composition rule, parameter distribu-
tions, and exponential-trend construction, and extend it with
road-network sampling and graph-structured spatial pertur-
bations to produce multivariate traffic processes.

Results
Datasets and metrics.
We evaluate GAS-PFN on six
widely used traffic forecasting benchmarks, all sampled at
five-minute intervals. METR-LA contains traffic-speed mea-
surements from 207 highway sensors in Los Angeles County
collected from March through June 2012, while PEMS-BAY
contains speed measurements from 325 sensors in the San
Francisco Bay Area collected from January through May
2017 (Li et al. 2018). PEMS03, PEMS04, PEMS07, and
PEMS08 contain traffic-flow measurements from 358, 307,
883, and 170 sensors, respectively, drawn from the cor-
responding California Performance Measurement System
districts (Chen et al. 2001). Their collection periods span
September–November 2018, January–February 2018, May–
August 2017, and July–August 2016, respectively (Guo et al.
2019). These benchmarks provide a standard basis for com-
parison while covering both speed and flow prediction across
road networks of different sizes and geographic settings. We
use chronological 70%/10%/20% train, validation, and test
splits and report MAE and MSE cumulatively at horizons of
6, 12, and 18 steps, corresponding to 30, 60, and 90 min-
utes; MAE summarizes typical absolute error, whereas MSE
places greater weight on large deviations. Following standard
traffic-forecasting practice, zero-valued targets are masked
when computing the training loss and evaluation metrics, as
zero commonly serves as the missing-value marker in these
benchmarks (Zuo et al. 2023; Li et al. 2018). More infor-
mation on datasets and experimental setups is provided in
Appendix C.

Conclusion
We introduced GAS-PFN, a graph-aware Prior-Data Fit-
ted Network for zero-shot traffic forecasting. GAS-PFN co-
designs graph-structured synthetic pretraining tasks with
mixed-context memory and dual mask-conditioned infer-
ence, enabling it to adapt to a target road network without
parameter updates and with only 90 minutes of contigu-
ous recent history. Across six traffic benchmarks, GAS-PFN
achieves the strongest overall zero-shot performance. It also
remains competitive with supervised cross-road forecasting
under limited target-road training. These results suggest util-
ity in two deployment settings: forecasting on newly added
roads when supervised coverage is limited, and forecasting
on existing networks when recent observations are inter-
rupted but older measurements remain available. Concep-
tually, these results support synthetic task priors as a practi-
cal mechanism for introducing domain-specific structure into
PFNs, suggesting applications in other structured prediction
domains. Disclosure: Generative AI tools assisted with lan-
guage editing, LATEX formatting, and manuscript review.

Additional Synthetic Prior Details
The synthetic prior used for pretraining was generated of-
fline and stored as a frozen bank. Construction proceeded
in two stages. First, a bank of univariate temporal base se-
ries was generated from compositional trend, seasonal, and
stochastic components. Each base series was then lifted to
a sampled road subgraph and modified using node-specific
gains, random-walk diffusion, and graph-correlated process
noise. The resulting bank contains one million multivariate
series of length T = 12,000.

Context Construction
The reported checkpoints and target-dataset evaluations use

larger randomly sampled context is reduced using recent raw
tokens, historical raw tokens, and attention-pooled memory

module is bypassed and each node receives K = 16 recent
and K = 16 historical raw support patches.

Additional Comparison Protocols
TimePFN fine-tuning.
The TimePFN fine-tuning com-
parison uses labeled-window budgets of 32 and 64 on
METR-LA, PEMS-BAY, PEMS03, PEMS04, and PEMS08;
PEMS07 is excluded because the registered fine-tuning con-
figuration exceeded available GPU memory. Budgets are
nested, and the same sampled window indices are shared
across the two TimePFN context variants for each seed.
All TimePFN parameters are updated using AdamW with
learning rate 2 × 10−4, weight decay 0.01, batch size 16,
and at most eight epochs. The learning rate is halved af-
ter each epoch, early-stopping patience is three epochs, and
checkpoint selection uses validation performance. Training
and validation minimize original-scale, exact-zero-masked
MAE. Results are aggregated over seeds 42, 43, and 44.
SCPT selected-road evaluation.
The cross-road experi-
ment uses SCPT (Prabowo et al. 2024) with a 12-step look-
back and an 18-step output. For each dataset, the fraction of
roads available for SCPT training ranges from 10% to 80%;
10% of roads are reserved for validation, and the remaining
roads form the unseen test set. Seeds 42, 43, and 44 de-
fine independent road permutations. Each GAS-PFN result
is paired with the checkpoint having the same seed, while
SCPT is trained separately for that road partition.
SCPT retains its native preprocessing, which computes
one scalar mean and standard deviation over the complete
time-by-road matrix before the temporal and road splits.
GAS-PFN instead uses the train-only sensor-specific scaling
in Eqs. (95)– (97). For the final comparison, both methods are
inverse-transformed, aligned to the same raw target tensor,
and rescored using the identical exact-zero mask.
The selected-road results report the arithmetic mean and
sample standard deviation over the three paired model and
road-partition seeds. This variation therefore includes both
independently pretrained GAS-PFN checkpoints and differ-
ent supervised-road partitions.

TimePFN Fine-Tuning Comparison
Table 5 compares zero-shot GAS-PFN with TimePFN after
target-specific fine-tuning using 32 or 64 labeled patches.
For TimePFN, “Recent 18” uses only the 18 observations
immediately preceding each forecast origin, while “Recent +
prefix” also supplies a contiguous tail from the training split.
GAS-PFN instead uses its fixed mixed-context configuration
and performs no target-specific parameter updates. Across
the five evaluated datasets, GAS-PFN achieves the best mean
result in 27 of the 30 dataset–horizon–metric comparisons.
Fine-tuning TimePFN with 64 patches narrows the gap and
yields the best 90-minute MSE on PEMS03, PEMS04, and
PEMS08, but does not otherwise surpass GAS-PFN. These
results reinforce that the graph-aware prior and in-context
architecture remain competitive even against limited target-
domain parameter adaptation.
Full SCPT Comparison
Table 6 expands the main-paper cross-road comparison by
reporting complete MAE and MSE results at the cumulative
30-, 60-, and 90-minute horizons. SCPT12 is trained sepa-
rately for each road split using the full historical record from
the indicated fraction of roads, with 10% reserved for val-
idation and the remaining roads held out for testing. GAS-
PFN instead performs zero-shot inference without target-
specific parameter updates. The 60-minute results reproduce
the main-paper finding that GAS-PFN is strongest when su-
pervised road coverage is limited: it outperforms SCPT12 on
all six datasets at the 10% budget and on five of six datasets
at both 20% and 30%. The additional horizons show the
same broad pattern, while SCPT12 generally becomes more
competitive as its access to supervised roads increases. Full
preprocessing and split details are provided in Appendix C.

Ablation Tests
Table 7 presents targeted frozen-checkpoint ablations of
three components: dual-branch fusion, mixed-context sup-
port, and hybrid neighborhood routing. Across the resulting
36 dataset–horizon–metric settings, the full learned mixture
achieves the best mean in 23 cases and ranks among the top
two in 35. It outperforms both individual experts in 34 set-
tings, while either learned or fixed equal fusion is best in 32,
supporting the use of complementary self-channel and multi-
channel representations. Removing the historical supports
degrades performance in all 36 settings, indicating that the
trained model makes effective use of its mixed-context mem-
ory. The hybrid router also outperforms graph-only routing
in 33 settings, although these gains are generally smaller and
remain dataset-dependent. Because these variants use frozen
checkpoints, they isolate the inference-time contribution of
each component.
