# Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) generally lack deep causal reasoning capabilities in temporal reasoning. Inspired by document layout reordering, this paper pro-poses a paradigm shift from discrete narratives to causal topologies. It constructs two architectures: a training-free neuro-symbolic framework, temporal causal reasoning agent (TCR-Agent), which achieves interpretable causal deduction and counterfactual truncation through external causal graph instantiation and a sym-bolic intervention engine; and an end-to-end model, temporal causal reasoning former (TCR-Former), which internalizes these mechanisms into the Transformer attention space via a causal-topological attention mask and temporal span biases. Experiments on the TempoCausal benchmark demonstrate that TCR-Agent compre-hensively surpasses existing training-free baselines. At the same time, TCR-Former achieves an accuracy 16.5% higher than that of state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods, outperforming large-scale models such as GPT-5.4 and DeepSeek-V3.2 with only approximately 8.6B parameters. Further analysis reveals a structure-reasoning disconnection phenomenon in existing methods: external symbolic constraints and chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning can improve reasoning steps, yet fail to enhance deep causal deductive capability. By internalizing temporal causal reasoning ca-pabilities into the model, TCR-Former effectively bridges this gap, providing an efficient and reliable end-to-end foundation for complex reasoning scenarios. The code and data can be obtained through https://github.com/Translab-SCUT/TCR-Former. 

# 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong generalization capabilities in tasks such as natural language understanding and instruction following [1, 2]. However, when tasks shift from time-independent semantic understanding to complex reasoning with temporal dependencies, existing models generally exhibit pronounced temporal blind spots [3, 4]. Studies indicate that LLMs perform poorly in temporal semantic parsing, long-context utilization, and complex event ordering, tending to rely on superficial textual order rather than genuine chronological order [5, 6]. To address this challenge, researchers have advanced the field along two dimensions: evaluation benchmarks and rea-soning methods. On the evaluation side, early temporal question-answering benchmarks established foundational frameworks for task definition [7–9]. In contrast, subsequent evaluations have further revealed systematic deficiencies of LLMs in real-world temporality, multi-level dependencies, and

cross-calendar reasoning [3, 5, 6, 10]. On the methodological side, recent work has partially improved performance in timeline ordering and explicit temporal question answering through techniques such as multi-hop temporal chain-of-thought (CoT) [11], temporal graph intermediate representations [4], and temporally-aware retrieval augmentation [12, 13]. Nevertheless, the evolution of the aforementioned benchmarks and methods has primarily centered on the explicit modeling of event temporal relations, essentially remaining at the level of superficial timeline ordering [4, 14, 15] and generally lacking attention to the deep causal relationships underlying temporality. In real-world decision-making scenarios involving implicit temporal windows, multi-entity narratives, and counterfactual reasoning, models must not only sequence events chronologically but also capture the causal topology among events to support abductive and hypothetical inference. However, reasoning within structural causal models involves complex operations such as abduction, intervention, and counterfactuals, which cannot be readily achieved through simple event ordering [16] or flat retrieval [17]. Some efforts have attempted to enhance reasoning reliability through causalized CoT [18, 19]. Still, these methods generally impose causal structure by externally controlling the reasoning process and do not genuinely internalize causal topology awareness and intervention mechanisms into the model’s forward computation, thus remaining constrained in cross-scenario generalization and reasoning efficiency [15]. This predicament reveals that merely superimposing causal structure through external symbolic constraints does not address the essence of the problem. The crux of temporal causal reasoning lies in internalizing the topology-aware capacity for temporal causation into the model’s reasoning process. Inspired by visual document understanding, we observe a profound computational isomorphism between complex temporal causal reconstruction and layout structuring tasks. ROAP [20] and DeepSeek-OCR 2 [21] reorganize discrete, disordered visual blocks in two-dimensional space into logically ordered one-dimensional sequences through reading-order-aware biases and visual causal flow mechanisms. The core challenge faced by temporal reasoning is highly analogous; both essentially require reconstructing discrete, disordered perceptual fragments into a structured logical topology. Based on this insight, this paper aims to transfer the logic of layout reordering to temporal causal reconstruction by modeling the causal topology among events and internalizing causal topology awareness and temporal priors into the model’s reasoning process and attention mechanisms. Motivated by the above, we propose the temporal causal reasoning agent (TCR-Agent) framework, which explicitly reconstructs the causal topology in complex narratives through an external causal graph and a symbolic intervention engine. To further enhance end-to-end reasoning efficiency and integrate temporal causal priors into the underlying model representations, we extend this framework by proposing the temporal causal reasoning former (TCR-Former), which employs a causal-topological attention mask, explicit temporal span biases, and a native counterfactual intervention mechanism to achieve end-to-end temporal causal topology awareness. The main contributions of this paper are as follows. • We construct the TempoCausal-Bench to address the lack of deep causal reasoning evaluation in existing benchmarks. • We propose the TCR-Agent framework, a training-free, model-agnostic external causal graph and symbolic intervention scheme compatible with various open-source and proprietary LLMs, providing interpretable and intervenable system-level support for complex temporal causal reasoning. • We propose the TCR-Former model, an end-to-end architecture that internalizes causal topology awareness and intervention mechanisms into Transformer forward computation, providing an efficient and reliable temporal causal reasoning backbone for complex reason-ing scenarios. 

# 2 TCR-Agent Algorithm 

TCR-Agent adopts a neuro-symbolic reasoning architecture and the core of TCR-Agent lies in leveraging an external graph memory mechanism to reconstruct the latent causal structure, enabling a pre-trained LLM to perform verifiable counterfactual reasoning and multi-hop temporal deduction without modifying its internal parameters. The framework formally integrates the structural causal model (SCM) and the potential outcome framework (POF). SCM provides a formal representation 2and operations for the causal topology, while POF is utilized to evaluate evolutionary outcomes under counterfactual settings. The core algorithmic flow is presented in Algorithm 1 (Appendix D). 

2.1 Causal Graph Extraction 

This module aims to reconstruct the causal topology among events from unstructured text explicitly. We design a query-aware spatiotemporal extractor Eθ and a symbolic graph constructor B to work in concert, achieving end-to-end construction of a structured causal directed acyclic graph (DAG) from raw text. Temporal question answering typically involves one or more text segments describing the background of a question. Assume it is a sequence of Nc text chunks, C = {c1, · · · , c Nc }. Let P

denote the user query prompt, composed of a temporal question and other relevant hints, such as a CoT for solving the question. The spatiotemporal extractor Eθ takes C and P as input and extracts a causal graph by invoking the LLM. The extraction process is shown in Equations (1) and (2).

Graw denotes the raw graph data, with Vraw being the set of extracted event nodes and Eraw the set of causal edges among these nodes. Subsequently, the symbolic graph constructor B instantiates Graw into a directed acyclic graph 

G = ( V, E). For each node vi ∈ V , its original temporal anchor ti is preserved, and its textual description and quantitative data are fused into a unified semantic representation, as formulated in Equation (3).

Consequently, each node simultaneously carries the temporal anchor ti and the enriched semantic representation ˜vi, forming a three-dimensional representation space that fuses temporal, semantic, and numerical information. For each directed edge eij ∈ E , the associated attributes include the source node src i, the target node tgt j , the relation type type ij , and the reason reason ij , where reason ij records the causal derivation basis from the source event to the target event. During instantiation, the graph constructor 

B resolves potential contradictions among node timestamps via topological sorting, enforces the acyclicity constraint, and ultimately outputs a strictly structured logical trajectory Glogic .To distinguish between causal and non-causal relationships among events, we categorize events in complex temporal question answering scenarios into three types and apply differentiated processing strategies: 

(1) Irrelevant Events: Events with no logical intersection in semantics or time. The system directly filters these out during the causal graph extraction phase. 

(2) Co-occurring Events: Events that merely overlap or follow a sequential order in time, but lack substantial causal drivers. The system retains only the independent states and temporal anchors of these nodes in the causal graph, without establishing any causal directed edge connections. 

(3) Causal Events: Events with explicit causal dependencies. The system not only establishes directed edge connections but also generates attribute fields such as reason to accurately characterize and record the specific causal derivation basis from the source event to the target event. 

2.2 Counterfactual Intervention 

This module aims to support what-if reasoning by performing symbolic-level structural perturbations to truncate and redirect causal chains. To this end, we design a graph intervention engine I. Semanti-cally, this intervention operation is equivalent to the do-operator in SCM, cutting off incoming edges 3to specific variables via counterfactual truncation and observing system changes. The intervention op-eration is triggered by counterfactual assumptions in P . When P contains counterfactual statements such as "if an event had not occurred," the LLM parses the intervention parameters ψ = ( vk, action ),where vk is the target node and action ∈ { remove , cut _edges } specifies the type of intervention. Given the intervention parameters ψ, I applies structural perturbations to the current causal graph 

G = ( V, E) to produce a modified causal graph G′ = ( V′, E′), as computed in Equation (4).

The perturbed causal graph G′ is linearized via a depth-first traversal into a topological description string D(G′), which explicitly encodes the temporal gaps, semantic descriptions, and causal justifications between event nodes. 

2.3 Dual-Track Reasoning 

This module aims to constrain autoregressive generation into a verifiable symbolic execution process through serialized cognitive procedures and explicit invariant checking. The core reasoning is executed by a dual-track reasoner R, which integrates the unstructured raw text C and the structured logical trajectory D(G′). To regulate the LLM’s reasoning process, we constrain the generation trajectory using serialized tags organized into three parts: checking, reasoning, and reflection. Their specific contents are as follows. 

(1) Checking: Describes D(G′) in natural language, and based on C checks the temporal computation correctness and causal derivation consistency of D(G′), and if incorrect, corrects D(G′).

(2) Reasoning: Performs reasoning according to C, P , and D(G′), and returns Unknown if the information is insufficient. 

(3) Reflection: Reflects on whether the answer is correct, and if the answer is inconsistent with C

and D(G′), corrects it and returns the correct answer. 

# 3 TCR-Former Architecture 

TCR-Former embeds the temporal causal modeling logic of the TCR-Agent paradigm into the Transformer’s forward computation pipeline. TCR-Former consists of a temporal causal encoder and a reasoning decoder. The encoder extracts high-dimensional causal latent variables from fragmented text, and the decoder feeds these latent variables, along with the original factual information and user query instructions, into the autoregressive generation process. The architecture of the TCR-Former model is illustrated in Fig. 1. The training strategy of TCR-Former is detailed in Appendix F. 

3.1 Temporal Causal Encoder 

The temporal causal encoder uses Qwen-3-0.6B as its backbone, aiming to extract structured temporal causal latent variables from unstructured text fragments. 

3.1.1 Input Sequence Processing 

For a sequence of Nc text chunks C = {c1, · · · , c Nc }, the model first converts them into continuous text token representations V ∈ RB×Lc×denc through a word embedding layer, where B is the batch size, Lc is the total length of the concatenated text tokens, and denc is the encoder hidden dimension. To discover the implicit causal structure within the text, we introduce a set of N learnable event anchors as a causal query token embedding matrix Q = {q1, · · · , qN }. Each event anchor qk is used to query and aggregate a potential causal event from the text. In experiments, we set N = 128 ,which exceeds the maximum possible number of events in any sample, and the surplus slots are set to an empty event state during training. qk consists of two components, as shown in Equation (5). 4Figure 1: TCR-Former architecture. 

qk = Eanchor (k) + Espan (k), k ∈ [1 , N ] (5) where, Eanchor ∈ RN ×denc is a position-independent base anchor embedding that provides an independent basis vector for each potential event slot. Espan ∈ RN ×denc is a learnable time span bias embedding, which endows each event slot with a different temporal domain prior, for example, making certain anchors more inclined to attend to short-term events while others to long-range events. Both Eanchor and Espan are trainable parameters. The text tokens and query tokens are finally concatenated into a one-dimensional input sequence 

X = [ V; Q] ∈ RB×(Lc+N )×denc . For differentiated treatment in subsequent attention computations, we label text tokens as type 0 and query tokens as type 1. 

3.1.2 Causal-Topological Attention Mask 

Temporal causal reasoning requires simultaneously handling two types of interactions with distinct natures: bidirectional semantic understanding among text tokens and strict causal ordering among event query tokens. To this end, we design a causal-topological attention mask M.Let the total sequence length be L = Lc + N , where positions 1 to Lc correspond to text tokens and positions Lc + 1 correspond to query tokens. The mask matrix M ∈ RL×L is dynamically constructed before the attention computation of each layer. Its element assignment rule is given in Equation (6).

Among query tokens, where i, j > L c ∧ j ≤ i, the condition j ≤ i ensures that the overall direction of event queries follows a causal order, meaning future queries cannot inform past ones. 53.1.3 Time-Aware Positional Bias 

To further strengthen the encoder’s perception of the physical time dimension, we introduce an explicit time bias matrix Btime . For the query token embeddings Q and text token embeddings V,we apply learnable linear projections Proj Q and Proj V, respectively, and then compute their inner product to obtain the bias matrix Btime , as shown in Equation (7). 

Btime = Proj Q(Q) · Proj V(V)⊤ (7) 

Btime is added to the region where i > L c, j ≤ Lc in Equation (6), enabling query tokens to adjust their attention weights based on temporal logic when aggregating textual information. 

3.1.4 Auxiliary Timestamp Prediction 

To enable the latent space representation to encode physical temporal positions explicitly, we attach a lightweight timestamp regression head to the encoder. This head consists of two fully connected layers with GELU activation and maps the final hidden state of each query token to a one-dimensional normalized timestamp prediction. The regression head is supervised only during training via mean squared error (MSE) loss, injecting temporal awareness into the latent variables. 

3.1.5 Computation Flow 

The overall computation flow of the encoder can be summarized as follows. The text embeddings V

and event anchor embeddings Q are concatenated and fed into the backbone network. Before each attention layer computation, the model dynamically constructs the causal topological attention mask 

M based on the token type identifiers and adds the time bias matrix Btime . After the network output, the hidden states corresponding to all query tokens are extracted to obtain the causal latent variable sequence Zcausal ∈ RB×N ×denc .

3.2 Reasoning Decoder 

The reasoning decoder is built on the Qwen-3-8B model and aligns the encoder’s causal latent variables with user query instructions while performing autoregressive text generation. Since the latent space dimensions and semantic distributions differ between the encoder and decoder, a nonlinear projector P is placed between them for dimension alignment and representation adaptation. The projector consists of two fully connected layers with GELU activation. To simultaneously leverage the fine-grained semantics of the original factual information and the topological priors in the causal graph structure during reasoning, the decoder adopts a fact-logic bypass fusion strategy. The input embedding sequence of the decoder is formed by concatenating three parts, as shown in Equation (8). 

Einput = [ Vtext ; Zaligned ; Eprompt ] (8) where, Vtext is the token embeddings of the original text chunks obtained through the decoder’s word embedding layer, which constitutes the factual track. Zaligned is the aligned causal latent variables from the encoder output via the projector, which constitutes the logical track. Eprompt is the embedding representation of the user query instruction. The corresponding attention mask Afull is constructed in a block-diagonal form. Within Vtext , a standard causal mask is maintained. The Zaligned part is globally visible to all positions. The Eprompt 

part follows the standard autoregressive mask. 

3.3 Native Counterfactual Intervention 

TCR-Former supports executing counterfactual interventions at the attention mechanism level, without re-encoding the input text or invoking an external graph manipulation engine. The intervention operation is implicitly triggered by counterfactual assumptions in the user query prompt P . When the user prompt contains counterfactual statements such as "if an event had not occurred," the system matches them with event anchors in the encoder input. It resolves the query token index kψ

corresponding to the target event. 6After obtaining the index kψ , the encoder performs topological pruning on the mask matrix M

when constructing the causal-topological attention mask. The element-wise modification rule for the intervention mask Mψ is given in Equation (9). 

Mψi,j = Mi,j · I(i̸ = kψ ) · I(j̸ = kψ ) (9) That is, all incoming and outgoing attention connections of the target query token are cut off by setting the mask values at its corresponding positions to −∞ . Through this operation, the causal latent variables Zcausal output by the encoder implicitly reflect the topological structural changes after the causal chain is broken. In the decoding stage, Zaligned carries this intervention information into the autoregressive generation process, and the model reasons about the outcome under the counterfactual scenario based on the latent representations of the remaining causal paths. 

3.4 Forward Computation Flow 

The overall forward computation flow of TCR-Former is presented in Algorithm 2 (Appendix D). The input consists of the text chunk sequence C and the user query prompt P . First, the encoder generates text token embeddings V and event anchor embeddings Q with time span biases, concatenates them, computes the time bias matrix, and constructs the causal-topological attention mask. If P contains a counterfactual assumption, the intervention index is resolved, and topological pruning is applied to the mask. The encoder outputs hidden state sequences, from which the causal latent variables Zcausal 

corresponding to the query tokens are extracted, and timestamps ˆT are predicted via the regression head. Subsequently, Zcausal is aligned to the decoder space by the projector, yielding Zaligned . The decoder concatenates the original text embeddings Vtext , the aligned latent variables Zaligned , and the prompt embedding Eprompt , constructs the full attention mask, performs autoregressive decoding, and finally outputs the generated response Y .

# 4 TempoCausal-Bench 

Existing mainstream temporal benchmarks mostly rely on explicit timestamps to anchor questions, allowing models to answer easily through shallow matching and making it difficult to reach deep temporal causal reasoning. However, complex decision-making and long-range narratives in real-world scenarios precisely involve characteristics such as implicit temporal boundary delineation, concurrent state evolution of multiple entities, and long-range causal lags. To systematically evaluate the topological causal reasoning capabilities of large language models in these scenarios, we build the TempoCausal-Bench by upgrading linear temporal question answering into a structured temporal causal reasoning task through a five-dimensional reconstruction paradigm, based on the difficult samples from TimeQA-Hard [8]. The detailed construction process of the TempoCausal-Bench is presented in Appendix E.

6.3 Error Attribution Analysis 

To systematically analyze the failure mechanisms of models in complex temporal tasks, we categorize error attributions into four primary error patterns: Temporal Boundary Misjudgment (Error 1), Causal Chain Breakage (Error 2), Rule Inference Mismatch (Error 3), and Other Errors (Error 4). Detailed explanations of the various errors and a detailed analysis of this section are provided in Appendix C.4. Based on Qwen-3-8B, we randomly sample 200 instances per method and perform error attribution. Fig. 3(c) presents the error distribution for each method. Upon deeper analysis of the experimental results, we observe a noteworthy structure-reasoning disconnection phenomenon : external graph structures and CoT reasoning can make the model’s reasoning structure more precise, but they fail to fundamentally address the model’s deficiency in deductive capability. As shown in Fig. 3(c), compared with existing methods, TCR-Agent drastically reduces the error proportions of temporal boundary misjudgment (8.4%) and causal chain breakage (9.9%). However, rule inference mismatch remains as high as 13.0%, nearly on par with TISER (13.8%). This indicates that combining external graph structures with CoT for reasoning still struggles to handle error types such as Rule Inference Mismatch, which demand deeper deductive capabilities. In contrast, TCR-Former keeps the proportions of all three error types below 3.4%, with rule inference mismatch at only 2.8%, indicating that internalizing temporal causal priors into the Transformer attention space can effectively alleviate the structure-reasoning disconnection problem. 

# 7 Related Work 

Existing research on temporal causal reasoning primarily centers on two main paradigms. In neuro-symbolic reasoning, approaches leveraging graph structures and symbolic working memory [28–30] effectively enhance multi-hop compositional stability, yet they predominantly target static common-sense domains, lacking dedicated mechanisms for dynamic temporal evolution and counterfactual deduction. In temporal reasoning for large language models, existing approaches, including times-tamp injection, temporal graphs, and chain-of-thought prompting [5, 8, 11, 31, 32], predominantly remain confined to superficial chronological ordering, failing to internalize temporal span biases and causal topological constraints into the foundational self-attention computation of the model. Furthermore, at the underlying computational architecture level, this work draws critical inspiration from layout rearrangement studies in visual document understanding. This field has progressed from early spatial coordinate modeling [33] to recent paradigms that directly encode reading order topology into forward computation via attention biases, such as ROAP [20] and DeepSeek-OCR 2 [21]. This motivates us to design the Causal-Topological Attention Mask and temporal span biases, transferring topology awareness into the self-attention layer to achieve end-to-end temporal causal modeling. The complete related work is detailed in Appendix A.

We propose TCR-Agent and TCR-Former, two systematic approaches that, respectively, follow the training-free neuro-symbolic framework and the natively internalized causal architecture to tackle temporal causal reasoning in complex narratives. On the TempoCausal-Bench, TCR-Agent consistently surpasses existing training-free baselines across multiple models. Moreover, TCR-Former achieves 75.7% accuracy with approximately 8.6B parameters, outperforming the previous best method by 16.5%, and breaking through the structure-reasoning disconnection bottleneck under external symbolic constraints. Structural Rearrangement and Topology Modeling in Document Visual Understanding. In document visual understanding, the core challenge lies in reconstructing discrete and disordered visual elements on a two-dimensional layout into a one-dimensional sequence conforming to a logical reading order [34–36]. LayoutLM [33] introduces spatial coordinate embeddings to provide preliminary modeling of document layout, yet subsequent studies indicate that relying solely on geometric position information is insufficient to capture complex layout structures [37, 38]. To address this, researchers have introduced hierarchical structures [39], markup languages [40], and other techniques for structured modeling, and have gradually established reading order as a key variable linking spatial layout and semantic logic [41–45]. Recent methods such as ROAP [20] and DeepSeek-OCR 2 [21] further encode topology priors related to reading order directly into the attention computation of Transformers via structure-aware attention biases and query mechanisms, achieving end-to-end rearrangement of unordered visual blocks into an ordered logical sequence. These studies collectively demonstrate that explicitly encoding latent structures into attention [21] is more effective than simple spatial modeling, inspiring us to transfer topology-aware attention design to temporal causal reasoning. 

Graph Structures and Neuro-Symbolic Methods for Complex Reasoning. To alleviate the lost-in-the-middle problem in long-context reasoning [8], researchers have begun exploring graph structures and neuro-symbolic methods. In retrieval-augmented generation, path planning and subgraph retrieval based on knowledge graphs have effectively enhanced multi-hop reasoning capabilities [28, 46– 48, 29, 49, 50]. In broader complex reasoning tasks, explicitly encoding entity relations via graph structures [51–53] or introducing symbolic working memory [30, 54] has improved the stability of models in handling cross-task compositional rules. However, the above methods are primarily oriented towards non-temporal scenarios, such as general reasoning and commonsense question answering, and lack targeted designs for complex temporal dependencies and counterfactual reasoning mechanisms. In contrast, the TCR-Agent framework proposed in this paper explicitly models temporal causal topology through an external causal graph and interventional topology pruning. 

Temporal Perception and Causal Reasoning in LLMs. Standard retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems typically treat temporal information as shallow textual features, struggling to handle the dynamic evolution of events and long-range dependencies [1, 2, 7, 8]. Early methods relying on timestamp injection or multi-turn prompting [7, 8] remain at the level of superficial temporal ordering and cannot support deep causal modeling [5, 8, 55], a limitation also revealed by multiple benchmarks such as TRAM [56], ETRQA [57], and CausalBench [31]. Although subsequent studies have introduced strategies such as temporal graphs [11, 32], multi-hop data [58], and instruction tuning [59] to enhance structured temporal representations, these methods still essentially depend on external prompts or intermediate structures and do not internalize temporal biases and causal constraints into the core attention mechanism of the model. In contrast to these works, TCR-Former introduces temporal span biases and a causal-topological attention mask into the attention layers, thereby fundamentally reconstructing a nonlinear causal attention mechanism with topology-awareness. 

# B Limitation and Future Work 

Although the proposed TCR-Agent and TCR-Former demonstrate significant performance advantages in complex temporal causal reasoning tasks and effectively alleviate the core "structure-reasoning disconnection" dilemma in existing methods, this study still has several limitations that warrant further exploration and refinement in future work. 

Limitation. In terms of computational efficiency and training paradigms, TCR-Agent relies on multiple calls to large language models and cascaded scheduling of an external symbolic engine, resulting in high end-to-end inference latency that makes it difficult to meet high-throughput, low-latency real-time decision-making requirements. While TCR-Former reduces inference overhead by approximately 62% through architectural internalization, its three-stage training strategy is rel-atively complex. It has currently been validated mainly on Qwen-series backbones, leaving its cross-architecture generalization yet to be further verified. Second, regarding the causal represen-tation construction mechanism, the causal graph instantiation in this paper heavily relies on large language models’ text-extraction capabilities, resulting in an essentially "narrative-driven" topolog-ical reconstruction. In long texts with implicit information, ambiguous references, or conflicting 16 expressions, hallucinations or structural biases during extraction may propagate downstream through the DAG, thereby affecting the reliability of counterfactual intervention. Third, regarding the bench-mark’s multimodal coverage, the proposed TempoCausal-Bench currently focuses solely on the plain-text modality. However, numerous temporal causal reasoning tasks in real-world scenarios involve multimodal signals such as text, charts, and numerical sequences. The existing benchmark cannot measure the model’s ability to fuse cross-modal information and perform collaborative causal inference, limiting the extrapolation and validation of architectures such as TCR-Former for practical multimodal applications. 

Future Work. To address the above limitations, future work will proceed along the following directions. To improve efficiency, we will explore end-to-end joint optimization or representation distillation strategies to simplify the training pipeline and introduce sparsification or linear-complexity variants to reduce the computational overhead of dynamic masking. Meanwhile, we will attempt to establish formal connections between the Causal-Topological Attention mechanism and structural causal models, thereby providing theoretical bounds on the identifiability and error propagation in counterfactual deduction. We will further investigate causal discovery and uncertainty modeling, extending beyond the pure text extraction paradigm. By integrating symbolic causal discovery algorithms or incorporating Bayesian graph structure learning, we will probabilistically model causal edge weights and intervention paths, thereby quantifying the structural uncertainty during topology construction and enhancing the robustness of counterfactual truncation. To expand the multimodal benchmark, we plan to extend the evaluation dimensions of the TempoCausal-Bench to include multimodal scenarios, constructing a temporal causal reasoning evaluation dataset that includes modalities such as images, structured tables, and time-series plots. By designing tasks such as cross-modal event alignment, multi-source information conflict resolution, and chart-based trend reasoning, we will systematically assess the model’s capability in causal topology reconstruction and counterfactual deduction in complex multimodal environments, providing a standardized testbed for developing a unified multimodal temporal causal reasoning backbone.

C.2 Reasoning Efficiency Analysis 

To systematically evaluate the computational efficiency of each method in practical deployment, we test the inference overhead of various methods on the TempoCausal-Bench using Qwen-3-8B, with a batch size of 1 to simulate real-world deployment scenarios, running on 2 × NVIDIA A100-40G GPUs. C.3 Causal Graph Robustness Analysis 

To evaluate the impact of causal graph extraction errors on downstream reasoning, we randomly select 500 samples from TempoCausal and conduct noise injection experiments based on TCR-Agent (GPT-5.4) and its ablation variant (w/o Dual-Track Reasoning). Specifically, we design two perturbation paradigms: 

(1) Edge Deletion: randomly removing directed edges to simulate dependency omission. 

(2) Node Interference: replacing node text with irrelevant, distracting text to simulate entity semantic distortion. Fig. 3(b) illustrates the performance degradation curves under the two types of noise. After removing dual-track reasoning, the system’s sensitivity to noise increases significantly. Taking the 15% noise ratio as an example, under edge deletion, the accuracy of the complete TCR-Agent decreases from 70.4% to 68.5% (a drop of 1.9%), whereas that of the ablation variant drops sharply from 65.2% to 58.2% (a drop of 7.0%). The contrast is even more pronounced under node interference: at a high noise ratio of 30%, the complete TCR-Agent still maintains an accuracy of 57.2%, while the ablation variant plummets to 42.3%, far below the Standard baseline without a causal graph (51.3%). These results indicate that the original text pathway in dual-track reasoning provides critical fault tolerance against causal graph noise. Through fact-logic dual-track synergy, TCR-Agent can correct deviations by referencing the original text even when the causal graph is corrupted, thereby achieving significantly enhanced robustness. 

C.4 Error Attribution Analysis 

To systematically analyze the failure mechanisms of models in complex temporal tasks, we summarize error attribution into four primary error patterns. 18 (1) Temporal Boundary Misjudgment: inability to accurately locate the start and end ranges of implicit temporal windows or concurrent states, corresponding to typical error types in dimensions D1 and D2. 

(2) Causal Chain Breakage: omitting critical nodes, reversing chronological order, or incurring intervention pruning failure in multi-hop dependencies, predominantly manifested in dimension D3. 

(3) Rule Inference Mismatch: when faced with hypothetical questions or long-cycle lagged deduc-tion, the model ignores explicit calculation rules and quantitative constraints given in the text, instead relying on superficial word associations or general commonsense to make erroneous inferences, leading to deduction results that severely violate the original textual logic; concentrated in dimensions D4 and D5. 

(4) Other Errors: errors not falling into the above categories. Based on Qwen-3-8B, we randomly sample 200 instances per method and perform error attribution. Fig. 3(c) presents the distribution of error types for each method. The Standard prompt and the three prompt-enhanced baselines all exhibit high proportions across the three primary error types. Although TG-LLM and TISER achieve slight improvements by introducing graph structures or reflection mechanisms, the overall gains are limited. Upon deeper analysis of the experimental results, we observe a noteworthy structure-reasoning disconnection phenomenon: external graph structures and CoT reasoning can make the model’s reasoning structure more precise, but they fail to address the model’s deficiency in deductive capability fundamentally. As shown in Fig. 3(c), compared with existing methods, TCR-Agent drastically reduces the error proportions of temporal boundary misjudgment (8.4%) and causal chain breakage (9.9%). Yet, rule inference mismatch remains as high as 13.0%, nearly on par with TISER (13.8%). Even when LoRA fine-tuning is applied on top of TCR-Agent, rule inference mismatch only drops to 9.2%, significantly higher than the other two error types (5.9% and 7.4%), further corroborating the structure-reasoning disconnection phenomenon. This indicates that relying solely on external graph structures for reasoning still struggles to handle error types like Rule Inference Mismatch that demand deeper deductive capabilities. In contrast, TCR-Former keeps the proportions of all three error types below 3.4%, with rule inference mismatch at only 2.8%, achieving balanced, substantial convergence across all dimensions. The above results indicate that relying solely on external structural constraints can achieve only static reconstruction of the causal topology, whereas embedding temporal causal priors into the attention flow and representation update mechanisms can simultaneously overcome the dual challenges of precise reasoning structure and insufficient deductive capability.

# E TempoCausal-Bench 

E.1 Five-Dimensional Evaluation 

Existing mainstream temporal benchmarks mostly rely on explicit timestamps to anchor questions, allowing models to answer easily through shallow matching and making it difficult to reach deep temporal causal reasoning. We build the TempoCausal-Bench by upgrading linear temporal question answering into a structured temporal causal reasoning task through a five-dimensional reconstruction paradigm, based on the difficult samples from TimeQA-Hard [8]. The specific dimensions are introduced as follows. 

(1) Implicit Temporal Windows and Conditional Filtering. Existing benchmarks often directly provide explicit time ranges, weakening the model’s autonomous ability to perceive event boundaries. This dimension aims to test the model’s reasoning ability to delineate temporal boundaries through event anchors without explicit dates. 

(2) Concurrent Multi-Entity State Tracking. Traditional temporal data mostly unfold linearly along a single timeline. This dimension aims to test the model’s ability to synchronously track the independent state evolution of multiple interacting entities within the same time window. 

(3) Temporal Lags and Multi-Hop Dependencies. Causal chains in real narratives often span multiple paragraphs. This dimension tests the model’s ability to concatenate and reconstruct scattered event fragments with relative time offsets from across the text into a complete long-range causal chain. 

(4) Counterfactual Cascading Deduction. This dimension tests the model’s understanding of a system’s intrinsic rules. By reversing or removing key historical nodes, the model must deduce subsequent timeline evolution based on the quantitative rules provided in the text. 

(5) Lagged Amplification of Minute Perturbations. Inspired by the bullwhip effect in supply chains, this dimension aims to test the model’s ability to capture long-range lag propagation and asymmetric causal relationships. # F TCR-Former Training Strategy 

To equip the model with end-to-end reasoning capability from fragmented narratives to topological causal reconstruction while avoiding gradient conflicts and representation degradation commonly encountered in multi-task joint optimization, we propose a three-stage training strategy. During training, the optimization objectives for temporal perception, cross-modal alignment, and causal 25 reasoning are decoupled, enabling synergistic incremental improvement while ensuring stable con-vergence of each module. In the first stage, the temporal causal encoder is trained, with the base language model’s parameters frozen and only the event anchor embeddings, time span biases, and the timestamp regression head updated. In the second stage, the cross-modal projector is trained, with all encoder and decoder parameters frozen and only the nonlinear projection layer optimized. In the third stage, end-to-end instruction fine-tuning is performed, with the encoder and projector frozen and the decoder base efficiently fine-tuned using LoRA. TCR-Former is trained on 4 × NVIDIA A100-40G GPUs.

F.3 Third Stage End-to-End Instruction Tuning 

The third stage performs end-to-end instruction tuning across the entire TCR-Former architecture, strengthening its reasoning capabilities for complex temporal question-answering and counterfactual deduction tasks. In this stage, all encoder and projector parameters are frozen, and low-rank adaptation (LoRA) [62] is used for parameter-efficient fine-tuning of the decoder base. The rank hyperparameter

F.4 Training Dataset Construction 

To support the three-stage training of TCR-Former, we constructed a structured training dataset based on multiple public temporal reasoning benchmarks and the TempoCausal-Bench. 

F.4.1 First Stage Dataset 

The first stage aims to train the encoder for temporal causal perception and structured modeling. We extracted and integrated 60,000 long-text narrative paragraphs from public datasets, including TGQA [4], TempReason [27], and TimeQA [8]. These texts cover multiple domains, including biographies, historical events, and organizational changes. The core of data construction lies in extracting event sequences that contain causal associations from the text along with their corresponding times. Specifically, for each text, we utilized DeepSeek-V3.2 to annotate explicit and implicit temporal and causal cues, forming a set of ordered event-timestamp pairs. Each event is summarized in a concise description and paired with the time point at which it occurs. The physical time is first uniformly converted to a floating-point number with years as the unit. For time points specified only with a year, the fractional part is zero. Subsequently, all timestamps are normalized to the [0, 1] interval, enabling the encoder’s regression head to predict relative temporal positions stably. Through this process, each 27 training sample contains a sequence of original text chunks, a sequence of extracted causal event descriptions, and a corresponding sequence of normalized target timestamps. # G LLM-Acc 

Since EM and F1 lack semantic judgment, we introduce a semantic-level accuracy metric, LLM-Acc. This metric employs two LLMs, DeepSeek-V3.2 and Gemini-3.1-flash, as independent evaluators. For a generated answer, each evaluator compares the predicted answer with the standard answer for semantic equivalence and gives a binary judgment of correct or incorrect. The final LLM-Acc is the average of the two evaluators’ scores. During evaluation, the generation temperature of both evaluators is set to 0 to ensure determinism and reproducibility. The prompt template for the LLM is shown in Table 12.    

> Table 12: LLM judgment prompt example.
> LLM Judgment Prompt Example You are an expert evaluator for temporal causal reasoning tasks.
> Your job is to judge whether a model’s predicted answer is semantically equivalent to the ground-truth answer. Do not focus on superficial wording differences. Focus on the core facts, logical consistency, and causal implications. If the prediction conveys the same meaning as the ground truth, it should be considered correct.
> Question: {question}
> Ground-Truth Answer: {ground_truth}
> Model’s Response Answer: {model_response} Based on the above, is the Response Answer semantically correct? Please first provide a brief reasoning for your decision, then output your final verdict. Use the following format exactly: <reasoning> [Your reasoning here] </reasoning> <verdict> Correct or Incorrect </verdict>

Fig. 4 illustrates the confusion matrix of LLM judgments based on samples from Qwen-3-8B, and we use Cohen’s Kappa to measure the inter-judge agreement between the two LLMs. The average Kappa value across different methods is 0.826, indicating good agreement. To further verify the reliability of this automatic evaluation metric, we conducted a human review on 200 randomly sampled instances. The absolute deviation between human accuracy and LLM-Acc is 1.5%, validating that the automatic evaluation scheme using LLMs as evaluators can effectively replace human evaluation with sound robustness.

H.3 TISER 

TISER [15] is a framework designed to enhance the temporal reasoning capabilities of large language models. It extends the traditional CoT into a four-stage cyclic inference paradigm comprising preliminary reasoning, timeline construction, reflection and verification, and answer generation, by scaling reasoning traces at test time. As shown in Table 15, the specific workflow of TISER is as follows: 

(1) Stage 1 – Initial Reasoning. The model first generates a preliminary, raw reasoning trajectory based on the provided question and temporal context. 

(2) Stage 2 – Timeline Construction. Relevant temporal events are extracted from the context and the initial reasoning trajectory and organized into an explicit, ordered timeline. 

(3) Stage 3 – Reflection and Verification. The model compares its initial reasoning trajectory with the newly constructed timeline to self-verify its logic. This stage is responsible for detecting and correcting inconsistencies, ambiguities, or omissions in the initial reasoning, thereby generating a refined reasoning trajectory. 

(4) Stage 4 – Answer Generation. Relying on the error-corrected reasoning trajectory and the structured timeline, the model ultimately generates a concise, accurate, and logically coherent answer.

# I Ablation Study Setup 

To systematically analyze the actual contributions of each design element in TCR-Agent and TCR-Former, we conduct component-level ablation on the two frameworks on the TempoCausal-Bench. 

I.1 TCR-Agent Ablation Setup 

The complete TCR-Agent pipeline based on GPT-5.4 contains three key stages: causal graph extrac-tion, counterfactual intervention, and dual-track reasoning. By removing critical components from each stage individually, we obtain the following ablation variants. 

w/o Scratchpad: During the causal graph extraction stage, the LLM is required to directly output the raw graph Graw with nodes and edges, without generating intermediate reasoning steps. The instruction regarding the scratchpad is removed from the extraction prompt, and the content of the scratchpad S is completely excluded from subsequent reasoning. 

w/o DAG Instantiation: The structured DAG instantiation step is skipped. The extracted Graw is directly serialized into a textual description as a list of natural language edges, without performing multi-dimensional attribute projection, timestamp consistency verification, or topological sorting with the directed acyclic constraint, thereby degenerating into a flat relational chain. 

w/o Counterfactual Intervention: When processing counterfactual-type queries, the graph inter-vention engine is deactivated, and the original causal graph Graw is used for reasoning, meaning no intervention operation is performed on the causal structure. 

w/o Dual-Track Reasoning: The dual-track reasoning is downgraded to a single-track mode. Only the serialized description of the structured causal graph D(G′) is provided to the LLM as context, while the original narrative text is excluded, thereby isolating the auxiliary effect of unstructured factual details on structured logical deduction. 

Standard: All external symbolic modules of TCR-Agent are completely removed, and only the original text and question are directly fed to GPT-5.4, which is required to output an answer, serving as the upper-bound reference for the pure language model baseline. 

I.2 TCR-Former Ablation Setup 

The complete TCR-Former architecture introduces core designs, including the causal-topological attention mask, explicit time-span bias, and fact-logic bypass fusion, on top of the Qwen-3-8B decoder. The role of each mechanism is examined through the following structural degradations. 

w/o Causal-Topological Attention Mask: The causal-topological attention mask is replaced with the standard strictly lower-triangular causal mask. 

w/o Time Span Bias: The learnable time span bias embeddings are frozen and removed, causing the initial representation of query tokens to degrade to relying solely on the position-independent base anchor embeddings. Meanwhile, the time bias matrix is no longer added during attention computation. 

w/o Fact-Logic Bypass Fusion: At the decoder input, the word embedding track Etext of the original text is removed, and only the aligned causal latent variables Zaligned from the encoder and 33 the instruction prompt embedding Eprompt are concatenated and fed into the decoder. This setup forces the generation process to rely entirely on the topology representation compressed in the latent space to examine the degree of factual information retention during compression. 

Only Decoder: The encoder and the projection layer are removed, retaining only the native Qwen-3-8B. Since it cannot execute the first two training stages, we perform instruction tuning on it using the high-quality causal question-answering data generated in the third stage. 

Only Encoder + Decoder: Only Qwen-3-0.6B is adopted as the encoder and Qwen-3-8B as the decoder, with the projection layer between the two models, undergoing the full three-stage training pipeline of TCR-Former.
