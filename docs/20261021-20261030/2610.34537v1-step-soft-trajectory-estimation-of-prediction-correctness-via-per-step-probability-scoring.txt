## Abstract 

Estimating the correctness of large language model (LLM) predictions without access to ground truth is critical for reliable deployment in high-stakes settings. Existing approaches, such as entropy and perplexity, operate solely on final output statistics, discarding the rich information encoded in intermediate reason-ing steps. To address this limitation, we pro-pose STEP (Soft Trajectory Estimation of 

Prediction correctness) , a training-free frame-work that scores each reasoning step via lightweight forward passes to obtain a soft probability distribution qi over candidate an-swers, from which we derive a set of soft tra-jectory features capturing per-step confidence and its progression toward the final answer. Extensive experiments on various benchmarks demonstrate consistent and substantial improve-ments, particularly in AUROC, over output-based baselines and hard-indicator trajectory methods. Notably, Temporally-Weighted Con-sistency , which assigns higher importance to later reasoning steps where answer distribu-tions have stabilized, contributes the largest gains, highlighting the importance of temporal structure in reasoning trajectories. These re-sults further validate the advantage of preserv-ing continuous probability signals over binary discretization. 

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across a wide range of rea-soning tasks, from mathematical problem solving to multi-step commonsense inference. Despite their impressive performance, these models remain prone to generating incorrect answers with high apparent confidence, making correctness estima-tion at inference time a critical requirement for

reliable deployment. It is essential, particularly in high-stakes settings such as medical question an-swering, legal reasoning, and automated decision support, where acting on a wrong prediction can carry serious consequences. Existing methods typically rely on final output statistics derived from the model’s token-level dis-tributions: entropy over the output (Malinin and Gales, 2021; Kuhn et al., 2023), token-level per-plexity (Guo et al., 2017), or the probability as-signed to the most likely answer (Hendrycks and Gimpel, 2017). Although these signals are sim-ply accessible from the model’s logits, they oper-ate at the level of individual tokens and capture only local fluency and output sharpness (Guo et al., 2017). They do not reflect whether the model has engaged in coherent, logically grounded reasoning at a semantic level (Kuhn et al., 2023). A model can assign high probability to a wrong answer sim-ply because that answer is superficially plausible (Kadavath et al., 2022); conversely, a correct an-swer may receive modest token-level probability when the model remains genuinely uncertain yet ultimately reaches the right conclusion. Without ex-amining the reasoning process itself, such methods lack a principled connection to semantic correct-ness. Another approach to correctness estimation is to dive into the latent space of the model. A line of work has shown that the internal representations of LLMs carry signals correlated with factual cor-rectness, and lightweight probes trained on hid-den states can reliably predict output correctness (Azaria and Mitchell, 2023; Kossen et al., 2024). However, even lightweight probes require labeled training data and are sensitive to distribution shift, limiting their applicability when the task domain or model changes. Our approach requires no such training, and is thus applicable out of the box across tasks and models. arXiv:2610.34537v1 [cs.CL] 25 Oct 2026 Another line of work exploits the internal geome-try of reasoning trajectories. Wang et al. (2025) and Bi et al. (2025) derive correctness signals from the trajectory of hidden states across layers, showing that correct and incorrect responses exhibit mea-surably different geometric patterns in embedding space. A related but distinct approach is taken by Zhang et al. (2025), who operate in the output probability space rather than the latent space: cor-rect trajectories exhibit high consistency, where intermediate steps steadily converge toward the final answer, while incorrect trajectories deviate erratically before settling on a wrong conclusion. However, this framework has two limitations that prevent direct application to inference-time cor-rectness estimation. First, it requires sampling N

independent trajectories per prompt, which scales inference cost proportionally. Second, correctness is measured via hard binary indicators that discard the full spectrum of confidence information, treat-ing near-chance and near-certain probabilities iden-tically. In this work, we propose a training-free frame-work for correctness estimation of LLM output in closed-set tasks, which represent a broad and prac-tically important class of real-world applications: from standardized tests and professional certifica-tion exams to clinical decision support and auto-mated document classification, many high-stakes settings inherently present a finite, predefined set of options. Inspired by the trajectory-based insight of Zhang et al. (2025), we address both limitations by re-scoring the already-generated reasoning tra-jectory: truncating it at each intermediate step i and performing a lightweight forward pass to obtain a soft probability distribution qi over all candidate answers, requiring no additional generation and no resampling. This per-step distribution is a strictly richer sig-nal than a binary correct/incorrect label: it cap-tures not just which answer the model favors at each step, but how confidently and consistently it does so across the full trajectory. From these dis-tributions, we derive a family of soft trajectory features reflecting distinct aspects of reasoning dynamics (Section 3). We conduct extensive experiments across 4 datasets and 2 models, evaluating our fea-tures against output-based methods, training-free trajectory-based methods, and latent-trajectory ap-proaches. Our soft features achieve consistent and substantial improvements, particularly in AUROC, across models and datasets. Our contributions are as follows: 1. We propose STEP , a single-trajectory, training-free framework for correctness esti-mation in closed-set tasks, grounded in per-step soft probability distributions over the can-didate answer set. 2. In STEP, we introduce three soft trajectory metrics that preserve continuous confidence signals discarded by prior binary-indicator methods. 3. We provide systematic empirical evidence across four benchmarks and two open-source models that soft trajectory features achieve strong and consistent performance relative to existing baselines, and find that later reason-ing steps carry disproportionately more pre-dictive signal. 

## 2 Related Work 

Output-statistics-based uncertainty estimation. 

A foundational line of work estimates output re-liability from token-level statistics derived from a single forward pass. Predictive entropy over the output distribution (Malinin and Gales, 2021), length-normalized log-probability, and the maxi-mum softmax probability (Hendrycks and Gimpel, 2017) are canonical examples. These signals are cheap to compute but operate at the level of in-dividual tokens: they capture local fluency and output sharpness rather than semantic correctness. A model can assign high token-level probability to a wrong answer that is superficially fluent, and low probability to a correct but uncertain answer (Guo et al., 2017). 

Semantic and sampling-based methods. To capture meaning-level uncertainty, Kuhn et al. (2023) introduce semantic entropy , which clusters multiple sampled responses by semantic equiva-lence and computes entropy over the resulting dis-tribution, substantially improving over token-level baselines. Farquhar et al. (2024) extend this to a practical hallucination detection method, demon-strating its effectiveness at scale. However, both ap-proaches require sampling N responses per prompt, which is a significant inference overhead that limits deployment in latency-sensitive settings. Probing-based methods. A complementary line of work shows that LLM hidden states encode sig-nals correlated with truthfulness and correctness. Burns et al. (2023) and Marks and Tegmark (2024) show that truthfulness is linearly represented in activation space and can be recovered without su-pervision, by finding directions that separate true from false statements. While these unsupervised methods require no correctness labels, they still re-quire fitting to the target distribution and have been shown to generalize poorly to structurally different inputs. Supervised probes face the same limitation more acutely: Azaria and Mitchell (2023) train a classifier on hidden layer activations to predict statement truthfulness, and Kossen et al. (2024) train linear probes to approximate semantic en-tropy from a single forward pass. In both the su-pervised and unsupervised settings, the reliance on distribution-specific fitting limits applicability when the task domain or model changes, which is a limitation our training-free approach avoids entirely. 

Trajectory-based correctness estimation. Sev-eral works exploit the structure of the reasoning process rather than the final output alone. Wang et al. (2025) show that the trajectory of hidden states across layers exhibits measurable differences between correct and incorrect responses: correct trajectories tend to travel greater distances in em-bedding space and maintain more consistent direc-tion, and their CoE-R and CoE-C scores aggregate these geometric signals as training-free correctness estimates. Bi et al. (2025) model the dynamics of hidden state transitions across reasoning steps from a theoretical perspective, deriving a scalar score that reflects the soundness of the reasoning trajec-tory. These methods operate entirely in the latent space and require white-box access to specific layer activations. A related but distinct approach is taken by Zhang et al. (2025), who operate in the output probability space rather than the latent space. In their CoVo 

framework, correct trajectories exhibit high con-sistency (intermediate steps consistently favor the final answer) and low volatility (the last deviating step occurs early in the chain). Our method builds on this insight but addresses two key limitations: 

CoVo requires sampling N independent trajecto-ries per prompt, and reduces per-step confidence to hard binary indicators, discarding fine-grained confidence information. We exploit the closed-set property of the task to operate on a single trajectory, and replace binary indicators with soft probability distributions that preserve the full confidence sig-nal. 

Chain-of-thought reasoning and faithfulness. 

Wei et al. (2022) demonstrate that prompting LLMs to generate intermediate reasoning steps substan-tially improves accuracy on multi-step tasks. Akey assumption underlying trajectory-based cor-rectness estimation, including our own, is that these intermediate steps are meaningful reflections of the model’s reasoning process. Our method exploits this structure by truncating the chain-of-thought at each step and probing the model’s implicit answer preference, treating the reasoning trajectory as a window into the model’s evolving commitment to candidate answers. This is complementary to pro-cess reward models (Lightman et al., 2024), which also exploit step-level structure but require anno-tated training data; our approach is training-free and derives correctness signals directly from the model’s output distribution at each step. 

## 3 Method 

3.1 Problem Formulation 

We focus on closed-set reasoning tasks , in which a model is given an input question x

and a predefined set of M candidate answers 

A = {a1, a 2, . . . , a M }, and generates a chain-of-thought (CoT) followed by a final answer ˆa ∈ A .Given the question x, candidate set A, and the generated chain-of-thought C, our goal is to com-pute a scalar correctness score ϕ(x, C) ∈ R that correlates with whether ˆa matches the ground-truth answer, without access to labels. We evaluate meth-ods using AUROC, AUPR-C and AUPR-E (see Section 4.1 and Appendix C for formal definitions). 

3.2 Per-Step Answer Distribution Step Segmentation. The model is prompted to generate reasoning in a structured format, with each logical step on a separate line. The resulting chain-of-thought C is segmented into T discrete steps 

{s1, s 2, . . . , s T }. The prefix context at step i is defined as 

ci = [ x, s 1, s 2, . . . , s i], i = 1 , . . . , T. 

Each prefix ci represents the model’s reasoning state after i steps: it captures everything the model has considered up to that point, without revealing how the reasoning will continue. By probing the model at each such prefix, we obtain a snapshot of its implicit answer preference at every stage of the reasoning process, allowing us to track how confidence evolves across the full trajectory rather than relying solely on the final output. Notably, this segmentation requires no additional generation: the steps {s1, . . . , s T } are extracted directly from the already-generated chain-of-thought, and each prefix ci is constructed by simple truncation.

To probe the model’s implicit preference for each candidate an-swer at step i, we append a short answer-elicitation prompt to ci and perform a single forward pass to obtain next-token logits (see Appendix A). Baselines. We compare against three categories of baselines. (1) Final-output statistics : Entropy ,token-level perplexity ( Perplexity ), and maximum softmax probability ( MaxProb ), which aggregate token-level statistics over the entire generated se-quence and discard the reasoning structure entirely. (2) Latent-trajectory methods : CoE-R and CoE-C (Wang et al., 2025), which derive correctness scores from the geometric trajectory of hidden states across layers, and CoT-Kinetics (Bi et al., 2025), which models the dynamics of hidden state transitions across reasoning steps. (3) Hard token-trajectory methods : CoVo-Con , CoVo-Vol , and 

CoVo-Linear , which are adaptations of the consis-tency and volatility concepts introduced by Zhang et al. (2025) to our single-trajectory setting. These variants derive confidence from per-step binary cor-rectness indicators rather than soft probabilities, discarding fine-grained confidence information. 

Evaluation Metrics. To better evaluate the quality of different scoring methods, we report AUROC (Fawcett, 2006), AUPR-C and AUPR-E (Boyd et al., 2013) as evaluation metrics, fol-lowing established protocols for misclassification detection (Hendrycks and Gimpel, 2017). AU-ROC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Charac-teristic curve) measures the overall discriminative ability between correct and incorrect predictions. AUPR-C (Area Under the Precision-Recall curve for the Correct class) and AUPR-E (Area Under the Precision-Recall curve for the Error class) treat correct and incorrect predictions as the positive class, respectively. Since LLM accuracy on our benchmarks ranges from 60% to 90%, the class dis-tribution is heavily skewed toward correct predic-tions, making AUROC alone insufficient (Corbi` ere et al., 2019). AUPR-C reflects the ability to confirm high-confidence correct predictions, while AUPR-E captures the more practically important ability to identify errors among the minority class. Formal definitions are provided in Appendix C. 

4.2 Main Results 

Table 1 presents AUROC, AUPR-C, and AUPR-E of all methods on four benchmarks and two models. STEP consistently outperforms all baselines. 

Across all datasets and both models, soft trajec-tory features achieve the best overall performance, surpassing output-statistics-based, latent-trajectory, and hard token-trajectory baselines in the vast ma-jority of settings. Although individual metrics oc-casionally favor a specific baseline on a particular dataset, the improvements are consistent across models and tasks, validating that preserving contin-uous probability signals across reasoning steps pro-vides richer information for correctness estimation than either final-output statistics or hard trajectory indicators. 

Weighting favoring later steps further boosts performance. Among the three variants of STEP, Temporally-Weighted Consistency Con temp 

achieves the best evaluation metrics in nearly all settings, outperforming the simpler Soft Consis-tency Con soft and Margin Consistency Con margin ;we recommend it as the single deployed estima-tor, reporting the other two as ablations rather than alternatives to combine at inference time. This sug-gests that not all reasoning steps contribute equally to correctness estimation: later steps, where the model’s answer distribution has begun to stabilize, carry more predictive signal. Con soft and Con margin 

still substantially outperform hard token-trajectory methods despite treating all steps uniformly, con-firming the gain from soft signals is orthogonal to the aggregation strategy.

The base variant of STEP, Soft Consistency , which simply averages q∗ through-out the chain-of-thought, is therefore shown to be effective.

Performance saturates at large β. Gains of AUROC plateau around β ≈ 5 to 10 and remain stable thereafter. To further verify that later steps are more informa-tive than earlier ones, we also explore negative val-ues of β, which up-weight early steps instead. Per-formance degrades consistently across all datasets, confirming our hypothesis: early steps, where the model has not yet begun to commit to any can-didate and the answer distribution remains noisy, carry weaker signal than the later steps where mean-ingful reasoning commitment emerges. 

## 5 Conclusion 

We presented STEP, a training-free framework for correctness estimation in closed-set reasoning tasks. By performing lightweight forward passes on an already-generated chain-of-thought, STEP derives soft trajectory features from per-step probability distributions over the candidate answer set. Across multiple benchmarks and two models, all three instantiations of STEP consistently outperform ex-isting baselines in AUROC while achieving com-petitive or improved AUPR performance. Among them, Temporally-Weighted Consistency achieves the strongest performance, confirming that later rea-soning steps carry disproportionately more predic-tive signal. These results validate the core premise of STEP: preserving continuous probability signals across reasoning steps provides richer information than hard binary indicators, at no additional gen-eration cost. Future work may explore extending soft trajectory features to open-ended settings by constructing candidate sets dynamically from the model’s top-k predictions at each step. 

## Limitations 

Scope of applicability. STEP is designed for closed-set tasks with a predefined candidate an-swer set, and is not directly applicable to open-ended generation. It further requires that candi-date answers be disambiguable by their first token; answers sharing a common prefix would require additional aggregation strategies. 

Dependence on reasoning structure. STEP re-lies on structured chain-of-thought reasoning to construct per-step prefixes. Models producing un-structured or highly variable reasoning, as well as formal symbolic tasks where correctness depends on exact computation rather than progressive se-mantic narrowing, may not benefit consistently. 

Access requirements. STEP requires access to next-token logits, available for open-source models but not for black-box APIs.

## A Answer-Elicitation Prompt 

To compute the per-step answer distribution qi

(Equation 1), we append the following prompt to the truncated context ci before performing the for-ward pass: 

Final Answer: 

This prompt is designed to elicit the model’s implicit preference over candidate answers at the current reasoning state, without generating any ad-ditional reasoning content. By appending it im-mediately after the truncated chain-of-thought, we place the model in a position where its next-token distribution directly reflects its answer preference given everything it has reasoned so far. The logits over the candidate answer tokens are extracted at this position and normalized via softmax over the candidate set to obtain qi, as described in Equa-tion 1. No further tokens are generated beyond this single forward pass. 

## B Chain-of-Thought Generation Prompt 

All models are prompted with the following tem-plate to elicit structured chain-of-thought reasoning. The placeholder {input data } is replaced with the dataset-specific question and answer choices. The candidate set is {A, B, C, D, E } for Common-senseQA and {A, B, C, D } for all other datasets.          

> Answer the following multiple choice question. Explain your reasoning in a few statements. Each statement should represent one small logical improvement over the previous statements. Write each statement on its own line. Do not number or label the statements. The final line must be: Answer: X Where X MUST BE one of A, B, C, D [, or E]. Do not write anything after the answer.
> {input data }

The prompt deliberately avoids numbered lists or structured headers to produce free-form reasoning steps that can be cleanly segmented by newline splitting. Each line of the response (excluding the final Answer: line) constitutes one reasoning step 

sk, yielding a trajectory of length T for confidence scoring.

## F Runtime and Hardware 

All experiments were run on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU, with no model training involved. To-tal inference time per dataset, including the initial chain-of-thought generation and all downstream scoring (final-output statistics, latent-trajectory, hard token-trajectory, and STEP), ranges from 20 minutes to approximately 3–4 hours depending on dataset size and model. Each of STEP’s additional forward passes (Sec-tion 4.1) processes a fixed truncated prefix and produces no additional output tokens. Unlike sampling-based methods, STEP therefore avoids autoregressive generation of additional full reason-ing trajectories.
