## ABSTRACT 

Constraint-based causal discovery relies on conditional-independence (CI) tests that may conflict in finite samples, leaving no causal graph that satisfies them all. Causal Assumption-based Argumentation (ABA) makes these tests explicit as defeasible commitments, and offers correspondence guarantees with a subset of them, but its existing repair procedure releases facts heuristically and offers no formal response to a user’s contestation of a release. We argue that contestable causal discovery requires an inspectable repair object, a justification for it, and a predictable revision procedure for such contestations. We therefore formulate CI-fact repair as a minimum-cost correction problem and implement it in An-swer Set Programming (ASP). Building on Causal ABA, we provide a compact and efficient ASP encoding of causal consistency, with soundness and complete-ness guarantees, and a two-way projection correspondence between optimal stable models and minimum-cost correction sets. These results establish fidelity to re-tained facts, subset-minimality and cost optimality of the repair, conflict-based justifications for released facts, and formally guaranteed redress under user con-testations. An empirical evaluation supports the formal account: OptABA-PC retains more correct facts, reduces ambiguity in the compatible outputs, and im-proves graph reconstruction over Causal ABA on common benchmarks. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Causal discovery supports scientific understanding and decision making by turning observational and/or interventional data into hypotheses about the underlying causal mechanisms. It is a central ingredient in tasks such as explanation, prediction under interventions, and policy evaluation (Pearl, 2009). A wide range of approaches exists, including constraint-based methods relying on inde-pendence testing, score-based methods that optimise a goodness-of-fit criterion over graphs, and functional causal model approaches that leverage assumptions on the data-generating mechanism; see e.g. (Glymour et al., 2019). Constraint-based algorithms such as PC (Spirtes et al., 2000) infer structure from conditional-independence (CI) information. Causal ABA (Russo et al., 2024) instead treats selected CI facts as defeasible commitments that every returned graph must satisfy if enforced, exposing data-derived premises and a point of contestation. In this context, a contestation asks the system to reconsider a result by revising those input facts. Computational argumentation supports this view: contestation should lead beyond static explanation to reliable revision when redress is warranted (Leofante et al., 2024); argumentative decision making captures that reliability through contestability guarantees un-der user-driven changes (Freedman et al., 2025). Finite-sample CI tests may conflict, making Causal ABA incoherent. Causal ABA restores coher-ence by fixed strength-ordered release, revealing discarded facts but offering neither optimality nor a guaranteed response to a contested release or cost. We make the correction set the redress ob-ject and prove optimal repair. Our claims concern CI-fact selection : redress guarantees revised 1arXiv:2610.33138v1 [cs.AI] 23 Oct 2026 Preprint commitments and consistency with them, without guaranteeing recovery of the generating graph. We focus on the realistic regime where finite-sample CI tests are noisy and potentially conflicting. Majority-PC (MPC) (Colombo & Maathuis, 2014), an improved PC variant for finite samples, learns a partially directed graph from CI tests. Under the standard assumptions it is sound with an oracle for error-free CI judgements, but finite-sample outcomes may conflict.

We build on work on explanation and repair in logic programming (Reiter, 1987; Liffiton & Sakallah, 2008; Alviano et al., 2023), particularly minimal unsatisfiable subsets (MUSes) and minimal cor-rection subsets (MCSes). A MUS isolates an irreducible conflict that explains incoherence; an MCS is a minimal set of rules or facts whose removal restores coherence. In Causal ABA, MUSes explain which CI-test outcomes conflict, while MCSes expose the minimal releases available as re-dress when a user contests a result. Operationally, weighted CI facts yield retained and released facts, compatible DAGs, and a conflict certificate for each release; a user can then require a fact or raise its release cost and re-solve. Concretely, we: (i) formulate Causal ABA’s released CI facts as an explicit repair; (ii) prove a two-way correspondence between optimal MCSes and optimal sta-ble models; (iii) prove soundness and completeness of a compact Bayes-ball encoding (Shachter, 1998); (iv) establish conflict certificates and guaranteed hard and graded contestability; (v) imple-ment the optimisation as OptABA-PC; and (vi) compare it with Causal ABA and standard baselines, generally improving fact selection and/or graph reconstruction while providing redress guarantees. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

The relationship between inconsistency, repairs, and hitting sets is well established. In model-based diagnosis, diagnoses are hitting sets of conflicts (Reiter, 1987); in constraint systems and SAT, MUSes expose irreducible conflicts and MCSes repair them by hitting those conflicts (Liffiton & Sakallah, 2008). Related ideas appear in optimisation as maximum feasible subsystem and IIS-cover problems, where one deletes a minimum-weight set of constraints to restore feasibility (Chinneck & Dravnieks, 1991; Amaldi et al., 2003), and in implicit hitting-set methods, which alternate between a feasibility oracle returning an unhit conflict and an optimiser selecting a new hitting set (Moreno-Centeno & Karp, 2013). Core-guided MaxSAT solvers operationalise the same pattern over soft clauses (Morgado et al., 2013). Our use of MUS/MCS terminology therefore does not introduce a new optimisation paradigm; rather, it makes explicit what the repair object is in Causal ABA. In causal discovery, solver-assisted methods have used SAT to enforce constraints from overlapping observational or interventional data and query graph features common to all solutions (Triantafilou et al., 2010; Triantafillou & Tsamardinos, 2015), weighted MaxSAT to infer ancestral relations that maximise satisfied evidence (Magliacane et al., 2016), and weighted constraint optimisation to ob-tain robust representative graphs (Borboudakis & Tsamardinos, 2016). These methods optimise causal conclusions or graph-level agreement; they do not make the correction set of released facts the explicit output. SAT/ASP has also been used to encode d-separation exactly. Hyttinen et al. (2013) introduce vari-ables for directed and bidirected edges and bounded d-connecting walks, then compute backbone features shared by all satisfying cyclic/latent causal graphs; inconsistent inputs yield no repair. Hyt-tinen et al. (2014) handle noisy inputs by deriving fail atoms when a graph violates a CI constraint and minimising their total weight. They study hard-dependence, constant, and log-derived weight-ing schemes; we call the DAG-restricted empirical implementation of this objective ASPCR-DAG. 2Preprint Our proposed OptABA-PC likewise uses weighted optimisation, but its CI-strength weights are re-lease costs, its released facts form an explicit correction set, and every retained fact is satisfied by every returned graph. The encoding of Hyttinen et al. (2014) instead permits penalised fail atoms in an optimal model. Compared to ASPCR, Causal ABA targets DAGs, which permits the compact Bayes-ball encoding while preserving an argumentative reading of retained facts. Hyttinen et al. (2017) recast the same weighted graph-disagreement objective as MaxSAT: weighted unit soft clauses represent CI constraints, hard clauses connect them to graph satisfaction, a SAT solver extracts unsatisfiable cores, and an integer program computes a minimum-cost hitting set of the accumulated cores. This is the closest prior optimisation architecture to OptABA-PC. Its cores and hitting sets already provide conflict/repair machinery; our additional results connect ex-plicit released-fact sets to optimal stable models and establish fact enforcement and contestability guarantees. Rantanen et al. (2020) use branch-and-bound with partial-graph propagation, tailored branching, and core-derived bounds. 1 Complementary SAT/ASP work weakens assumptions or ex-tends the graph semantics (Zhalama et al., 2017; 2019); OptABA-PC keeps Causal ABA’s DAG target and correspondence guarantees (that ASPCR lacks), but adds optimality and contestability. In Causal ABA, accepted CI facts are not merely soft clauses or post-hoc fail atoms: they are en-forceable ABA/ASP facts whose satisfaction is guaranteed in every returned stable model, yielding traceability from data-derived facts to graphs. OptABA-PC makes the released facts first-class: by placing weak constraints on unselected objective atoms, optimal stable models correspond to optimal correction sets of the Causal ABA program. Whereas existing ASP work on MUSes studies subset-minimality, enumeration, and cautious reasoning (Alviano et al., 2023), we connect optimal stable models to minimum-cost correction sets and establish contestability guarantees. Quantitative Argu-mentation for Causal Discovery (QACD) (Wei et al., 2026) also treats noisy CI evidence as graded arguments. It iteratively revises edge acceptability through gradual semantics on abstract argumen-tation frameworks, with no correspondence guarantees between CIs and output DAGs. Instead, our method identifies the optimal release of CI facts, with correspondence and redress guarantees. 

## 3 PRELIMINARIES 

We consider a finite set of variables V. We write X ⊥⊥ Y | Z for conditional independence between variables X and Y given conditioning set Z ⊆ V \ { X, Y }, and X̸ ⊥⊥ Y | Z for dependence ( X ⊥⊥ Y

and X̸ ⊥⊥ Y if Z = ∅). In our causal-discovery setting, a CI fact is a ground assertion of one such relation, obtained from a statistical CI test for a threshold α.A causal graph is a DAG G = ( V, E ) whose edges represent direct causal relations. A node Z on a path is a collider on that path if the two adjacent path edges both have arrowheads into Z, as in 

X → Z ← Y ; otherwise it is a non-collider . A node Y is a descendant of X if there is a directed path from X to Y . A collider is open given a conditioning set Z when the collider itself or at least one of its descendants belongs to Z; otherwise it is closed. DAGs entail CI relations through d-separation (Pearl, 1989): a path between X and Y is active given Z if every non-collider on the path is outside Z and every collider is open given Z; X and Y are d-connected given Z if some such active path exists, and d-separated otherwise. Observational data identify at most a Markov equivalence class (MEC), represented by a completed partially DAG (CPDAG) whose directed edges are shared by all DAGs in the class and whose undirected edges are reversible (Chickering, 2002a). 3.1 ANSWER SET PROGRAMMING

Causal ABA (Russo et al., 2024) uses assumptions for candidate graph choices (directed edges) and CI facts, and rules encoding acyclicity and d-separation. A stable extension of Causal ABA corre-sponds to a candidate DAG together with the CI facts it accepts. Enforcing a CI fact means requiring it to be in the accepted set and rejecting any stable extension whose graph violates it; releasing a fact means allowing it to be absent so coherence can be restored. Under the standard correspondence between ABA under stable semantics and ASP stable models (Rapberger et al., 2024), this ABAF can be solved as an ASP program. Hence Causal ABA inherits both the argumentation reading of assumptions and the computational machinery of ASP. Causal ABA may admit multiple stable ex-tensions for the same enforced CI facts, with corresponding DAGs spanning one or more MECs. This multiplicity is informative: the CI–DAG correspondence guarantees that every candidate satis-fies those facts, while differing graphs expose the structural ambiguity they leave unresolved. We now show how MUSes and MCSes can be used to identify sets of facts that carry desirable properties for repair. 3.4 MUS ES AND MCS ES 

Explaining and repairing incoherence is a classic theme in diagnosis of ASP programs (Reiter, 1987; Liffiton & Sakallah, 2008), and it naturally applies here because noisy CI-test outcomes need not be jointly satisfiable. MUSes and MCSes are defined relative to a base ASP program P and a distinguished set O of 

objective atoms . For S ⊆ O, let enforce (P , S, O) denote the program obtained from P by (i) adding a choice rule over atoms in O and (ii) forcing every atom in S to be true. Concretely: 

enforce (P , S, O) := P ∪ {{ o}. | o ∈ O} ∪ { :- not o | o ∈ S}.

Definition 1 (Alviano et al. 2023) . Let P be a program and O be a set of objective atoms. Then 

S ⊆ O is an unsatisfiable set if enforce (P , S, O) is incoherent. S is a minimal unsatisfiable set (MUS) if no strict subset S′ ⊂ S is an unsatisfiable set. 

Definition 2 (Alviano et al. 2023) . Let P be a program and O be a set of objective atoms. Then 

S ⊆ O is a correction set if enforce (P , O \ S, O) is coherent. S is a minimal correction set (MCS) if no strict subset S′ ⊂ S is a correction set. 

MUSes pinpoint irreducible conflicts, whereas MCSes identify subset-minimal repairs. Write 

M U S (P , O) and M CS (P , O) for the families of all MUSes and MCSes of P w.r.t. O, respectively. Every MCS must hit every MUS; indeed, M CS (P , O) is exactly the family of minimal hitting sets of M U S (P , O) , and conversely (Alviano et al., 2023, Thm. 5.3). Their number may be exponential. 

Example 4 (MUS/MCS intuition) . Consider a program P:                  

> a:-not b, o(1). b:-not c, o(2). c:-not a, o(3). :-not d, o(4).

with objective O = {o(1) , o(2) , o(3) , o(4) }. Enforcing all of O makes enforce (P , O, O) inco-herent, but for two independent reasons : enforcing o(4) forces d even though it cannot be derived, and enforcing o(1) , o(2) , o(3) activates the negative loop over a, b, c. Hence the MUSes are 

{o(4) } and {o(1) , o(2) , o(3) }, while the MCSes are the minimal hitting sets {o(1) , o(4) },

{o(2) , o(4) }, and {o(3) , o(4) }.

5.2 OPTIMALITY AND CONTESTABILITY GUARANTEES 

Theorem 2 (OptABA-PC guarantees) . Let V be finite, O a finite set of valid CI facts over V, and 

W assign strictly positive release weights to every fact in O. Then opt (P CA , O, W) has an optimal stable model. For every such model M , put R = ρ(M ) and T = O \ R. Then: 

(1) πG(M ) is a DAG on V satisfying every fact in T ;

(2) R is an MCS with minimum release cost among all correction sets of PCA w.r.t. O;

(3) for every f ∈ R, enforcing T ∪ { f } is incoherent, and a MUS Uf satisfies Uf ∩ R = {f }.Conversely, every optimal MCS R is ρ(M ) for some optimal stable model M , with the same cost. 

The correspondence is under projection, not a bijection: several optimal stable models may have the same released-fact projection and possibly different graph projections. Our implementation saves a selected repair and separately enumerates compatible graphs within a time budget; the re-ported graph sets need not exhaust the entire family. These guarantees support two forms of user contestation. ## 7 CONCLUSION 

We cast causal discovery from noisy data as transparent CI-fact repair. By aligning optimal MCSes with weak-constraint optimisation, OptABA-PC couples minimum-cost CI-fact repair with guaran-teed CI-DAG correspondence, conflict explanations, and predictable redress. The contribution is optimal and provable redress, not solely competitive graph accuracy. OptABA-PC traceable link from retained facts to every returned DAG supports transparency and contestation. The empirical 9Preprint evidence of higher true CI retention also supports the effectiveness of the approach in contexts where data is limited or uncertain and external input is needed and available, making it particularly suited to high-stakes decisions with modest variable sets. The observed benefits and theoretical guarantees motivate more efficient methods that preserve these properties. Our proposed Bayes-ball encoding is one concrete step in this direction; scalability and robustness to release costs remain priorities.

## A ASP S EMANTICS 

We make explicit the semantics used in the main paper. A finite ground normal rule is 

h :- a1, . . . , a m, not b1, . . . , not bn,

where the head h may be absent. Its body is true in an interpretation I iff ai ∈ I for every i and 

bj /∈ I for every j. The rule is satisfied iff its body is false or h ∈ I; when the head is absent, satisfaction therefore requires the body to be false. For a program P and interpretation I, the Gelfond–Lifschitz reduct P I deletes each rule having some negative body literal not b with b ∈ I and removes all remaining negative body literals (Gelfond & Lifschitz, 1988). Then I is a stable model of P iff it is a subset-minimal model of P I . Non-ground safe programs are interpreted through their finite ground instantiation. The encoding also uses ground choice and disjunctive constructs, including the inherited default-negated head literal in Listing 2, under their clingo stable-model semantics (Gebser et al., 2011; Calimeri et al., 2020). A ground weak constraint : ∼ B. [w@l,⃗ t ] is active in I when B is true in I. At priority l, the solver sums w once for each distinct activated tuple (w, l,⃗ t ) and minimises the resulting cost vectors lexicographically by priority. OptABA-PC uses 

: ∼ not o. [wo@1 , o ]

for every objective atom o. Including o as a tuple identifier is essential: two different facts of equal weight then activate different tuples and are both counted. All release weights are strictly positive.

Totality. It remains to prove that Kf is nonempty for every valid CI fact f . Corollary 1 shows that

A fact-enforcement contestation gives categorical redress by making f mandatory. A user may instead make a graded contestation, increasing the cost of releasing f while leaving it possible. The operational question is then how much that cost must increase before retaining f becomes at least as good as releasing it. 1. Below the margin. If δ < μ (f ), then crel (f ) + δ < c keep (f ). Hence no retaining repair is globally optimal and Rep δ ⊆ Lf .

2. At the margin. If δ = μ(f ), then crel (f ) + δ = ckeep (f ). A minimiser in each of Lf and Kf is therefore globally optimal, so Rep δ contains both a releasing and a retaining repair. 

3. Above the margin. If δ > μ (f ), then ckeep (f ) < c rel (f ) + δ. Hence no releasing repair is globally optimal and Rep δ ⊆ Kf .

Integer threshold. If all weights are integers, both category costs and μ(f ) are integers. The smallest integer increase strictly greater than μ(f ) is therefore μ(f ) + 1 , which by item 3 is the smallest integer increase forcing every returned repair to retain f .23 Preprint The margin is therefore not a graph-quality measure. It is an actionable threshold: μ(f ) = 0 exposes an already optimal retaining alternative, μ(f ) is the increase that creates a retaining–releasing tie, and any larger increase forces retention. Beyond enabling an individual contestation, the margin supports a comparative reading of incon-sistency. Logical incoherence remains binary, but μ(f ) quantifies its weighted consequence for a released fact: it is the additional repair cost incurred by resolving the conflict in favour of retaining 

f , rather than releasing it. Thus, within a fixed trace and weighting scheme, larger margins identify releases that are more strongly supported by the competing evidence and more resistant to reinstate-ment. The normalised margin μ(f )/w f gives the proportional increase in f ’s release cost required to reach a retaining tie, allowing comparisons across facts and traces that use the same weight se-mantics. It should therefore be interpreted as relative weighted tension within the repair objective, not as a probability that f is false or as an absolute degree of logical inconsistency. Appendix G.3 evaluates both hard-retention responses and these margins (Tables 6 and 8). These guarantees concern the enforced CI commitments and have a precise consequence for their graph witnesses. Fix an optimal repair R releasing f and put T = O \ R. By Theorem 2(3), enforcing T ∪ { f } is incoherent; Corollary 1 therefore implies that no DAG satisfying T can also satisfy f . Hard retention, or a cost increase δ > μ (f ), returns only graphs satisfying f , so it excludes every graph compatible with that releasing repair. A graph belonging to a different, already optimal repair retaining f may remain when μ(f ) = 0 . Below the margin, releasing optima remain optimal; at the margin, retaining and releasing optima coexist. Thus changing the enforced facts can force a change of graph witness, without guaranteeing improved graph accuracy or recovery of the generating DAG.

Benchmark choice. Using the benchmark design of Causal ABA (Russo et al., 2024), the fixed-network study includes Cancer ( 5 nodes, 4 edges), Earthquake ( 5, 4), Survey ( 6, 6), and Asia ( 8, 8)from the bnlearn repository (Scutari, 2014). These small, standard Bayesian networks include med-ical, legal, and policy decision models derived from published studies or expert elicitation. They are appropriate for this evaluation for three reasons: their known DAGs and conditional probability ta-bles (CPTs) provide ground truth for both CI facts and graph reconstruction; repeated finite samples expose the test errors and conflicts that motivate repair; and reusing the benchmarks and sampling design of (Russo et al., 2024) makes the comparison with ABA-PC a controlled extension rather than a change of empirical regime. We add Erd˝ os–Rényi (ER) (Erd˝ os & Rényi, 1959) and scale-free (SF) (Barabási & Albert, 1999) DAGs with five and eight nodes to vary topology beyond four fixed networks and to evaluate the MPC (Colombo & Maathuis, 2014) and FGS (Ramsey et al., 2017) reference methods under the same generated samples. 

Data generation. For each dataset we use 50 seeds, 2026–2075, and generate N = 5000 indepen-dent observations per seed. For a bnlearn benchmark, we load its BIF file, including the published DAG and CPTs, and forward-sample a new data matrix for each seed using the sampling protocol of (Russo et al., 2024). Thus the BN is fixed and only the finite sample changes. For a primary syn-thetic run, the seed determines both a new graph and its data-generating distribution. An ER graph is formed with n edges and given a random acyclic orientation and node permutation; an SF graph is generated by directed Barabási–Albert preferential attachment with one attachment per added node and then randomly permuted. Each node is categorical with at least two states; its allowed cardinal-ity is reduced when it participates in a larger parent configuration, using 20 as the implementation’s CPT-enumeration target. For each node and parent configuration, its probability vector is drawn from a symmetric Dirichlet distribution whose concentration is sampled uniformly from [1 , 5] , af-ter which the BN is forward-sampled. Categorical states are numerically encoded and columns are aligned with the generating adjacency matrix. Before CI testing, the bnlearn codes are additionally standardised; this is a one-to-one relabelling and therefore leaves the contingency counts for the G2

CI test (Spirtes et al., 2000) unchanged. FGS independently factorises every categorical column into integer levels for its BDeu score.

Majority-PC: use of tests versus output enforcement A procedure can use a CI test without con-straining its final graph to satisfy that test. This distinction is central to interpreting MPC. Within KPC a CI outcome is consumed during skeleton discovery and collider orientation, but is not posted as a global d-separation constraint on the returned CPDAG. Forcing a local test routine would there-fore only force that procedural branch; other paths, separating sets, or downstream orientation steps could still yield a Markov class contradicting the same relation. MPC also exposes neither a re-tained/released fact set nor a global release-cost objective, so crel (f ) and ckeep (f ) are not defined for it. The final columns of Table 7 report the MPC audit on the same 400 primary traces; the exact 250-trace comparison, including its denser ER(5)/SF(5) setting, appears in Table 6. Since every DAG in one CPDAG has the same conditional-independence model, one consistent extension suffices for the semantic check; endpoint-invalid outputs remain explicit and count as incompatible. MPC’s raw full-compatibility count is dominated by input inconsistency: only 88/400 primary traces are feasible. Conditional on feasibility, it succeeds on 80/88 and leaves eight avoidable gaps, five on ER(5) and three on SF(5). The separate exact audit gives the same conditional pattern: 126/139 compatible feasible traces and 13 gaps, two on ER(5) and 11 on SF(5). The corresponding success rates, 90 .9% and 90 .6% , show that the change in raw counts reflects trace feasibility and graph den-sity rather than a reversal in method behaviour. MPC can still be a strong graph estimator (Table 11), but it neither identifies a repair on inconsistent inputs nor distinguishes unavoidable contradictions from avoidable output gaps.
