# Fed-IGTD: A federated learning-based framework for tabular-to-image encoding 

David Chushig-Muzo, Eva Milara, Francisco J. Lara-Abelenda, Luis Carlos Estrada-Petrocelli, Diego H. Peluffo-Ord ´ o ˜ nez 

Abstract —Tabular-to-image encoding methods, which transform tabular data into image-like representations, have shown promising results in predictive tasks. Despite their benefits, these methods follow a centralized paradigm, in which the data are collected and models are trained on a central device. This raises privacy and security concerns, particularly the risk of data leakage, unauthorized data collection and access to users’ private information. Federated Learning (FL) provides a decentralized framework in which multiple clients jointly optimize a shared model without directly sharing training data. We introduce Fed-IGTD, a horizontal FL-based approach in which clients share a common feature space and upload only local statistics, from which the server reconstructs the global correlation matrix and obtains a feature-to-pixel mapping to train a convolutional neural network via Federated Averaging (FedAvg). We studied two differential privacy (DP) mechanisms: (i) a one-shot Gaussian mechanism protecting the correlation-aggregation step; and 

(ii) a client-level DP-FedAvg variant protecting the training with privacy loss composed via a R ´ enyi-DP accountant. We evaluated Fed-IGTD on five datasets under a systematic sweep of non-IID severity and privacy budgets. Privacy protection applied to the mapping-construction step has little impact on predictive performance. By contrast, DP-FedAvg substantially degrades utility at moderate privacy budgets with a small client cohort ( K = 5 ). Ablation study indicates that this degradation is driven mainly by the injected noise rather than by uniform averaging or update clipping. Macro-F1 exhibits sensitivity to performance degradation than accuracy under non-IID heterogeneity and class imbalance. These findings have direct implications for cross-silo federated deployments characterized by a limited number of participating institutions and heterogeneous local data distributions. These results show the potential of tabular-to-image encoding methods for supporting privacy-preserving learning in decentralized settings. 

Index Terms —Tabular-to-image methods, spatial encoding methods, synthetic images, IGTD, Tabular2image, federated learning

1 INTRODUCTION 

# TABULAR data is ubiquitous across domains such as healthcare, finance, and bioinformatics [1]. Ensemble tree-based models have been extensively used for predic-tive tasks using tabular data because of their high perfor-mance [2]. The adoption of deep learning architectures has been challenging because these models are not inherently designed to handle the characteristics of tabular data. Unlike images, which exhibit an inherent spatial structure where neighboring pixels are related and convolutional filters can capture patterns, tabular data lack inherent spatial organi-zation [3]. Recent studies in representation learning for tabular data have led to significant progress [1]. In particular, trans-forming tabular data into synthetic images has enabled the application of image-based models to structured data [4]. Several tabular-to-image encoding methods have been pro-posed, including DeepInsight [5], REFINED [6], TINTO [7], IGTD [8], TabSOM [9]. Federated Learning (FL) is a novel paradigm to address these challenges, enabling distributed joint training using decentralized data across heterogeneous devices [10]. In-stead of transmitting data from devices to the server, each device uses its local data to independently train a model and only shares model parameters with the server. Although FL has shown excellent results, its application within tabular-to-image methods has been partially explored. 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 Federated learning 

FL enables a shared global model to be trained across decentralized data holders by exchanging model updates rather than raw data [13]. In horizontal FL, clients share an identical feature schema but hold different samples, whereas in vertical FL, clients hold partially overlapping features over the same entities. These approaches require different training and aggregation strategies. In horizontal FL, clients independently train models on their local data and con-tribute model updates to a global aggregation procedure. In contrast, vertical FL typically requires entity alignment (e.g., via private set intersection) and split-model training across the partitioned feature space. Fed-IGTD operates in the horizontal FL setting. FedAvg is the standard aggregation algorithm for hor-izontal FL. At each round, a subset of clients initializes local training from the current global model, and the server aggregates the resulting local models, typically us-ing a sample-size-weighted average, to obtain the global model [14]. Although FedAvg was originally analyzed un-der the IID assumption, practical FL systems often exhibit statistical heterogeneity, as clients’ data reflect distinct local populations (e.g., hospital patient populations or regional customer bases). This non-IID setting has motivated meth-ods such as FedProx, which introduces a proximal term that penalizes local model deviation from the global model, improving training stability under heterogeneous data [15]. Beyond statistical heterogeneity, prior work has identified architectural limitations of parameter-only FedAvg. In par-ticular, FedBN showed that standard batch normalization is problematic under FedAvg because its mean and variance are non-learnable buffers and are therefore excluded from parameter aggregation, preventing the global model from appropriately updating its normalization statistics [16]. FL does not provide formal privacy guarantees. Al-though raw data remain decentralized, the model updates exchanged during training may still disclose information about individual training samples, making FL vulnerable to attacks such as membership inference, inference of prop-erties associated with training data among others [11]. DP provides formal privacy by perturbing data or model com-putations with calibrated noise, thereby reducing the risk of revealing information about individual users [11]. DP-SGD applies this principle at the sample level by clipping and perturbing individual gradients during centralized train-ing [17]. Since DP-SGD releases a noisy statistic at each mini-batch step, naive sequential composition over thousands of iterations can result in excessively loose ε bounds. The moments accountant and its R´ enyi DP (RDP) formulation enable tighter privacy accounting across repeated releases, with the accumulated privacy loss converted to a standard 

(ε, δ ) guarantee at the end of training [18]. DP-FedAvg extends this framework to provide client-level privacy in FL. Each client’s model update is clipped to a bounded norm, and Gaussian noise is added to the aggregated update before it is applied to the global model, providing privacy at the level of client participation rather than individual records [14]. We adopt the client-level threat model of DP-FedAvg. 

2.2 Tabular-to-image encoding methods 

Tabular-to-image methods aims to construct a feature-to-pixel mapping that preserves the underlying feature re-lationships by placing related features to spatially close pixels in the resulting image [4]. These approaches employ linear and nonlinear dimensionality reduction techniques, such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA), t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE), Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), Multidimensional Scaling (MDS), to embed features into a two-dimensional space from which pixel coordinates are derived. For in-stance, DeepInsight uses t-SNE, PCA and kernel PCA to project features into a two-dimensional space, which is then rasterized into a fixed-size image grid shared across all samples [5]. REFINED uses MDS and a hill-climbing algorithm to place correlated features near one another [6]. TINTO uses PCA and t-SNE to determine feature locations and the resulting coordinates are then transposed, scaled, and rounded to integer values [7]. 3

Unlike dimensionality reduction methods, IGTD directly optimizes a feature-to-pixel assignment through iterative pairwise swaps, minimizing the discrepancy between the rank orderings of feature-wise and pixel-wise distances on the target grid [8]. Its rank-based objective depends on the data only through a correlation matrix, which can be reconstructed exactly from additive per-client statistics. This structure yields a relatively simple and computation-ally efficient objective, making IGTD particularly efficient to federated optimization compared with the non-convex, iterative objectives of methods such as t-SNE and UMAP. IGTD is among the most widely used tabular-to-image en-coding methods, having been applied across domains such as cybersecurity [19], [20], the automotive industry [21], and biomedical engineering [22], [23], [24], [25], [26]. Despite these benefits, a decentralized version of IGTD that pro-vides formal privacy guarantees has not been systematically investigated. The combination of FL with tabular-to-image conversion is, to our knowledge, addressed by a single prior study [27]. The authors proposes FedVI, a FL module within a broader cross-domain financial risk-modeling framework [27]. It uses vertical FL, in which clients hold different, only par-tially overlapping feature schemas for the same entities ( e.g., 

a bank and a credit bureau holding different attributes of the same customers). A dual-stream schema-alignment mecha-nism combines semantic and statistical similarity measures to align heterogeneous feature spaces before constructing a shared image representation. FedVI reconstructs the correlation matrix used as input to its IGTD layout step through Gram-matrix aggregation, using a mechanism broadly similar to the horizontal ap-proach presented in this study. However, in its vertical, heterogeneous-schema setting, the reconstruction is approx-imate under non-IID data, with no formal error bounds provided. Additionally, FedVI does not employ DP or pro-vide noise calibration and privacy-loss accounting. Hence, FedVI’s privacy analysis is heuristic and does not provide formal privacy guarantees. It relies on the assumption that recovering the original sample matrix from the aggregated Gram matrix is infeasible when the feature dimension is substantially smaller than the number of samples. 

3 FED -IGTD 

Fed-IGTD is a federated framework for transforming tab-ular data into a shared image representation composed of two stages: mapping image construction and federated training .In the first stage, clients communicate only aggregate statistics from which the server reconstructs the global fea-ture relationships required by IGTD. The server then com-putes a single feature-to-pixel mapping and global feature-normalization parameters and broadcasts them to all clients. In the second stage, clients generate images once using the shared mapping and normalization, and train a CNN using either standard FedAvg or DP-FedAvg. In each round, the server sends the global model, receives client updates, and updates the model using either FedAvg (weighted aver-age, no clipping) or DP-FedAvg (clip locally, server adds Gaussian noise to the sum; uniform average). 3.6 Privacy approaches 

Fed-IGTD’s federated aggregation never transmits raw rows, but statistics such as a Gram matrix are not them-selves anonymous: an aggregated Gram matrix, especially with few clients or small nk, can still leak information about the underlying data distribution. Hence, we provide two complementary mechanisms for differential privacy: 

(i) a Gaussian differential privacy mechanism protecting the correlation-matrix aggregation step that determines the IGTD image layout; and (ii) a client-level DP-FedAvg mech-anism protecting the CNN training process itself across all federated rounds.

4.2 Experimental setup 

Data splitting. For each dataset, we hold out 20% of the samples as a stratified test set (evaluated centrally), with the remaining 80% partitioned across clients for federated train-ing. We simulate a cross-silo federation of K = 5 clients, all of whom participate in every communication round. To systematically control non-IID severity, we adopt the com-monly used Dirichlet-based label-skew protocol, in which each client’s class proportions are sampled from a Dirichlet distribution with concentration parameter α. Larger α yields a distribution closer to IID, whereas smaller α produces stronger label skew. This provides a single interpretable parameter for controlling the degree of heterogeneity. We considered a concentration parameter α ∈ { 1000 , 1.0, 0.1},corresponding to approximately IID, moderately non-IID, and severely non-IID partitions. 

Federated simulation. Each client independently computes its local statistics (nk, S k, G k), which are aggregated by the server to reconstruct the global correlation matrix and normalization bounds. The resulting IGTD mapping is op-timized via swap-based iterative manner with a maximum of 3000 iterations and a patience threshold of 400 consecu-tive non-improving proposals. The image grid dimensions 

H ×W are chosen as the smallest near-square grid satisfying 

H × W ≥ d for each dataset’s feature count d.

Model architecture and training. Each client trains a com-pact CNN classifier consisting of two convolutional layers (16 and 32 channels, 3 × 3 kernels) with Group Normal-ization followed by a fully connected layer (64 units) with dropout ( p = 0 .3) and a final classification layer. Models are trained via Adam (learning rate 10 −3) for 15 federated rounds with 1 local epoch per round, using either standard FedAvg (weighted by local sample count, no clipping) or client-level DP-FedAvg. As a centralized reference, we train the same architecture on the pooled training data using the centrally computed IGTD mapping. 

Privacy configuration. For the one-shot Gram-matrix DP mechanism, we sweep the privacy budget ε ∈ { 8, 2, 0.5}

at fixed δ = 10 −5, alongside a non-private baseline, holding non-IID severity fixed at α = 1.0 to isolate the effect of privacy noise from that of client hetero-geneity. For DP-FedAvg, we sweep the noise multiplier 

σ ∈ { 10 −4, 0.05 , 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8, 1.5, 3, 6, 12 }, reporting the actually-achieved ε at δ = 10 −5 as computed by the R´ enyi-DP accountant after composing privacy loss across all 15 training rounds, rather than a nominal, unaccounted noise level. The DP-FedAvg sweep uses the noise-free federated correlation reconstruction to construct the IGTD mapping. DP-FedAvg protects only the federated training, not the mapping-construction step. The R´ enyi-DP accountant eval-uates R’enyi orders α ∈ 1.1, 1.2, . . . , 10 .9 ∪ 11 , 12 , . . . , 63 ,comprising 143 orders in total, and reports the tightest resulting (ε, δ ) bound. The per-client update clip bound S

is calibrated per dataset via a short, non-private probe (3 rounds without noise), taking the median observed update norm as a practical estimate. 

Communication cost. Mapping construction requires each client to upload one Gram matrix ( O(d2)) and one sum vector ( O(d)) in a single round, after which the server broadcasts the resulting mapping as an O(d) lookup ta-ble plus O(d) normalization bounds – a fixed, one-time cost independent of subsequent training. Federated training then transmits one full model parameter vector per client per round, for 15 rounds, regardless of d; for the model architecture used here (Section 4.2), this per-round cost is several orders of magnitude larger than the one-time O(d2)

mapping-construction cost for every dataset in our suite, since d ≤ 60 ≪ the number of model parameters. 

Evaluation. We report two metrics: test accuracy and macro-averaged F1 score, the latter weighting all classes equally and therefore more sensitive to degradation on un-derrepresented classes. All federated and centralized results 7

are averaged over 5 independent seeds (3 for Wine Quality, due to its larger sample count and correspondingly higher per-run training cost), each seed governing the train/test split, the Dirichlet client partition, and model initialization jointly. We report mean and standard deviation across seeds.

4.4 Centralized versus federated predictive perfor-mance 

Figure 3 compares centralized and federated predictive per-formance under FedAvg across five clients with moderate label skew ( α = 1 .0) and without DP, establishing a baseline prior to the introduction of more severe non-IID heterogene-ity or privacy perturbation. As shown in Figure 3 (a), fed-erated training yields consistently lower accuracy than the centralized reference across most datasets, with decreases ranging from approximately two to ten percentage points for Diabetes (from 0.734 to 0.716), German Credit (from 0.780 to 0.749), Wine Quality (from 0.789 to 0.748), and Sonar (from 0.851 to 0.748). In contrast, Ionosphere demonstrates greater robustness to federated training, with accuracy de-creasing only from 0.947 to 0.938. It is also the only dataset for which the confidence intervals of the centralized and federated estimates exhibit substantial overlap, indicating limited performance loss under federation. The macro-F1 values in Figure 3 (b) reveal a larger centralized–federated performance gap than is apparent from accuracy alone, particularly for Sonar and German Credit. This finding reflects the greater sensitivity of macro-F1 to class-specific performance degradation. Unlike ac-curacy, macro-F1 assigns equal weight to each class, pre-venting errors concentrated in minority classes from be-ing masked by the performance of the majority class. Ionosphere again exhibits the smallest and most stable centralized–federated discrepancy across both metrics, sug-gesting that the impact of federated training is inherently dataset-dependent. 

4.5 Effect of non-IID severity across domains 

We first characterize the three federation severity levels (IID, Moderate, and Severe) at the client level. Figure 9 in Appendix B shows the resulting Dirichlet label-skew parti-tions across all datasets, with each row reporting client-level sample counts and class distributions for the three sever-ity levels, together with the mean normalized per-client entropy. Note that 1.0 indicates a perfectly balanced class distribution and 0.0 indicates a single-class distribution. For every dataset, increasing federation severity is accompanied by a monotonic reduction in mean entropy, although the underlying partition characteristics vary across datasets. For instance, German Credit exhibits the most extreme severe partition in terms of mean entropy (0.18), with four of the five clients receiving fewer than 10 samples, effectively concentrating the available training data in a single domi-nant client. Ionosphere provides a contrasting case, with a relatively high mean entropy of 0.62 despite approximately 85% of the samples being assigned to a single client, because the dominant client’s local class distribution remains com-paratively balanced. 4.7 Characterizing the privacy–utility tradeoff in client-level DP-FedAvg 

Figure 6 characterizes the DP-FedAvg privacy–utility trade-off by examining two complementary quantities: the frac-tion of client updates clipped per round (left) and the resulting test macro-F1 (right), both as a function of the achieved privacy budget ε. For three of the five datasets (Diabetes, German Credit, and Wine Quality), the two quan-tities exhibit a clear inverse relationship: decreasing ε is associated with a progressively higher clipping fraction and lower macro-F1. This behavior aligns with the mechanism described above, as noise grows relative to the clip bound S,a larger share of client updates are truncated before aggre-gation, degrading the federated model’s effective training signal. Sonar and Ionosphere, however, deviate from this pat-tern. For Sonar, the clipping fraction remains nearly constant across the ε range, decreasing slightly from 0.46 to 0.44 at the tightest privacy budgets, while macro-F1 nevertheless de-clines sharply from approximately 0.74 to 0.40. Ionosphere exhibits a similar disconnect, with the clipping fraction varying only modestly and non-monotonically, reaching a peak of approximately 0.48 before declining to 0.40, whereas macro-F1 drops from approximately 0.95 to 0.27. For these two datasets, the clipping fraction alone does not fully explain the observed degradation in macro-F1, suggesting that the magnitude of the injected Gaussian noise relative to the model and dataset sizes constitutes an additional determinant of DP-FedAvg utility. This effect appears par-ticularly pronounced for smaller datasets, such as Sonar with 208 samples, as well as for datasets that exhibited comparatively greater instability under severe skew in the non-IID experiments. These results indicate that the clipping fraction is an important, but not sufficient, determinant of the DP-FedAvg utility decline. Clipping appears to be the primary contributor to performance degradation in larger and more homogeneous datasets, while the magnitude of the injected noise relative to model and dataset scale in-troduces an additional, dataset-dependent effect that is not captured by clipping frequency alone.

5 DISCUSSION 

This paper introduces Fed-IGTD, a horizontal FL-based framework for privacy-preserving tabular-to-image trans-formation based on IGTD. The proposed approach enables the correlation structure required by IGTD to be recon-structed through federated aggregation, without requiring the underlying tabular data to leave individual clients. Across the five datasets, the results demonstrate substantial dataset dependence in the robustness of Fed-IGTD to fed-eration, statistical heterogeneity, and DP. For instance, Iono-sphere, despite achieving near-ceiling performance under centralized and IID settings, exhibits pronounced sensitivity to severe non-IID skew and DP-FedAvg noise, whereas Diabetes and German Credit show comparatively grad-ual performance degradation. This pattern suggests that models operating near the performance ceiling may have limited tolerance to perturbations, such that relatively small changes in the learned decision boundary can result in substantial losses in predictive performance. Sonar exhibits a different form of sensitivity, which is consistent with its limited sample size. The consistency of these patterns across the non-IID severity analysis, the DP-FedAvg clipping analysis, and the direct comparison of privacy mechanisms further indicates that dataset characteristics play a substantive role in deter-mining federated robustness. These findings underscore the importance of reporting dataset scale, class distribution, and baseline performance when evaluating federated tabular-to-image methods, as aggregate predictive performance alone may not adequately characterize robustness across different data regimes. The privacy experiments also reveal an important dis-tinction between protecting a single preprocessing step and protecting the complete learning procedure. Gram-matrix DP introduces limited utility degradation across the evalu-ated privacy budgets because privacy protection is applied only to the one-shot correlation-aggregation step preceding model training. In contrast, client-level DP-FedAvg applies perturbation throughout the iterative training process, re-sulting in substantially greater and more dataset-dependent utility degradation, particularly for the small client cohort (K = 5 ) considered in this study. These results motivate a layered approach to privacy protection in practical de-ployments of Fed-IGTD. When protection of the correlation-aggregation step is sufficient for the intended threat model, Gram-matrix DP offers a comparatively low-cost privacy mechanism. When protection of the complete training pro-cess is required, DP-FedAvg provides stronger end-to-end protection but may require additional resources, such as a larger client cohort, or a less stringent privacy target to preserve useful predictive performance. The appropriate mechanism therefore depends not only on the desired pri-vacy budget but also on which components of the pipeline must be protected. The evaluation also highlights the importance of metric selection when assessing federated and privacy-preserving learning. Across the experiments, macro-F1 was generally more sensitive to degradation than accuracy, particularly under severe non-IID skew and strong DP perturbation. This difference reflects the fact that accuracy can remain relatively stable when performance deteriorates dispropor-tionately on minority classes, whereas macro-F1 gives equal weight to each class. Consequently, accuracy alone may ob-scure practically important degradation in class-imbalanced federated settings. This consideration is particularly rele-vant for cross-institutional deployments, where heteroge-neous class distributions and limited per-client sample sizes are common. We therefore recommend reporting macro-averaged or per-class metrics alongside accuracy when evaluating federated tabular-to-image methods, especially under distribution shift or privacy constraints. To our knowledge, FedVI remains the only previously published system that combines FL with tabular-to-image encoding, providing an important point of comparison for Fed-IGTD. The two approaches differ in several fundamen-tal points. First, FedVI adopts vertical FL in which heteroge-neous client feature spaces require a dual-stream schema-alignment procedure, resulting in an approximate recon-struction of the correlation matrix under heterogeneous data distributions. In contrast, Fed-IGTD adopts horizontal FL that permits the required correlation structure to be recon-structed, independent of client heterogeneity. Second, the privacy treatment differs substantially be-tween the two frameworks. FedVI does not provide aformal DP mechanism or explicit privacy-loss accounting. Fed-IGTD, by contrast, incorporates two formally speci-fied privacy mechanisms with explicit guarantees: Gaussian perturbation of the one-shot correlation-aggregation step and client-level DP-FedAvg for protection throughout the training process. The latter is composed using a R’enyi differential privacy accountant to obtain an explicit (ε, δ )

guarantee rather than relying on an uncalibrated noise level. 13 

Third, whereas the empirical evaluation of FedVI is limited to two financial credit-risk datasets and three participating clients, our evaluation spans five domains, including health-care, finance, chemistry/agriculture, signal processing, and radar/ physics, and systematically examines both non-IID severity and privacy budgets. This evaluation reveals dataset-dependent robustness patterns that are not apparent from a single-domain setting. 

6 CONCLUSIONS 

This work introduced Fed-IGTD, a horizontal FL for privacy-preserving tabular-to-image transformation based on IGTD. Fed-IGTD preserves the IGTD representation without requiring raw tabular data to leave individual clients. Results on five datasets demonstrated that the framework preserves utility under IID federation and showed that privacy protection applied only to the one-shot correlation-aggregation step incurs substantially lower utility costs than protecting the full training procedure with client-level DP-FedAvg, whose utility degradation becomes particularly pronounced in small-client regimes and under stringent privacy budgets. Fed-IGTD provides a principled framework for integrating tabular-to-image representation learning with horizontal federated learning and formal pri-vacy guarantees, while offering a foundation for extending this approach to larger federations, heterogeneous feature schemas, secure aggregation, and broader privacy–utility analyses. The results demonstrate the potential of IGTD-based image representations to facilitate the application of deep learning to diverse tabular data domains within privacy-constrained and distributed settings.
