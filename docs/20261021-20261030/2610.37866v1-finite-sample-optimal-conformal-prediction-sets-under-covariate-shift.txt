Abstract 

We study conformal prediction under covariate shift with a known covariate density ratio, seeking prediction sets with minimum expected length under the target distribution. For every calibration sample size, we prove that the rule combining the inverse conditional-density score with randomized weighted conformal calibration minimizes this expected length among all rules valid over the full weighted-exchangeability class. We approximate the oracle using quantile and kernel histograms, including forest-weighted estimators, and select estimators and tuning parameters by resampling to estimate calibrated target length. On a bounded response domain, we bound excess length linearly in L1 density error at fixed calibration size, without requiring a uniform positive lower bound on the true density. We also bound selection-pool and resampling errors. Under explicit growth conditions, the selected procedure approaches the same oracle while the calibration size remains fixed. Experiments on 36 known-density configurations and four public regression datasets compare coverage, prediction-set length, and runtime with existing methods. In targeted low-coverage and strong-shift comparisons, inverse-density selection yields shorter mean sets in most cases and lower mean runtime than the evaluated weighted baselines, with some differences in achieved coverage. 

Keywords: conformal prediction, covariate shift, prediction sets, finite-sample optimality, model selection 

# 1 Introduction 

Conformal prediction provides finite-sample marginal coverage under exchangeability without a correctly specified prediction model (Shafer and Vovk, 2008; Angelopoulos and Bates, 2023). Under covariate shift, calibration and test laws P, Q share the conditional response density f (y | x) but have different input distributions. Weighted calibration retains target coverage when w = dQ X /dP X

is known (Tibshirani et al., 2019). We study which calibrated rule minimizes target expected prediction-set length at a fixed calibration size. Existing methods use quantile regression (Romano et al., 2019), conditional distributions or densities (Chernozhukov et al., 2021; Izbicki et al., 2020, 2022; Wang and Qiao, 2025), quantile histograms (Sesia and Romano, 2021; Luo and Zhou, 2025), length-oriented optimization (Kiyani et al., 2024; Le Bars and Humbert, 2025), or unions of a prescribed number of intervals (Gao et al., 2025). Their coverage constraints and optimality benchmarks differ. For finite-sample optimality, 1arXiv:2610.37866v1 [stat.ML] 27 Oct 2026 we build on Tibshirani, Barber, and Ramdas (2026, Theorem 7) and its precedents (Hoff, 2023; Koning and van Meer, 2025). Our main contributions are: 1. Weighted finite-sample optimality. For every calibration size m, randomized weighted calibration with score 1 /f (y | x) minimizes target expected volume among all rules valid over the full class of weighted permutation mixtures (Theorem 1). Risk is evaluated under P m × Q; validity is required over the full weighted class. The density ratio enters calibration but cancels from the optimal score ordering. Prediction sets may be disconnected. 2. Estimation and excess length. We bound excess length by score error and, on a finite-volume response domain, by density error without a uniform positive density lower bound or clipping (Section 4). For normalized estimates, Proposition 8 gives a linear L1 bound at fixed m and an example showing that its error order cannot be improved uniformly. Quantile and kernel histogram bounds, including forest weights (Section 5), yield fixed-m consistency under the conditions in Section 6.2. 3. Selection by calibrated target length. Building on conformal predictor selection (Yang and Kuchibhotla, 2025; Liang et al., 2026), we analyze bootstrap calibration from a labeled pool and length evaluation on an independent input pool (Section 6.1). Separating approximation, pool, and resampling errors gives consistency after selection toward the same fixed-m oracle under explicit growth conditions (Corollary 14). Independent final calibration retains exact target marginal coverage. Figure 1 summarizes inverse-density prediction with length selection or negative log-likelihood (NLL) selection.

A further study removes the density floor, fixes m = 200, and increases training, selection pools, and bootstrap repetitions. Four public datasets compare coverage, transformed-scale length, and runtime; controlled comparisons separate score and density-selection effects. Highest predictive density (HPD) scores (Izbicki et al., 2022) measure the estimated conditional probability mass of responses with higher density than the candidate response. In targeted comparisons, inverse-density selection yields shorter mean sets than our weighted HPD implementation in most tested low-coverage settings and than our weighted implementation of distributional conformal prediction optimized for short intervals (DCP-opt) (Chernozhukov et al., 2021) in most tested strong-shift settings. Measured mean runtime is lower throughout these comparisons (Section 7.5; Appendix D.1). These comparisons use common nominal coverage levels, with some differences in achieved coverage. Sections 2–6 develop the framework and theory; experiments and related work follow in Sections 7 and 8.

# 8 Related work and benchmark scope 

Finite-sample optimality. Faulkenberry (1973) constructs prediction regions by conditioning on a sufficient statistic. Hoff (2023) combines conditional testing with Bayes risk minimization under frequentist coverage constraints; Koning and van Meer (2025, Theorem 8 and Corollary 2) derive conformal optimality within an e-value framework. Tibshirani, Barber, and Ramdas (2026) give a direct testing derivation and explain these precedents. Our proof applies conditional optimization to weighted permutation constraints, with risk under P m × Q and validity over Hw.

Density estimation and prediction sets. Weighted calibration and density-based regions are established (Tibshirani et al., 2019; Izbicki et al., 2020, 2022). Wang and Qiao (2025) estimate conditional densities by reference-distribution classification for weighted treatment-effect inference, motivated by a population density-threshold optimum. Our finite-m benchmark includes calibration randomness. CTI supplies our equal-mass quantile-bin ordering (Luo and Zhou, 2025). Full CD-split partitions inputs and calibrates locally; our global comparator uses one calibration cell. HPD-split and DCP transform estimated densities or CDFs, with DCP also adjusting interval shape (Izbicki et al., 2022; Chernozhukov et al., 2021). Their benchmarks and Gao et al. (2025)’s restriction to k

26 intervals differ from ours. Other interval methods include CQR, CHR, and CIR/CIR+ (Romano et al., 2019; Sesia and Romano, 2021; Guo et al., 2026). 

Selection and data reuse. CPL optimizes length under coverage moments (Kiyani et al., 2024); EffOrt and Ad-EffOrt optimize error quantiles (Le Bars and Humbert, 2025). VFCP selects by width before independent final calibration (Yang and Kuchibhotla, 2025). ModSel-CP and ModSel-CP-LOO incorporate the hypothesized response into selection, retaining exchangeable finite-sample coverage without a separate selection/calibration split and providing width guarantees under candidate-class regularity (Liang et al., 2026). Our separate-pool selector uses fresh weighted calibration and targets a fixed-m oracle. Their published guarantees assume exchangeability, so we do not include a weighted adaptation. ORCA learns radii around conditional samples (Zheng and Zhu, 2026); WGCP combines generated quantiles with estimated shift weights (Li et al., 2026). Isaev et al. (2026) study nonasymptotic CQR and fixed-score calibration limits under known shift. Theorem 1 compares all rules valid over Hw at the same calibration size. 

# 9 Discussion 

The consistency results require growing estimation resolution and selection resources; the original fixed-library, 30-resample experiments do not implement these sequences. Optimality requires validity over Hw (Appendix A) and concerns marginal coverage and volume on the stated response scale. It does not imply conditional coverage, optimality among k-interval sets, or shortest original-unit length after a nonlinear transformation. All formal coverage guarantees use the true density ratio; estimated-weight error remains outside this analysis.

# B Synthetic distributions and complete configuration grid 

Section 7.1 specifies the density pairs, sampling design, bin counts, and bandwidths. Quantile histograms use empirical j/B quantiles without grid shrinkage and fixed outer endpoints 0 and 1. Each Gaussian kernel is normalized on [0 , 1] before bin integration; the resulting kernel histogram is mixed with the uniform density using weight 10 −6.
