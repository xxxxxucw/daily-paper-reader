## “Not” is Not Lost in Translation: A Causal Analysis of Negation Representations in Multilingual LLMs 

Giang Son Nguyen 1,† Wray Buntine 2,3 Dung D. Le 2 Wenya Wang 1

Abstract 

Negation occurs in every language, yet its sur-face realization differs drastically. Despite this, multilingual large language models (LLMs) translate negation robustly, raising the question of whether they share an underlying represen-tation of negation across languages. We study this question in six open models across En-glish, French, Vietnamese, and Chinese, using causal interventions on a synthetic contrastive dataset and a negation translation dataset de-rived from FLORES-200. Across models, we find strong evidence that negation represen-tations are causally controllable and largely shared across languages. In particular, we show that a contrastive activation direction ex-tracted from one language can induce or re-move negation in the others, and directions ex-tracted from different languages are substan-tially aligned. We further find that negation is represented in sparse features: a single sparse autoencoder (SAE) feature per model suffices to control polarity across languages, while re-sponding selectively to negated inputs. Finally, both dense and sparse interventions general-ize to four additional languages and FLORES-derived sentences. We release data and code to support future work: a negation-translation.

Mechanistic interpretability (Olah et al., 2020; Elhage et al., 2021) offers tools to study internal representations, with recent work find-ing evidence of shared grammatical features and refusal directions across languages (Brinkmann et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). However, whether negation representations are shared during transla-1arXiv:2610.32281v1 [cs.CL] 23 Oct 2026 tion remains less clear. Brinkmann et al. (2025) identify features associated with negation in two LLMs, but report weak cross-lingual sharing and do not test their causal role. Meanwhile, Zhou et al. (2026) study negation mechanisms in mono-lingual English, and Lasnier et al. (2026) ana-lyze translation-related mechanisms without target-ing specific semantic phenomena. We instead in-vestigate this question via representation-based causal interventions and cross-lingual interven-tion transfer , testing whether negation representa-tions extracted from one language can control nega-tion polarity in others. To this end, we construct two complementary datasets. First, we create Mini-minus-4 , a con-trolled set of minimally contrastive sentence pairs in four languages, to extract and evaluate nega-tion representations. Second, we derive FLORES-minus-4 from FLORES-200 (NLLB Team, 2024) by introducing negation into naturalistic sentences, to test whether the intervention effects persist on longer, out-of-domain sentences. We release both datasets to facilitate future research. We first identify candidate negation representa-tions as contrastive activation directions and val-idate their causal role through interventions that add or remove negation during translation, using Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) (Rimsky et al., 2024) (§4.1; examples in Figure 1). We then examine whether these directions are shared across languages through cross-language transfer and similarity analysis (§4.2). Next, we investigate whether individual sparse autoencoder (SAE) fea-tures (Huben et al., 2024; Templeton et al., 2024) also capture negation and exhibit causal effects across languages (§4.3). Finally, we test whether the intervention effects of both dense directions and sparse features generalize to unseen languages and naturalistic sentences (§4.4). We study six open models from three families — Gemma 3, Llama 3.1, and Qwen3/3.5 (Gemma Team, 2025; Llama Team, 2024; Qwen Team, 2025, 2026) — with English, French, Vietnamese, and Chinese as the primary languages. Our findings suggest that negation representations are 

causally controllable, transfer strongly across languages, and exhibit substantial cross-lingual alignment . Specifically, we show that: • Negation representations are controllable and shared across languages : intervening along a single contrastive direction adds or removes negation, and directions extracted from one language retain strong control in oth-ers. Per-language directions are substantially aligned. • Negation is represented in sparse features: 

a single SAE feature can respond selectively to negated contexts and exert strong causal control over polarity across languages. • These causal effects generalize: both dense directions and sparse features flip polarity in unseen languages and naturalistic sentences.

2.2 Dataset Construction 

We construct two complementary datasets. Mini-minus-4 provides controlled minimal pairs for ex-tracting and evaluating negation representations, while FLORES-minus-4 tests whether their inter-vention effects can generalize to naturalistic sen-tences. Full construction steps are in Appendix C. 

Mini-minus-4 is a synthetic minimally con-trastive pair dataset in English (EN), Vietnamese (VI), French (FR), and Chinese (ZH). Using GPT-5.4-mini (OpenAI, 2026b), we generate single-clause sentences with explicit verbal negation us-ing the primary marker of each language: not (EN), 2không (VI), ne…pas (FR), and 不 (ZH). Each neg-ative sentence is paired with a positive counterpart identical in lexical content and structure, differing only in polarity. The purpose of this minimally contrastive setup is to isolate the negation signal us-ing the mean difference between positive and neg-ative activations. Examples of Mini-minus-4 pairs are shown in Figure 2. We construct 350 pairs per language, split into 

150 extraction / 100 evaluation / 100 test pairs. The extraction split is used to estimate represen-tation and steering directions, the evaluation split for hyperparameter selection and analysis, and the test split for held-out evaluation. Predicates do not overlap across splits. To test generalization beyond the four languages used for representation extrac-tion and selection, we additionally construct 100 test pairs each in Russian (RU), Hindi (HI), Arabic (AR), and Indonesian (ID). 

FLORES-minus-4 is a multilingual negation translation dataset, derived from the FLORES-200 devtest split (NLLB Team, 2024) by inducing nega-tion into the original sentences. We first use GPT-5.6-Luna (OpenAI, 2026a) to select sentences with a main clause whose predicate can be meaningfully negated, then generate a polarity-flipped counter-part in a separate prompt. Finally, GPT-5.6-Terra verifies that the result is grammatical and preserves the original meaning apart from polarity. Since the dataset supports any-to-any translation, we re-tain an item only when it is verified in all four lan-guages , yielding 216 items .

Activation extraction. At layer l, we extract residual-stream activations at the output of decoder block l. We consider two token positions (Fig-ure 2): the negated predicate (NP), the first token of the negated verb and its corresponding positive counterpart 1, and the final source token (FST), typically the period.

2.5 Intervention Success Evaluation 

We evaluate intervened translations using four cri-teria. Thresholds and judge prompts are provided in Appendix B.2. 1. Polarity. The output exhibits the intended polarity change. For Mini-minus-4, we use language-specific negation-marker detec-tion rules. For FLORES-minus-4, GPT-5.6-Terra judges whether the intervened output is negated or affirmative. 2. Target language. fastText language identifi-cation (Joulin et al., 2016, 2017) verifies that the output is in the requested target language. 3. Non-degeneracy. The output satisfies min-imum token-count and unique-token-ratio thresholds, filtering out degenerate or repeti-tive generations. 4. Content preservation. For Mini-minus-4, the output must exceed a chrF threshold (Popović, 2015) against the model’s baseline translation of the opposite-polarity counter-part. 3 For FLORES-minus-4, GPT-5.6-Terra judges whether the baseline and steered trans-lations describe the same underlying content except for the polarity difference. 

> 3We compare against the model’s own baseline output so that this criterion measures wording changes introduced by steering rather than divergence from a human reference.

We compute the Flip Rate as the percentage of eligible examples that satisfy all four criteria. An example is eligible only if the un-intervened base-line translation preserves the source polarity. This prevents attributing incorrect baseline polarity to the intervention. Where necessary, we refer to this metric as the Addition Rate (positive −→ negative) and Removal Rate (negative −→ positive). 

Human verification. We verify our automatic evaluation with human evaluators (details in Ap-pendix B.3). Evaluators independently judged all steered outputs of Gemma-3-4B on both datasets, covering all languages and both flip modes. Over four languages, human evaluations agree with the rule-based evaluator on Mini-minus-4 in 96.5% and with the LLM judge on FLORES-minus-4 in 93.7% of samples. These high agreement rates sup-port the reliability of the automated Flip Rates ,which we use throughout our experiments.

5 Related Work 

Negation in NLP and machine translation. 

Language models often under-utilize negation cues (Kassner and Schütze, 2020; Ettinger, 2020), and modern LLMs continue to struggle on negation-focused benchmarks (Hossain et al., 2020; Truong et al., 2023; García-Ferrero et al., 2023). Dropped or inverted polarity is also a long-documented fail-ure mode in machine translation (Fancellu and Webber, 2015; Tang et al., 2021). However, this literature is largely behavioral, leaving how multi-lingual LLMs internally represent negation during translation underexplored. 

Linear representations and activation steering. 

The linear representation hypothesis posits that high-level concepts correspond to directions in ac-tivation space (Park et al., 2024). Causally effec-tive directions have been identified for truth, sen-timent, and refusal (Marks and Tegmark, 2024; Tigges et al., 2024; Arditi et al., 2024), and can be manipulated through activation steering (Turner et al., 2024; Rimsky et al., 2024). In this work, we employ activation steering to validate the causal role of identified representations. 

Sparse feature decomposition. Sparse autoen-coders (SAEs) decompose dense activations into sparse feature dictionaries (Huben et al., 2024; Templeton et al., 2024), with pretrained SAEs released for several major model families (Mc-Dougall et al., 2025; He et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2026). In multilingual settings, Al Ghussin et al. (2026) show that SAEs trained on multilingual data yield more reliable cross-lingual steering, while Nguyen et al. (2026) find that most SAE features do not causally transfer across languages despite recurring activations. Whether SAE features can causally control desired behaviors as effectively as dense directions remains contested (Wu et al., 2025b; Jørgensen and Hansen, 2026; Arad et al., 2025). 

Cross-lingual structure in multilingual LLMs. 

Evidence for language-neutral representations in multilingual LLMs is mixed. Some work finds English-centric or dominant-language semantic spaces (Wendler et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025a), while Brinkmann et al. (2025) find shared latent grammatical concepts across typologically diverse languages using SAEs. Causal studies similarly range from a universal refusal direction (Wang et al., 2025) to language-dependent steering effec-tiveness (Schut et al., 2025). 

Adjacent mechanistic work. Zhou et al. (2026) study negation mechanisms in monolingual En-glish, while Lasnier et al. (2026) analyze mecha-nisms supporting machine translation. The former does not study cross-lingual translation or causal polarity control, and the latter targets whether and in which language translation occurs rather than specific semantic content. Brinkmann et al. (2025) identify SAE features associated with negative po-larity, but find weak cross-lingual sharing. They also do not examine dense directions or test the causal role of these features. We instead investi-gate whether negation representations are causally shared through cross-language interventions on both dense directions and sparse features. 

6 Conclusion 

In this work, we investigated how multilingual LLMs represent negation during translation in four main languages. We showed that negation can be causally controlled in multilingual LLMs through both dense activation directions and individual SAE features. Importantly, these representations transfer across languages despite large differences in how negation is expressed, generalizing both to naturalistic sentences and to languages unseen during representation extraction. Similarity anal-ysis revealed negation directions are substantially similar, and activation inspection showed SAE fea-tures consistently responding to negation in every language. These findings suggest that multilin-gual LLMs encode negation in a largely shared and causally controllable representation. 87 Limitations 

Our extraction and interventions focus on verbal negation marked by explicit lexical cues (e.g., not ,

không , ne…pas ). We also focus only on the transla-tion task. Morphological negation (e.g., un-, im-), implicit negative expressions (e.g., fail , lack ) and the role of negation representations in other tasks remain for future work. Activation addition, like other linear steering methods, depends on the α coefficient (the steering strength), which is a known limitation in the steer-ing literature (Vu and Nguyen, 2025; You et al., 2026; Aparin and Gaintseva, 2026). We select α

empirically by sweeping values on the evaluation set rather than a principled selection. For sparse feature analysis, we rely on pre-trained SAE releases, thus our analysis does not cover all models. Our feature-level interpretation is also limited to one selected feature per model.

B.2 Evaluating Flip B.2.1 Using Pre-determined Rules 

We provide the thresholds for a successful interven-tion on Mini-minus-4 dataset below. • Language identification : fastText’s (lid.176.bin 5) top-1 prediction is the intended language and confidence is ≥0.5. • Non-degeneracy : The output sentence con-tains 2–200 words and ≥30% of tokens are unique. • Content preservation : For Mini-minus-4, the output scores ≥30 chrF against the model’s un-intervened translation of the op-posite polarity. 

B.2.2 Using LLM-as-Judge 

On FLORES-minus-4, we replace the heuristic po-larity rules used for Mini-minus-4 with an LLM-as-judge protocol (GPT-5.6-Terra) using the prompt in Figure 8. 

Baseline eligibility. For each item, we first de-termine whether the un-intervened baseline trans-lation preserves the source polarity. Given the source sentence, the annotated main clause, and the baseline output, the judge classifies the polar-ity of the translated main clause as negated , affir-mative , missing , or unclear . A row is eligible for scoring only if the baseline polarity matches the source polarity; otherwise, the model did not pre-serve the source polarity before intervention, so the effect cannot be cleanly attributed to the interven-tion. 

> 5https://fasttext.cc/docs/en/language-identification

Intervention evaluation. For eligible items, the judge evaluates the baseline and intervened trans-lations as a blind pair, labeled A and B, without being told which output received the intervention. Given the source, annotated main clause, and both translations, the judge reports three fields: (1) the main-clause polarity of A, (2) the main-clause po-larity of B, and (3) whether the two translations pre-serve the same underlying event or situation when polarity differences are set aside. 

Flip rate. The flip rate is the proportion of eli-gible items for which the intervention successfully changes the main-clause polarity to the target po-larity while retaining meaning.

C.1 Mini-minus-4 

Mini-minus-4 6 is a synthetic minimal-pair dataset covering English (EN), Vietnamese (VI), French (FR), and Chinese (ZH). For each language, we generate single-clause sentences paired with their polarity-flipped counterparts, differing only by the presence of a negation marker. Each language is 

> 6“Mini” indicates this is a dataset of minimally contrastive sentence pairs and the sentences themselves are short and min-imalistic.

pinned to a single negation regime to avoid con-founds: • EN : copula or modal frame ( be + adjective, or can/will/should + verb), negated with not .This avoids do-support, ensuring the only dif-ference between pairs is the negator. • VI : preverbal không only, excluding aspect-or register-carrying alternatives ( chưa , chẳng ,

chả , đâu , đừng ). • FR : standard written ne…pas with elision, formal register. Excludes jamais , plus , rien ,

Generation. Pairs are generated natively in each language using GPT-5.4-mini (temperature 0.9), with prompts rotated across 20 everyday domains (food, travel, health, sports, etc.) for lexical diver-sity. Each prompt requires that the two sentences differ only by the negation marker, with identical subject, predicate, and tense. We overgenerate by a factor of 1.3 × to allow for filtering losses. 

Filtering. Generated pairs pass through three fil-ters: (1) per-language marker validation, which verifies that the negative sentence contains exactly the expected marker in the correct position and that no other negation markers are present; (2) dedupli-cation by sentence hash and predicate lemma; and (3) a predicate-disjoint split (seed 42), which parti-tions pairs into 150 for extraction, 100 for evalua-tion and 100 for test, guaranteeing that no predicate appears in more than one split. 

Final yield. 350 pairs per main language (150 ex-traction + 100 evaluation + 100 test). 

Additional-language test set. We additionally generate 100 held-out test pairs each in Russian (RU), Hindi (HI), Arabic (AR), and Indonesian (ID), using the same minimal-pair construction. C.2 FLORES-minus-4 

We derive FLORES-minus-4 7 from the FLORES-200 devtest set (NLLB Team, 2024) through a three-stage pipeline: candidate collection, LLM-assisted flip generation, and LLM verification. 

Stage 1: Candidate collection. We begin with the 1,012-sentence devtest split, restricted to the parallel intersection of our four languages (EN, VI, FR, ZH). Sentences are shuffled with a fixed seed to avoid the domain clustering present in the orig-inal row order. We retain sentences with 6–30 En-glish words and partition them by polarity. The 

positive pool excludes sentences containing any English negation marker, including morphological forms ( un-, non-, -less ). The negative pool re-tains only sentences with verbal analytic negation (not , n’t ), excluding lexical negators ( never , noth-ing ) and morphological negation. Both pools reject sentences with neg-raising verbs ( think , believe , seem ) and sentences whose parallel Vietnamese or Chinese translations con-tain non-standard negators (e.g., VI chưa /chẳng ,ZH 未能 /从未 ). This yields 795 candidate par-allel 4-tuples. GPT-5.6-Luna also annotates the main clause where polarity differs for each language, return-ing verbatim substrings used to scope the polarity judge and define intervention token spans. 

> 7The “minus” indicates that the dataset is a negated ver-sion (since the original dataset is called FLORES+), while 4 indicates the number of languages.

Stage 2: Flip generation and annotation. For each candidate, GPT-5.6-Luna generates a polarity-flipped counterpart in all four languages. The prompt enforces the following constraints: use only standard verbal negation (EN did not /does not ; VI không ; FR ne…pas ; ZH 不/没有 selected by aspect); preserve all content words; apply only grammar-forced co-changes (e.g., EN some ↔any ,FR partitive du →de ); and reject rather than pro-duce a strained flip. The model outputs structured JSON that records the target predicate, aspect, and negator choice before the flipped sentence, prevent-ing post-hoc rationalization. This produces 439 items with at least one successful flip. 

Stage 3: Verification. A verifier (GPT-5.6-Terra) judges each item. Per-language checks ver-ify that: (1) the flip reverses propositional polarity, (2) negation targets the main predicate rather than a subordinate clause, (3) modal scope is correctly realized, (4) no content beyond grammar-forced changes was altered, (5) the negator suits the as-pect and predicate type, and (6) the flip is natural. Item-level checks verify that (7) all four languages negate the same proposition and (8) the negative sentence does not introduce a scope ambiguity that would translate differently across languages. Items where the verifier is uncertain are flagged for hu-man review rather than silently dropped. 

Final dataset. The final FLORES-minus-4 set consists of items that pass all verification checks and have valid main-clause annotations in all four languages, yielding 216 parallel sentences .16 D Extensions of Main Results
