Abstract 

Retrieval-based in-context learning (ICL) en-ables large language models (LLMs) to pre-dict tabular labels from similar examples, but it remains unclear whether LLM inference im-proves on direct voting over those same ex-amples. We address this question by compar-ing four general-purpose LLMs with distance-weighted nearest-neighbor voting (Distance-KNN) using identical retrieved neighbors across 19 tabular classification datasets. With the number of demonstrations fixed, enlarg-ing the retrieval candidate pool improves ac-curacy for all predictors, yet Distance-KNN achieves the highest mean accuracy at every pool size. More detailed analyses reveal that LLM gains are concentrated where retrieved neighbors exhibit low label consensus and vary across models. These gains further depend on the average feature-space distance to the test query: under low label consensus, some LLMs outperform Distance-KNN in close and inter-mediate neighborhoods, but all fall behind on average in the most distant group. Complemen-tary experiments on the number of demonstra-tions, prompt formulation, retrieval representa-tion, and label-balanced demonstration selec-tion largely preserve the overall findings. These results characterize when LLM inference adds predictive value beyond distance-weighted vot-ing in retrieval-based tabular ICL. 

1 Introduction 

Tabular classification is a widely studied machine learning task with applications in healthcare (Yang et al., 2024), commerce (Sun and Li, 2024), and energy (Alawi et al., 2024). The goal is to predict class labels from instances described by numerical and categorical features. and tree-based ensembles, which have demon-strated strong performance on heterogeneous tabu-lar data (Chen and Guestrin, 2016; Grinsztajn et al., 2022). Recent research has explored alternative ap-proaches that leverage pretrained models for tabular prediction. Specialized tabular foundation models, including TabPFN, learn transferrable predictive patterns through pretraining (Hollmann et al., 2023, 2025; Ma et al., 2025). Another line of work adapts language models to tabular data through supervised or instruction tuning (Hegselmann et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). More recently, researchers have explored apply-ing frozen general-purpose large language models 1arXiv:2610.29520v1 [cs.IR] 21 Oct 2026 (LLMs) through few-shot in-context learning (ICL) (Brown et al., 2020; Wu and Hou, 2025; Yuan et al., 2025). In this setting, labeled tabular instances are serialized as demonstrations, often selected ac-cording to their similarity to a test query, enabling prediction without task-specific parameter updates. Despite these advances, it remains unclear how much predictive value LLM inference adds be-yond direct voting on the same retrieved exam-ples. In retrieval-based tabular ICL, performance differences may arise from either the selection of demonstrations or the predictions made from them, making these contributions difficult to distinguish when different retrieved examples are used. More-over, expanding the candidate pool changes the available retrieval evidence, but whether LLM in-ference benefits more from this change than direct voting remains unclear. Their relative performance may also depend on two characteristics of the re-trieved neighborhood: label consensus, the fraction of neighbors sharing the most frequent label, and feature proximity, measured by the mean feature-space distance from the test query to those neigh-bors, as illustrated in Figure 1. A controlled com-parison on identical retrieved neighbors is therefore needed to assess when LLMs can provide addi-tional predictive value. In this work, we systematically compare four general-purpose LLMs with distance-weighted nearest-neighbor voting (Distance-KNN) across 19 tabular classification datasets using a shared-neighbor evaluation protocol. At each candidate-pool size, both predictors use the same eight re-trieved examples: LLMs predict from their seri-alized features and labels, while Distance-KNN predicts from their labels and retrieval distances. By expanding the candidate pool while keeping the number of retrieved examples fixed, we exam-ine their performance and how LLM gains vary with label consensus and feature proximity. Our re-sults show that Distance-KNN achieves the highest mean accuracy across all evaluated candidate pools, while LLMs exhibit selective gains under low label consensus. Feature proximity further distinguishes these gains, with improvements occurring in se-lected close and intermediate neighborhoods but disappearing in the most distant ones. In summary, our contributions are as follows: 

1) We present a systematic shared-neighbor com-parison of four frozen LLMs and Distance-KNN across 19 datasets, enabling a direct assessment of the contribution of LLM inference over distance-weighted voting. 2) We find that Distance-KNN achieves the highest mean accuracy across all evalu-ated candidate pools, while LLMs exhibit selective gains in low-consensus neighborhoods that vary with feature proximity. 3) We further examine how the number of demonstrations, prompt formula-tion, retrieval representation, and label-balanced demonstration affect the comparison, finding that the advantage of Distance-KNN largely persists across these experiments. 

2 Related Work 

Tabular Prediction and Tabular Foundation Models Tabular prediction has traditionally re-lied on distance-based methods such as k-nearest neighbors (KNN) and tree-based ensembles, in-cluding Random Forest, XGBoost, and CatBoost (Cover and Hart, 1967; Breiman, 2001; Chen and Guestrin, 2016; Prokhorenkova et al., 2018). To circumvent task-specific training while achieving strong generalization across unseen datasets, recent work has introduced tabular foundation models. For instance, TabPFN is pretrained on synthetic tabular tasks to make predictions directly from labeled examples (Hollmann et al., 2023, 2025). Extending this paradigm to real-world data, Tab-DPT introduces a dedicated tabular architecture for in-context prediction, rather than adapting general-purpose LLMs (Ma et al., 2025). 

Adapting Language Models for Tabular Predic-tion A common paradigm relies on additional training to adapt language models for tabular pre-diction. Existing methods fine-tune LLMs on se-rialized rows for few-shot classification (Hegsel-mann et al., 2023), train on synthetic table tasks (Li et al., 2024), or leverage diverse tabular datasets for cross-dataset transfer (Wen et al., 2024; Gard-ner et al., 2024), while others explore retrieval-guided tuning (Wen et al., 2025) and structure-aware parameter adaptation (He et al., 2025). In contrast, our study investigates tabular prediction using frozen, general-purpose LLMs without addi-tional task-specific training, leveraging Distance-KNN to provide a controlled comparison on identi-cal retrieved demonstrations.

However, these studies primarily investigate how retrieval strate-gies or additional information can improve LLM predictions. 3 Experimental Setup 

For each test query, a shared retriever extracts the 

k nearest (most similar) labeled training instances based on feature-space distance. Distance-KNN predicts from their labels and metric distances, whereas the LLM receives their serialized features and labels followed by the query features. By scal-ing the candidate-pool size under a fixed demon-stration budget ( k = 8 ), we compare their pre-dictive behaviors on identical retrieved evidence (Figure 2). 

3.1 Models 

We evaluate four open-weighted LLMs: Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-32B chat models (Yang et al., 2025), alongside instruction-tuned Gemma3-12B and Gemma3-27B (Gemma Team et al., 2025). All generative LLMs are deployed locally via vLLM without quantization. Qwen3-Embedding-4B (Zhang et al., 2025a) is employed for text em-bedding retrieval in §5. Throughout our experi-ments, Distance-KNN serves as the reference pre-dictor, using the exact same retrieved neighbors as the LLMs. Full checkpoint identifiers and imple-mentation details are provided in Appendix A.1. 

3.2 Datasets 

We experiment on 19 public tabular classification datasets from UCI Machine Learning Repository (Kelly et al., 2023) and OpenML (Vanschoren et al., 2014). These datasets span binary and multiclass tasks across diverse domains, including healthcare, education, finance, industry, and software engi-neering. Each dataset is partitioned into training, validation, and test splits with the ratio of 8:1:1 via stratified sampling. All predictors share iden-tical splits within each random seed. The training split serves as the retrieval candidate pool. We re-port macro-averaged accuracy across all datasets to prevent large test sets from dominating aggre-gate evaluation. Dataset statistics are detailed in Appendix A.2.

LLM inference. The prompt concatenates the task description, feature names, label verbalizers, and the retrieved demonstrations. Crucially, the LLM receives the exact same retrieved neighbors as Distance-KNN, with retrieval distances omitted from the prompt. The prompt template is provided in Appendix A.4.

4.2 When Does LLM Inference Add Value Beyond Distance-KNN? 

To further examine when LLM inference adds value beyond Distance-KNN, we group test queries by label consensus among the shared retrieved ex-amples. Figure 4 reports the mean accuracy differ-ences of LLMs relative to Distance-KNN under the full-pool setting. 

Low label consensus creates selective oppor-tunities for LLM gains. At the lowest reported consensus level ( 4/8), three of the four LLMs sur-5pass Distance-KNN with positive mean accuracy gaps, led by Gemma3-27B showing the largest im-provement of 3.60 percentage points. Qwen3-8B, however, falls behind Distance-KNN, indicating that low consensus does not benefit all models equally. The override analysis in Appendix B.2 shows that beneficial corrections outweigh harmful overrides for the three improving models. These results suggest that some LLMs can leverage in-formation in mixed-label demonstrations beyond what is captured by distance-weighted voting. 

High label consensus favors Distance-KNN. 

Across consensus levels of 5/8–7/8, Distance-KNN consistently outperforms all evaluated LLMs. The most pronounced deficit occurs for Qwen3-8B, which trails Distance-KNN by 9.91 percentage points at 5/8 consensus, while Gemma3-27B re-mains the closest competitor throughout this range. As detailed in Appendix B.2, harmful overrides exceed beneficial corrections across all models in these groups. This suggests that LLM inference can disrupt useful local evidence when the retrieved ex-amples exhibit a high label consensus. At full label consensus ( 8/8), both types of overrides become rare, and the accuracy gaps approach zero. 

Takeaway. The benefits of LLM inference are concentrated in low label consensus neighborhoods and depend on the model, whereas higher label con-sensus generally favors distance-weighted voting.

5 Complementary Analyses 

We conduct four complementary analyses to exam-ine how the number of demonstrations, prompt formulation, retrieval representation, and label-balanced selection affect the comparison between LLM inference and distance-weighted voting. All experiments cover 19 datasets and three seeds us-ing Gemma3-12B and Qwen3-8B. The first two analyses retain feature-based nearest-neighbor re-trieval, while the latter two examine alternative re-trieval representations and label-balanced selection, respectively. 

Number of demonstrations. We vary the num-ber of demonstrations k ∈ {4, 8, 16 } to test whether the findings in §4.1 hold beyond the de-fault setting of k = 8 . Figure 6 compares accu-racy across all candidate-pool sizes, using the same retrieved examples for each LLM and Distance-KNN. Results show that mean accuracy increases with candidate-pool size for all predictors across three demonstration budgets, consistent with the re-sults in Figure 3. Distance-KNN also maintains the highest mean accuracy in 19 of the 21 pool– k set-tings, with Gemma3-12B slightly ahead at the two smallest pools when k = 16 . Increasing k benefits Gemma3-12B more consistently than Qwen3-8B, while Distance-KNN shows no further improve-ment beyond eight neighbors. Overall, the main findings in Section 4.1 remain largely consistent across demonstration budgets, with limited excep-tions at small candidate pools. Additional results are provided in Appendix C.1. 

Prompt formulation. To examine whether the default prompt formulation limits LLM perfor-mance, we compare three formulations: key–value serialization (KV), natural-language serialization (NL), and rule-augmented natural-language seri-alization (Rule-Augmented NL). Our main exper-iments use KV, which presents features as key– value pairs, whereas NL expresses them as natural-language statements. Rule-augmented NL further supplements NL with training-derived classifica-tion rules using a procedure adapted from Insight-Tab (Yuan et al., 2025). All three formulations use the same eight retrieved demonstrations and their order, with additional rules supplied only in Rule-Augmented NL. Figure 7 shows that prompt formulation af-fects the two LLMs differently, while Distance-KNN maintains the highest mean accuracy across all seven candidate-pool sizes. Gemma3-12B generally performs best with KV, with only mi-nor differences among formulations. In contrast, Qwen3-8B consistently benefits from NL and Rule-Augmented NL over KV, with the latter achiev-ing a notable accuracy gain of 3.53% at 64 can-didates. These observations indicate that prompt formulation can improve LLM predictions, but the performance gap identified in §4.1 is not solely attributable to the default prompt formulation. Prompt templates and rule construction are detailed in Appendix C.2.

6 Conclusion 

We investigate whether and when LLMs add pre-dictive value beyond distance-weighted voting on the same retrieved tabular examples. Across 19 datasets and expanding candidate pools, Distance-KNN achieves the highest mean accuracy at every pool size, although its margin over the strongest LLM is small. Some LLMs outperform voting under low label consensus, with gains varying by model and feature proximity. As label consensus increases, Distance-KNN generally retains the ad-vantage, and low-consensus gains do not extend to the most distant neighborhoods. Complemen-tary analyses indicate that changing the number of demonstrations, prompt formulation, retrieval representation, or label-balanced selection does not substantially alter these findings. Our work high-lights the importance of comparing LLMs against traditional classifiers on the same retrieved exam-ples and motivates further work on identifying the neighborhoods in which LLMs are most useful. 8Limitations 

Our study focuses on the predictive value of general-purpose LLMs given the same retrieved neighbors as Distance-KNN. Although this design controls demonstration selection, it does not eval-uate independently optimized end-to-end systems or task-specific model adaptation. Moreover, while our evaluation spans 19 diverse datasets, validat-ing whether these behavioral patterns generalize to other emerging tabular benchmarks or highly domain-specific datasets remains a promising di-rection for future work. 

Ethical Considerations 

Our study uses publicly available benchmark datasets and existing pretrained language models, involving no new data collection or human annota-tion. Our experiments aim to provide empirical in-sights into retrieval-based tabular prediction rather than establish the suitability of these methods for real-world deployment. Downstream applications in sensitive domains would require additional eval-uation of reliability and fairness.

C.1 Demonstration count 

Figure 6 compares k = 4 , 8, 16 on 19 datasets and three seeds. Full-pool Distance-KNN accuracy rises by 0.80 percentage points from k = 4 to 

8, then falls by 0.21 at k = 16 . Gemma3-12B leads at the two smallest pools with k = 16 , while Distance-KNN leads at full pools for every tested budget. 

C.2 Prompt formulation Conditions. KV uses feature key–value pairs; NL uses statements of the form “the {feature} is {value}”; Rule-Augmented NL augments NL with rules from labeled candidate-pool data. All con-ditions share the same eight retrieved examples, labels, and order, and use three seeds. 

Rule construction. For Rule-Augmented NL, we adapt the Summarize–Exemplify–Reflect rule-distillation procedure of InsightTab (Yuan et al., 2025). For each dataset, seed, and candidate pool, a class-balanced subset of up to 128 samples trains an auxiliary XGBoost classifier (100 trees, maxi-mum depth 6, learning rate 0.3, exact tree method). The first tree’s leaves define sample groups. Pre-dictive entropy guides the selection of eight low-entropy examples and a high-entropy subset con-taining approximately half of the selected samples, 14 with selection aiming to balance classes. The classification checkpoint (google/gemma-3-12b-it or Qwen/Qwen3-8B) also summarizes and merges group-wise rules. Using these rules and the low-entropy examples, it predicts the high-entropy examples; errors and their correct labels support one additional round of rule generation. XGBoost assists rule construction, while the LLM makes the final test predictions. 

Validation and reuse. Rules are JSON objects with one to three feature conditions joined by AND and a predicted class. Generated, merged, and addi-tional rules are evaluated on the entire current can-didate pool. Acceptance requires support ≥ 3, cov-erage ≥ 0.01 , precision ≥ 0.70 , and a 95% Wilson lower bound ≥ 0.40 ; this empirical lower bound replaces the rule confidence. Unparseable outputs produce no rules, invalid rules are discarded, and an empty retained set gives empty rule arrays in the prediction template. Construction, validation, and refinement use training features and labels only. Rules are cached per dataset, seed, candidate pool, and model con-figuration, then reused across test queries. The construction examples do not replace the eight re-trieved demonstrations. Rule-generation requests use temperature 0 and at most 512 output tokens; input sections for summarization, merging, and reflection are limited to 24,000 characters, with excess content omitted or truncated. Final classi-fication uses temperature 0 and first-token label log-probabilities. LLM parameters remain frozen; only the auxiliary XGBoost model is trained. 

Prediction template. The merged and additional rules below are validated JSON arrays. The labeled examples are the same eight rows used by Distance-KNN.
