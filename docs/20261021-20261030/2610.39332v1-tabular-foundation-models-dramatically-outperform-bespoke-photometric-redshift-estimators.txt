Tabular Foundation Models Dramatically Outperform
Bespoke Photometric Redshift Estimators
Biprateep Dey1,2∗& Joshua S. Speagle (沈佳士)1
1University of Toronto
2Vector Institute
Abstract
Estimating galaxy redshifts (a proxy for distance) from imaging data alone, also
known as photometric redshifts (photo-z’s), is essential for studies of extragalactic
astrophysics and cosmology. However, data-driven estimators rely on spectroscopic
training datasets, which are scarce and systematically biased toward bright and
easily observed galaxies. In this work, we show that tabular foundation models
(TFMs), whose synthetic pretraining amounts to a learned prior over regression
problems, can outperform bespoke estimators using only in-context learning. Eval-
uating on a widely used benchmark dataset, we find that TFMs predicting full
probability distributions in a single forward pass systematically outperform bespoke
estimators across both representative and biased training data regimes, matching
or exceeding baselines trained on over 40,000 galaxies with just 1,000 training
context examples. We also show that the TFM advantage is largest when little to
no calibration data are available, and that it persists even when the bespoke base-
lines are supplemented with up to 10,000 representative calibration spectra. This
shows that TFMs offer a sample-efficient framework for upcoming imaging-only
astronomical surveys, where observational selection bias can be mitigated simply
by including a small representative calibration sample in context†.

Introduction
Determining galaxy distances is fundamental to extragalactic astrophysics and cosmology. Because
spectroscopy is observationally expensive, multi-band imaging (photometry) vastly outpaces spectro-
scopic data volume, requiring astronomers to infer photometric redshifts (photo-z’s) from imaging
alone. Upcoming surveys like the Vera C. Rubin Observatory’s Legacy Survey of Space and Time
[LSST; Ivezi´c et al., 2019] will observe billions of galaxies and rely critically on photo-z’s. Existing
estimators broadly divide into physical template-fitting and data-driven machine learning models
trained on spectroscopic sets [see, e.g., Brescia et al., 2021, Newman and Gruen, 2022, for reviews].
To systematically evaluate them, the LSST Dark Energy Science Collaboration (DESC) benchmarked
twelve photo-z codes under controlled conditions in Data Challenge 1 [DC1; Schmidt et al., 2020].
Recently, tabular foundation models (TFMs) pretrained on synthetic datasets have emerged as
powerful tools for regression and classification, excelling even in small-sample regimes [Hollmann
et al., 2022, Qu et al., 2025]. These transformers [Vaswani et al., 2017] are pretrained on billions
of synthetic tables drawn from random structural causal models [SCMs; Pearl, 2009] and then
perform in-context learning, wherein labeled training examples and unlabeled queries are supplied
as input tokens, and targets are predicted in a single forward pass with no weight updates. Such
a network approximates the Bayesian posterior predictive distribution under its pretraining prior
[Müller et al., 2021]. Trained in context, these models can then match task-specific estimators on

Recent studies indicate that TFMs can outperform traditional regression methods on selected photo-z
datasets [Izbicki and Rodrigues, 2026, Valença et al., 2026]. However, they do not fully explore
sample efficiency, performance when the training context is not representative of the test set, or the
in-context use of a small calibration sample, the three properties an effective learned prior should
provide. In this work, we therefore evaluate TFMs, with and without realistic spectroscopic selection
bias, on the LSST DESC DC1 [Schmidt et al., 2020] benchmark, which simulates Rubin Observatory
photometry with a matched spectroscopic training catalog. We analyze performance scaling with
context size, demonstrate that TFMs systematically and substantially outperform bespoke estimators
with a fraction of the training data, and show that a small representative calibration sample supplied
in context almost entirely mitigates selection bias. We also release our prediction infrastructure as a
Python package alongside code to reproduce our results3.

Methods
In-context Learning for Conditional Density Estimation.
To infer photo-z’s, we estimate the
conditional probability density function (PDF), p(z | x), where x ∈RD denotes observed multi-
band photometry and z ∈[0, zmax] is the true redshift. Standard supervised estimators are fit to
a training catalog Dtrain = {(xi, zi)}Ntrain
i=1
and must be retrained and re-tuned for each survey.
In contrast, TFMs frame conditional density estimation as in-context learning. Conditioned on a
prompt of labeled training context Dtrain and an unlabeled query galaxy x∗, the predictive PDF
p(z | x∗, Dtrain) is obtained in a single forward pass with frozen weights and no gradient updates,
fine-tuning, or hyperparameter searches.
Tabular Foundation Models.
We evaluate three leading4 open-weight TFMs from the TabArena
benchmark [Erickson et al., 2025]: Kumo Tabular [Qu et al., 2026], LimiX-2 [Zhang et al., 2026,
Wang et al., 2026] and TabPFN-3.5 [Jäger et al., 2026]. TabPFN-3.5 and LimiX-2 treat regression as
classification over 5,000 buckets of the standardized target, so their native output is the probability in
each bucket, while Kumo predicts 999 quantiles. Taking the density as uniform within each bucket
and between consecutive quantiles, both forms give a piecewise-linear cumulative distribution, which
we evaluate at the edges of a common grid of 4,000 bins (∆z = 0.0005) to obtain the probability in
each bin without further interpolation. To reduce prediction variance, we ensemble 8 predictions with
different feature permutations and preprocessing. As LimiX-2 was optimized for contexts of up to
about 20,000 rows, we use bootstrap aggregating (also called bagging; Breiman 1996) to use the full
dataset. Each ensemble member sees a bootstrap-resampled training context of 15,000 objects, and
the predicted densities are averaged.
Baseline Estimators.
We benchmark against the three top-performing algorithms from the LSST
DESC DC1 study [Schmidt et al., 2020]: FlexZBoost [Izbicki and B. Lee, 2017, Dalmasso et al.,
2020], Color-Matched Nearest Neighbors [CMNN; Graham et al., 2017], and GPz [Almosallam
et al., 2016]. FlexZBoost estimates conditional densities by regressing orthonormal cosine basis
coefficients using gradient-boosted trees [XGBoost; Chen and Guestrin, 2016]. CMNN kernel-
smooths spectroscopic redshifts of photometric neighbors in color-magnitude space, and GPz models
heteroscedastic uncertainties using non-stationary sparse Gaussian processes. All baselines are
implemented and evaluated within the Redshift Assessment Infrastructure Layers [RAIL; The RAIL
Team et al., 2026] package; newer neural estimators in RAIL are left to future work. We trained the
three baseline methods and performed an extensive hyperparameter search with Optuna [Akiba et al.,
2019] to determine the optimal settings on the benchmark data (see Appendix ). Across all metrics,
our implementations outperform the results reported in Schmidt et al. [2020]. For a direct comparison
with the TFMs, we evaluate all the predicted distributions on the common grid.

Mitigating Selection Bias.
We follow recent observational strategies that advocate collecting small,
representative deep spectroscopic samples [e.g., Newman and Gruen, 2022, Dey et al., 2026], and test
concatenating a modest representative calibration sample directly into the in-context prompt. While
we tested importance reweighting of the biased training set [Lima et al., 2008, Izbicki et al., 2017]
and stratified learning [StratLearn; Autenrieth et al., 2023] as possible alternatives to direct in-context
concatenation, both yielded negligible gains (< 10% relative CDE loss change; cf. shift-robust
pretraining priors, Helli et al. 2024).

Results and Discussions
Table 1 compares all six estimators at full context size on representative (Ntrain = 43,486) and biased
(Ntrain = 35,011) DC1 data. On representative data, all three TFMs outperform bespoke estimators
in CDE loss, scatter, and outlier fraction. TabPFN-3.5 attains the best CDE loss (LCDE = −15.30),
followed by LimiX-2 (−15.15) and Kumo (−14.95), while LimiX-2 has the lowest scatter (σIQR =
0.0135). Compared to the strongest bespoke baselines (CMNN at LCDE = −11.23 on par with GPz
at −11.21), TabPFN-3.5 improves CDE loss by 4.07 units, reduces scatter by ∼20%, and lowers
the outlier fraction by 0.8 percentage points. Figure 1 shows the performance on the representative
training set as a function of training context size, and we see that all three TFMs with just 1,000
context galaxies (LCDE = −11.62 to −11.21) match or outperform all three bespoke estimators
trained on the full 43,486 catalog (LCDE = −11.23 to −10.87).
Under spectroscopic selection bias (Table 1, right), all three TFMs reach a lower CDE loss than
every bespoke estimator trained on the same biased catalog, with LimiX-2 at LCDE = −15.26 and
σIQR = 0.0143 vs. −12.07 and 0.0172 for CMNN, the strongest bespoke method in this regime.
The TFMs fall within 0.29 units of CDE loss of one another (LCDE = −15.26 to −14.97), whereas
the bespoke estimators spread over 5.41 units (LCDE = −12.07 to −6.66). All three TFMs also
keep a lower scatter than every bespoke estimator (σIQR = 0.0143 to 0.0149 vs. 0.0172 to 0.0202).
This is consistent with the TFMs not extrapolating catastrophically into regions of feature space
left uncovered by the biased training set. Scaling trends are similar to the representative case (see
Appendix Figure 4), except that with 1,000 biased galaxies the TFMs (LCDE = −11.74 to −11.45)
outperform GPz and FlexZBoost trained on the full biased catalog but not CMNN (−12.07), which
all three overtake by 5,000 galaxies (−13.93 to −13.68).

a non-representative training set, across context (Ntrain = 1,000 to 35,011) and calibration data
sizes (Ncal = 0 to 10,000). While representative calibration galaxies improve both, the size of the
gain depends on how much non-representative context the model already has. With 1,000 training
objects, 1,000 calibration galaxies lower LimiX-2’s CDE loss by 1.61 units (LCDE = −11.74 to
−13.35), whereas at the full 35,011 training context, the same addition gains only 0.08 units (−15.26

training set and 10,000 calibration galaxies. However, adding 1,000 calibration galaxies (−13.35)

In summary, TFMs deliver exceptionally accurate in-context photo-z’s without survey-specific retrain-
ing. With 1,000 context examples they match bespoke estimators trained on 43,486 representative
galaxies, and under spectroscopic selection bias they lead every bespoke estimator trained on the same
biased and calibration samples by at least 2.90 units in CDE loss. With only 1,000 biased galaxies,
however, they need a small representative calibration sample to overtake CMNN trained on the full
biased catalog. On DC1, the TFM point estimates at full context size meet the per-galaxy LSST
targets quoted by Schmidt et al. [2020]. While meeting the DESC requirements on tomographic n(z)
[The LSST Dark Energy Science Collaboration et al., 2018] requires a dedicated calibration analysis
on realistic data, their strong performance under realistic conditions leads us to advocate for TFMs as
drop-in photo-z estimators in future studies, with fine-tuning and calibration as future refinements
rather than prerequisites.

A.1
Spectroscopic Selection Function and Sample Properties
Figure 3: Impact of the spectroscopic selection function on the training sample distributions compared to
the representative test and calibration samples. Left: Redshift distribution showing the biased spectroscopic
training sample (blue), the representative test sample (orange), and the representative calibration sample (green).
Right: Distribution in the selection plane of i-band magnitude vs. (g −z) color, where solid contours enclose
11.8%, 39.3%, 67.5%, and 86.4% of each sample’s galaxies (the two-dimensional Gaussian equivalents of
0.5σ, 1σ, 1.5σ, and 2σ). We observe that the spectroscopic selection preferentially removes faint (i > 24) and
high-redshift (z > 0.8) galaxies, producing an unrepresentative training distribution, whereas the calibration
sample closely matches the representative test population across all magnitudes and colors.
In real spectroscopic surveys, finite exposure times and target prioritization preferentially yield
redshifts for bright galaxies with prominent emission or absorption features. This induces both
covariate shift in optical photometry and label shift in redshift. To model this observational reality
within the LSST DESC DC1 benchmark, we implement a spectroscopic selection algorithm based
on the Subaru Hyper Suprime-Cam (HSC) PDR2 spectroscopic sample, adapted from the Redshift
Assessment Infrastructure Layers [RAIL; The RAIL Team et al., 2026].
To construct the biased training sample, we apply a two-dimensional selection over i-band magnitude
and (g −z) color combined with a color-dependent redshift ceiling. We first map each galaxy onto a
100 × 100 grid spanning 13 ≤i ≤26 and −2 ≤g −z ≤6 and assign it the spectroscopic success
ratio r (defined as the ratio of spectroscopic to photometric galaxies in HSC PDR2 and available as a
downloadable dataset) of its corresponding cell, setting r = 0 outside the grid boundaries. We then
compute the 99th percentile of HSC spectroscopic redshifts observed in each cell and discard any
galaxy whose redshift exceeds this ceiling, removing all galaxies from cells lacking spectroscopic
coverage. This step is critical because it makes the selection explicitly dependent on redshift rather
than photometry alone, introducing a truncation that cannot be inverted from photometric features.
Finally, within each distinct ratio cell, galaxies are retained at random with probability proportional
to r.
Because this selection retains only 8.66% of representative galaxies, the full 434,476-galaxy DC1
parent catalog yields at most 37,626 biased candidates. To ensure sufficient holdout data for eval-
uation, we carve out a biased training pool of 35,011 galaxies. From the remaining holdout, we
construct an independent representative test set of 19,383 galaxies and a representative calibration
sample of 10,000 galaxies.

cuts off sharply above z ∼0.8, with fewer than 1% of galaxies retained at z > 1.2. In contrast,
the representative test and calibration samples extend continuously out to z = 2.0. The right panel
shows the sample distributions in the (i, g −z) selection plane, where solid contours enclose 11.8%,

Performance under Selection Bias
Figure 4 shows the performance scaling of all six estimators when trained on the spectroscopically
biased dataset (Ntrain = 1,000 to 35,011) and evaluated on the representative test catalog. As shown
in the left panel, the bespoke baselines spread over a wide range of CDE loss under spectroscopic
selection, whereas the TFMs remain close together. In particular, FlexZBoost exhibits the flattest
scaling curve with LCDE between −4.3 and −6.7, suggesting that gradient-boosted trees struggle to
extrapolate into truncated regions of feature space. CMNN and GPz scale more steadily, reaching
LCDE = −12.07 and −10.62 at full context, respectively. In contrast, all three TFMs scale smoothly
toward CDE losses below −14.9, with LimiX-2 achieving −15.26 at 35,011 galaxies. Remarkably,
TFMs trained on just 1,000 biased context galaxies attain CDE losses between −11.5 and −11.7,
outperforming GPz and FlexZBoost trained on the full 35,011-galaxy catalog; only CMNN (−12.07)
stays ahead, and all three TFMs overtake it by 5,000 galaxies (−13.7 to −13.9).

TFMs consistently produce lower scatter across the entire context range. At small training sizes
(Ntrain = 1,000), bespoke estimators suffer substantial dispersion (σIQR ≈0.027 for FlexZBoost
and CMNN), whereas the TFMs maintain σIQR ≈0.018 to 0.019. At full context, LimiX-2 achieves

FlexZBoost (0.0182), and GPz (0.0202). This suggests that the pretraining phase equips TFMs
with inductive priors that prevent catastrophic degradation when spectroscopic training samples are
truncated.

A.3
Hyperparameter Search and Final Settings
We tuned every bespoke method separately for each regime and training set size with Optuna [Akiba
et al., 2019], using its multivariate tree-structured Parzen estimator sampler. Each trial fits a method
on four folds of the training set and predicts the fifth, repeating this across all five folds, and a trial
that completes all five is scored by the CDE loss on the common grid averaged over the held-out folds.
A median pruner stops unpromising trials early, and the test set is never used during tuning. Settings
that only post-process a fitted model’s predictions are evaluated on a grid within every trial, including
the number of trees, the number of series terms kept, and the sharpening exponent for FlexZBoost,
the kernel width for CMNN, and the scale and floor of the predicted widths for GPz.
On representative data, FlexZBoost uses a Fourier basis of 60 terms whose coefficients are regressed
together by trees with vector leaves (191 trees, a learning rate of 0.0068, and a maximum depth of
13) and a sharpening exponent of 1.225. On biased data, it keeps the Fourier basis with 55 terms
and uses 256 trees, a learning rate of 0.019, a maximum depth of 16, and a sharpening exponent of
