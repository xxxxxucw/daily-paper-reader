# Recertifying decisions via e-closure: uniform improvements of conformal risk control and randomized e-BH 

# Tian Bai and Aaditya Ramdas Stanford University 

# {tianbai,aramdas }@stanford.edu 

Abstract 

Statistical procedures often control expected loss at a prescribed level while leaving part of their risk budget unused. Building on the e-value representation of risk-controlled decisions and the e-closure principle for multiple testing, we formulate recertification , a general paradigm for improving procedures by exploiting computable slack in their validity certificates. In particular, a valid procedure’s losses normalized by their risk budgets form a state-indexed e-value collection; conversely, decisions whose normalized losses are bounded by this collection inherit its risk guarantees. Recertification uses distributional structure to quantify slack, enlarge the certifying e-values while preserving validity, and construct less conservative decisions. We develop two applications. For conformal risk control (CRC) with a known loss family, recertifica-tion redistributes unused risk budget among the held-out points of each hypothetical augmented sample. The resulting controller is valid under every finite exchangeable law and is no more conservative than CRC on any calibration sample. For convex losses on an interval of actions, repeated recertification converges geometrically to an admissible controller. For false discovery rate control with arbitrarily dependent e-values, recertification exploits unused conditional budgets in randomized e-BH. It yields a closed-form randomized procedure whose rejection set contains that of randomized e-BH for every realization of the shared randomization and whose expected number of rejections is maximal among randomized procedures satisfying the same conditional e-value budget constraints. Recertifying instead with the larger e-collection of closed e-BH gives a second closed-form procedure, whose rejection set contains that of the first. Experiments on CIFAR-100 hierarchical classification and multiple testing simulations demonstrate improved budget utilization and gains in informativeness and power over the corresponding baselines. 

Keywords: e-values, conformal risk control, false discovery rate, multiple testing, exchangeability, admissibility. 

# 1 Introduction 

Many statistical procedures turn data into an informed action, while guaranteeing that some expected loss stays below a prescribed level. Statistical decision theory formalizes this task through a decision rule mapping data to actions, a loss depending on the action and an unknown state of nature, and the resulting statewise expected loss, or risk [Wald, 1950, Berger, 1985]. The bound imposes a risk budget that must hold in every state. Among rules satisfying this constraint, one seeks the most useful decisions, as in the constrained problems of Hodges and Lehmann [1952]. Multiple testing is a decision problem of exactly this kind. With K hypotheses, the action is a rejection set R ⊆ [K], so the action space is the collection 2 [K] of all subsets of [ K], and the unknown state is the set T ⊆ [K] of true null hypotheses. The false discovery proportion |T ∩ R|/(|R| ∨ 1) is the loss, and false discovery rate (FDR) control bounds its expectation by α. The procedure of Benjamini and Hochberg [1995] achieves this by comparing the ordered p-values with linearly increasing thresholds; its e-value analogue, 

1arXiv:2610.35821v1 [stat.ME] 26 Oct 2026 the e-BH procedure of Wang and Ramdas [2022], applies the reciprocal thresholds to e-values, which are nonnegative statistics with expectation at most one under their null hypotheses [Vovk and Wang, 2021, Ramdas and Wang, 2025]. The recent work of Xu et al. [2026b] introduces the e-closure principle, a general principle that uniformly improves many testing procedures based on e-values, including e-BH. Specifically, given an e-value ET for each candidate true-null set T , valid when T is the set of true nulls, the principle certifies every rejection set R that satisfies 

|T ∩ R||R| ∨ 1 ≤ αE T for every T ⊆ [K]. (1.1) Under the actual null set, the left side is the false discovery proportion and the right side has expectation at most α. The false discovery rate is therefore controlled even when the analyst chooses among the certified rejection sets after seeing the data. Conversely, Xu et al. [2026b] show that a procedure controls the false discovery rate simultaneously over the rejection sets it may report if and only if all of them are certified in this way by some family of e-values. The principle applies equally to other error rates that are expected losses, and it contains the classical closed testing principle for the familywise error rate [Marcus et al., 1976] as the case of e-values taking only the values 0 and 1 /α .Nothing in this argument depends on the actions being rejection sets. In a general decision problem, a state s specifies a class of distributions for the data X, an action a is chosen from an arbitrary set, and each of several losses Lt(s, a ; X), indexed by t, carries its own budget αt(s). A family of e-values, one for each state and risk, certifies the actions whose losses fit within all the budgets it allows. Every data-dependent choice among the certified actions then controls every risk, and every procedure with this guarantee is certified by some such family [Gr¨ unwald, 2023, 2024, Andrews and Chen, 2025]. In Section 2 we review the e-closure principle at this level of generality, with menus of arbitrary actions, possibly continuously indexed states and risks, and losses that may depend on the data. The correspondence can be read in both directions, and the direction from procedures to e-values suggests how to improve a procedure. Improving a decision rule directly is difficult: a more liberal rule may have higher power or utility, but it is rarely clear how far a given rule can be pushed before its guarantee fails. By contrast, improving an e-value is easier. If an e-value has expectation strictly below one, and the shortfall can be computed, for instance conditionally on a statistic whose conditional law is known under the null, then adding the shortfall back yields a larger e-value. Through the e-closure correspondence, larger e-values translate back into decisions. Starting from a valid procedure, we apply this construction to its canonical e-values: its losses divided by their risk budgets. Inverting the enlarged certificate yields a menu containing the original actions and potentially more liberal ones, all with the same guarantee. We call this step 

recertification . It generalizes the anchoring idea of Ramdas [2026], which keeps an existing false discovery rate procedure and spends the slack in its validity proof, and it can be repeated. We apply recertification to two problems. The first is conformal risk control (CRC) [Angelopoulos et al., 2024], which extends conformal prediction from coverage to general monotone losses. Given n exchangeable calibration points and a known family of losses indexed by a conservativeness parameter λ, CRC selects the least conservative λ for which the calibration losses, together with a bound on the loss of the unseen test point, fit within the budget ( n + 1) α. The selected controller typically leaves part of this budget unused. Recertification computes this unused budget on every such sample, redistributes it among the n + 1 held-out positions, and inverts the resulting e-values. The new controller is valid under every finite exchangeable law, and for every calibration sample it is no more conservative than CRC. In this sense, for a fixed loss family and observation domain, it improves CRC uniformly. This recertification step can be repeated, with diminishing returns. For convex losses, the iterates converge geometrically to a controller that admits no further pointwise improvement, and on finite outcome spaces a coordinatewise sweep reaches such a controller without convexity (Section 3). Figure 1 illustrates these gains in hierarchical classification on CIFAR-100 (Section 3.6), using rules that give more specific predictions when the classifier is more confident. At a target of 15%, CRC attains a mean test risk of 14 .21%. Repeated recertification raises it to 14 .95%, close to the target, and reduces the mean prediction-set size from 10 .77 to 8 .92 labels, a reduction of 17 .2%.

The e-BH procedure is valid under arbitrary depen-dence but can be conservative, and Xu and Ramdas [2023] showed that external randomization recovers part of the lost power. Their U-eBH procedure boosts the e-values by dividing them by a single uniform random variable and then applies e-BH. In this setting, we treat the conditional law of the rejection set given the e-values as a randomized action. In these terms, U-eBH is certified by the family of e-values behind e-BH, through a version of the self-consistency condition of Blanchard and Roquain [2008] that holds on average over the randomization, and it typically leaves part of the budget allowed by this family unused. Recertification spends this budget and yields a closed-form randomized rule, recertified U-eBH (RU-eBH), which rejects at least as many hypotheses as U-eBH for every realization of the randomization and has the largest expected number of rejections among all randomized procedures certified by the same family. The same principle applies to closed e-BH, the improvement of e-BH by e-closure [Xu et al., 2026b], whose family of e-values is larger because it adapts to the number of true nulls. Recertifying with this family gives a second closed-form rule, recertified closed e-BH (RC-eBH), whose rejection set contains that of RU-eBH and which has the largest expected number of rejections among all randomized procedures certified by this family, closed e-BH included (Section 4). In simulations, both rules are more powerful than e-BH and U-eBH in every setting, and RC-eBH is the most powerful of the eight procedures we compare in nearly every setting. 

# 1.1 Related work 

E-values and decisions. E-values have become a standard tool for testing and multiple testing [Vovk and Wang, 2021, Ramdas and Wang, 2025]. Their link to decision making is developed by Gr¨ unwald [2024], who allows the significance level and the loss to be chosen after seeing the data, and by Koning [2023] and Chugg et al. [2026], who study post hoc testing and its admissible procedures. For a single risk, our certified menu is the set of actions whose e-posterior risk bound [Gr¨ unwald, 2023], with each state’s loss divided by its budget, is at most one. Andrews and Chen [2025] study decisions reported together with certificates of their loss, and Koning and van Meer [2026] give evidence-weighted loss bounds for decisions based on conformal e-prediction sets. Decision-theoretic views of conformal prediction also study how prediction sets should inform downstream actions [Lekeufack et al., 2024, Kiyani et al., 2025]. These works allow general states and actions; our focus is the constructive use of the correspondence, which keeps the error budget fixed and recertifies an existing procedure. 

3Closure principles. Closed testing [Marcus et al., 1976] controls the familywise error rate, and every coherent procedure for that error rate is a closed testing procedure [Sonnemann, 2008]. Closed testing also yields simultaneous bounds on the false discovery proportion of every rejection set [Goeman and Solari, 2011], and only closed testing procedures are admissible for that purpose [Goeman et al., 2021]. E-closure [Xu et al., 2026b] extends the closure principle to error rates defined by expected losses. Its follow-ups include the uniform improvement of the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure [Goeman, 2026], anchoring [Ramdas, 2026], online versions [Xu et al., 2026a], and complete-class and admissibility results for e-value procedures [Sun and Wang, 2026]. We note that representation and admissibility are separate questions. Every valid procedure is certified by some family of e-values, while admissibility requires further conditions, and our admissibility results for CRC rely on the structure of that problem. 

Randomized multiple testing. Randomization is classical in testing, where it makes tests based on discrete statistics exact [Lehmann and Romano, 2022]. For false discovery rate control with e-values under arbitrary dependence, Xu and Ramdas [2023] showed that stochastic rounding of the e-values and U-eBH increase the power of e-BH, and Xu et al. [2026b] combine stochastic rounding with e-closure. These methods randomize the e-values. Our procedures instead choose the distribution of the rejection set directly, subject either to per-hypothesis budgets that form a randomized version of the self-consistency condition of Blanchard and Roquain [2008] or to the constraints that certify closed e-BH. Other uniform improvements of e-BH include its minimally adaptive version [Ignatiadis et al., 2024], closed e-BH [Xu et al., 2026b], and conditional calibration [Fithian and Lei, 2022, Lee and Ren, 2024], which recalibrates the e-values given a sufficient statistic. 

Conformal risk control. CRC already handles several, even infinitely many, risks that share a monotone action parameter [Angelopoulos et al., 2024, Section 4.3]. Risk-Controlling Prediction Sets (RCPS) and Learn then Test (LTT) instead provide high-probability statements about population risks [Bates et al., 2021, Angelopoulos et al., 2025]. Extensions of CRC treat non-exchangeable data [Farinhas et al., 2024], cross-validation [Cohen et al., 2024], non-monotonic losses [Angelopoulos, 2026], and end-to-end training [Yeh et al., 2025]. The leave-one-out characterization of validity that we use (Lemma 3.1) is the risk-control counterpart of the universality of conformal prediction [Vovk et al., 2022, Proposition 2.9]; see also Tibshirani et al. [2026], and Angelopoulos [2026] for its sufficiency direction. Conformal prediction with e-values is developed by Vovk [2025], Koning [2026], Gauthier et al. [2025], and Koning and van Meer [2026]. Apart from the sharper bound on the unseen test loss in envelope CRC [Yeh et al., 2025], which serves as our starting point, we are not aware of work on improving CRC controllers pointwise or on their admissibility. We use CRC to show concretely how large such improvements can be and what they cost in computation.

# 2 Generalized e-closure and recertification 

This section sets up the decision framework used throughout the paper. We first describe states, actions and losses, then establish the correspondence between e-values and valid decisions, and finally introduce recertification, which turns this correspondence into a way of improving existing procedures. 

42.1 States, actions, and a family of risks 

Suppose that a statistician observes data X taking values in a space X . The distribution of X is governed by an unknown state s ∈ S , and each state specifies a nonempty class Ps of distributions of X. After observing 

X, the statistician takes an action a ∈ A , whose quality is measured by a family of known nonnegative losses 

Lt(s, a ; x), t ∈ T .

The index t ranges over the kinds of error that the statistician wishes to control, such as several error rates or a continuum of loss functions, and each loss may depend on the observation x as well as on the state and the action. The sets S, A and T are arbitrary and may be uncountable. Each loss comes with a budget. Given fixed positive levels αt(s), a decision rule A : X → A is valid if 

EP [Lt(s, A (X); X)] ≤ αt(s) for every s ∈ S , P ∈ P s, t ∈ T . (2.1) In words, whatever the state and whichever distribution within it generates the data, the expected loss of the selected action stays within its budget, separately for every loss in the family. The expectation is unconditional: it averages over the data and hence over the action that the rule selects. Throughout, we assume that all decision rules and all losses that we evaluate are measurable, and when we discuss sets of actions we also assume that their statewise loss envelopes, defined below, are measurable. Appendix A gives sufficient conditions and discusses the uncountable intersections that arise. Validity is certified by e-values. Recall that an e-value for a class of distributions is a nonnegative statistic whose expectation is at most one under every distribution in the class. Since (2.1) involves every state and every loss, we use one e-value for each such pair. An e-collection is a family of nonnegative measurable functions Es,t satisfying 

EP Es,t (X) ≤ 1 (s ∈ S , P ∈ P s, t ∈ T ). (2.2) In other words, an e-collection is a set of e-values, one for each combination of state and loss. Because an e-collection will be used to certify actions, we also refer to it as a certificate. 

# 2.2 From e-collections to decisions and back 

We now make precise the correspondence between e-collections and valid decisions described in the intro-duction. It is convenient to work with sets of actions rather than with single rules. A menu assigns to each observation x a nonempty set M(x) ⊆ A of actions that the statistician is prepared to take. After seeing the data, the statistician may choose any action from M(X), for instance by maximizing a utility or following a scientific preference. A decision rule is the special case of a menu with a single action at each observation. We first show how an e-collection produces a menu, then how a menu produces an e-collection, and finally that the two constructions are compatible. 

From e-collections to menus. An e-collection certifies an action at the observation x when every loss of that action fits within its budget, scaled by the corresponding e-value. The certified menu of E is therefore

Multiple testing as a special case. The e-closure principle for multiple testing is recovered as follows. Take both the state space and the action space to be 2 [K], let PT contain the distributions under which T is the set of true null hypotheses, and use the loss L(T, R ) = |T ∩ R|/(|R| ∨ 1). Section 4 reviews this setting in more detail. The general framework requires no structure on the actions, such as unions or an ordering, and no monotonicity of the losses. For instance, the false discovery proportion can decrease when a rejection is added.

# 2.3 Recertification: spending the unused budget 

A valid menu can be enlarged in two ways, and the distinction between them is central to our approach. The first is closing , which adds every action whose losses lie beneath the current envelopes of the menu. By Theorem 2.1, closing preserves every envelope: the closed menu offers more choice, which in multiple testing is the simultaneity gain described by Xu et al. [2026b], but it uses exactly the budget that the original menu uses. Any budget that the original procedure leaves unused therefore remains unused after closing. The second way, which we call recertifying , first replaces the envelopes by larger ones that still respect the budgets and then closes with respect to the new envelopes. The recertified menu can therefore contain actions whose losses exceed those of every action in the original menu, and this is how recertification spends the unused budget. To recertify, we need to quantify the budget that the baseline leaves unused. We do so conditionally on a statistic, chosen for each state–loss pair so that the required conditional expectations are known. When the unused budget can be computed without conditioning, the statistic is simply taken to be constant. Fix a valid baseline menu with envelopes gs,t , and let Vs,t denote the conditioning statistic for the pair ( s, t ). Suppose that known nonnegative functions Hs,t and Bs,t satisfy, for every P ∈ P s,

EP [gs,t (X) | Vs,t ] ≤ Hs,t (Vs,t ) ≤ Bs,t (Vs,t ), EP Bs,t (Vs,t ) ≤ αt(s). (2.8) The function Hs,t bounds the conditional expected loss of the baseline, while Bs,t is a conditional budget whose expectation does not exceed the original level. Their difference Bs,t − Hs,t is therefore an allowance that the baseline leaves unused, conditionally on Vs,t . The following proposition shows that any nonnegative increment within this allowance can be added to the envelopes.

EP [Ws,t (X) | Vs,t ] ≤ Bs,t (Vs,t ) − Hs,t (Vs,t ) (P ∈ P s), (2.9) 

then (gs,t + Ws,t )/α t(s) is an e-collection. Its certified menu contains the original menu and every action whose losses are at most gs,t + Ws,t for all s and t.Proof. Conditionally on Vs,t , the expectation of gs,t + Ws,t is at most Hs,t + ( Bs,t − Hs,t ) = Bs,t . Taking expectations once more, the unconditional expectation is at most αt(s). The two inclusions follow from 

Ws,t ≥ 0 and from the definition (2.3) of the certified menu. The increments Ws,t may depend on the data in an arbitrary way; only their conditional expectations are constrained by (2.9). How the allowance is computed depends on the problem. In CRC (Section 3), the state is a hypothetical augmented sample whose law is known by exchangeability, so no further conditioning is needed, and the allowance is shared equally among the held-out points. For randomized e-BH (Section 4), the input e-values supply the budget directly, and conditional on the e-values, the averaged losses are known because the law of the external randomization is known. Proposition 2.2 also describes an existing method, the conditional calibration of e-values [Fithian and Lei, 2022, Lee and Ren, 2024], which improves e-BH by recalibrating each e-value given a sufficient statistic; there the sufficient statistic provides the conditional expectations (Remark 4.3). Finally, the recertified e-collection is again an e-collection, so its certified menu is valid by Theorem 2.1 and can serve as the baseline for another round of recertification. 

73 Improving conformal risk control 

We now apply the framework of Section 2 to conformal risk control. Section 3.1 sets up the problem and two baseline procedures, and Section 3.2 characterizes validity exactly through the losses on hypothetical augmented samples. This characterization exposes the budget that a controller leaves unused, which recer-tification spends in Section 3.3. Repeating the step leads to admissible controllers, which we illustrate on a small example (Section 3.4). Section 3.5 extends the construction to multiple risks, nonconvex losses and test features, and discusses computation, and Section 3.6 reports a real-data study on hierarchical image classification. 

# 3.1 Setup and two valid starting rules 

Conformal risk control [Angelopoulos et al., 2024] calibrates a family of actions so that a prescribed expected loss is controlled at a new test point. The actions are indexed by a parameter λ in a finite ordered grid or a compact interval Λ = [ λ−, λ +], where larger values are more conservative. For example, λ may be a threshold that determines a prediction set, with larger λ giving larger sets. The quality of action λ on an observation z ∈ Z is measured by a known loss ℓ(z, λ ), such as the indicator that the prediction set misses the label, or the fraction of true labels that it misses. Following the original work, we assume that 0 ≤ ℓ(z, λ ) ≤ B, ℓ(z, λ ) is non-increasing and right-continuous in λ, ℓ(z, λ +) ≤ α, (3.1) where 0 < α < B is the target level. Thus the losses are bounded, a more conservative action never incurs a larger loss, and the most conservative action always meets the target. We observe calibration data D = ( Z1, . . . , Z n) and must choose an action for a test observation Zn+1 ,whose loss we cannot see. We assume only that ( Z1, . . . , Z n+1 ) is exchangeable; if the loss involves a trained prediction model, the model is held fixed, and exchangeability is assumed conditionally on its training data. The goal is a data-dependent action A(D) ∈ Λ satisfying 

E ℓ(Zn+1 , A (D)) ≤ α

under every finite exchangeable law on Zn+1 , and, subject to this constraint, as liberal as possible. We call such a rule A a controller. The observation space and the loss family are fixed throughout, and controllers are always compared for the same observation space and loss family. # 3.4 Iteration to admissibility under convexity 

Since recertification preserves validity, it can be repeated, and each round can only improve the selected action. For convex losses, the iterates converge to an admissible controller that cannot be improved further, in the following pointwise sense. 

Definition 3.4 (Pointwise admissibility for CRC) . A valid symmetric deterministic controller A is pointwise admissible if no other measurable symmetric deterministic controller C is valid under every finite exchange-able law and satisfies C(D) ≤ A(D) for every D, strictly at some D. The observation domain, loss family, sample size and action space remain fixed. 

Admissibility is thus a pointwise notion: controllers are compared on every calibration sample. This is meaningful because Lemma 3.1 imposes a constraint on every augmented multiset, with no exceptional null sets, so changing a controller at even a single calibration multiset can affect its validity. The notion concerns only how conservative the selected actions are. An admissible controller need not be optimal for a particular utility, and several admissible controllers may coexist, as the example at the end of this section shows. 

Theorem 3.5 (An admissible limit) . Suppose Λ = [ λ−, λ +] and every ℓ(z, ·) in (3.1) is convex. Suppose the exact inversions in (3.7) are measurably implemented. From any valid baseline, the iterates decrease to a valid pointwise-admissible controller A(∞). With ρ = n/ (n + 1) ,

0 ≤ A(k)(D) − A(∞)(D) ≤ ρk(A(0) (D) − A(∞)(D)) ≤ ρk(λ+ − λ−). (3.9) 

A valid controller is admissible if and only if it is an exact fixed point of R in this setting. 

The proof rests on a one-step comparison. Suppose that a valid controller C satisfies C ≤ A. On every augmented sample, each held-out loss of C exceeds that of A by a nonnegative amount, and by Lemma 3.1 these increases sum to at most ∆ A(S), so each is at most ∆ A(S). By convexity, the action 

ρA (D) + C(D)/(n + 1) increases each loss by at most a fraction 1 /(n + 1) of this amount, so it is feasible in (3.7), and therefore 

RA ≤ ρA + C/ (n + 1) . (3.10) The iterates decrease to a limit, which is valid by the right continuity of the losses. Applying (3.10) along the iterates and passing to the limit shows that no valid controller strictly dominates the limit, and taking

13 Computation. The risk-control guarantees hold after any number of rounds, so the number of rounds is a computational choice, whose cost depends on how the controller depends on the data. A single round is usually inexpensive. With K candidate outcomes, ( RA)( D) requires A(D) and the totals TA(D ∪ { z}), that is, the controller evaluated on the nK replacement samples D−j ∪ { z}, each a recalibration with the prediction model held fixed. For CRC and envelope CRC, these recalibrations share most of their work. A replacement changes a single term of the calibration loss Pni=1 ℓ(Zi, λ ), so the sum is computed once and adjusted for each replacement, and each replacement action is the smallest λ satisfying a constraint that is monotone in λ. Such an action is found by a binary search for general losses and directly for structured ones, such as piecewise-linear or step losses. Repeated rounds are more demanding, because the replacements compound. Round k evaluates A(k−1) 

on replacement samples, each of which requires A(k−2) on samples with a second point replaced, and so on. Hence A(k)(D) depends on the baseline at every calibration multiset obtained from D by replacing up to k

of its points with candidate outcomes. The cost of many rounds is governed by how many distinct multisets arise and whether they can be shared across calibration samples and test points, and both depend on how the baseline depends on the data. 

• Finitely many types. Suppose that the loss depends on an observation only through one of K types and that the baseline depends on the calibration data only through the type counts. Every iterate then inherits this property, so all the multisets above belong to a single table indexed by count vectors. The table has  n+K−1

> K−1

 entries, polynomially many in n for fixed K, and each round is one pass over it. It does not depend on the observed data, so it is computed once and reused for every calibration sample and test point. 

• Individual scores or features. When the baseline depends on individual observations, for instance through continuous scores or test features, no such table exists. The replacement samples must then be tracked separately for each calibration sample and test point, and their number grows exponentially in the number of calibration points whose loss is not identically zero. Exact reductions, such as grouping points with identical scores, can still make many rounds feasible (Appendix B.7). 

• Uncountable outcomes or risks. Here even a single round requires care. The constraints in (3.12) must hold for a continuum of z or t, and checking them requires a certified optimization or a uniform approximation bound, since finitely many sampled outcomes do not certify feasibility. Two observations reduce the cost in every case. First, the iterates decrease and the losses are non-increasing, so stopping at a data-dependent round no later than a fixed maximum incurs at most the loss of the maximal round and preserves validity. Computation can therefore stop once a computable bound shows that further rounds cannot change the action appreciably. Second, replacing the allowances ∆ A,t by conservative lower bounds preserves validity and the comparison with the baseline, although the limit may then fall short of admissibility. Exact recertification thus trades computation for a uniform improvement.

# 4 Improving randomized e-BH 

We now return to multiple testing, the setting of the e-closure principle that inspired this work [Xu et al., 2026b], and recertify a randomized version of the e-BH procedure. Let E = ( E1, . . . , E K ) be e-values for hypotheses H1, . . . , H K , with arbitrary dependence among them. In the framework of Section 2, the state is the unknown set T ⊆ { 1, . . . , K } of true nulls, whose class PT consists of all laws under which EP Ej ≤ 1 for every j ∈ T , and errors are measured by the false discovery proportion FDP T (R) = |R ∩ T |/(|R| ∨ 1) of a rejection set R, with budget α. Section 4.1 recalls how e-BH fits this framework. Section 4.2 casts randomized e-BH as an instance of generalized e-closure, with randomized decisions as actions, and identifies the budget it leaves unused. Section 4.3 recertifies it with the e-collection of e-BH, Section 4.4 with the larger e-collection of closed e-BH, and Section 4.5 reports simulations.

Remark 4.3 (Conditional calibration) . Conditional calibration [Fithian and Lei, 2022, Lee and Ren, 2024] is another way of improving e-BH, and it follows the same pattern with a different source of budget. We only recast the e-BH version of Lee and Ren [2024] in the present terms without improving or modifying it. Its baseline is the part of each e-value that e-BH actually uses. With R0 the e-BH rejection set, the pruned e-value hj = K 1{j ∈ R0}/{α(|R0| ∨ 1) } ≤ Ej leads e-BH to the same rejections. Suppose that under Hj a statistic Sj makes the conditional law of the data given Sj known. The budget left unused by the pruned e-value, E[Ej − hj | Sj ], is then known, and Lee and Ren [2024] spend it by raising hj to a larger e-value whose conditional expectation given Sj does not exceed that of Ej . This is Proposition 2.2 in e-value 

21 units, with conditioning statistic Sj and budget E[Ej | Sj ], and e-BH applied to the new e-values rejects at least R0. Conditional calibration thus spends unused budget across the values of the data that share the same Sj , whereas RU-eBH spends it across the values of the external seed. Appendix C.6 gives the exact correspondence.

# 4.5 Simulations 

We compare RU-eBH and RC-eBH with six procedures that, like them, control the FDR under arbitrary dependence. Four are deterministic: e-BH, its minimally adaptive version (MA-eBH) [Ignatiadis et al., 2024], closed e-BH [Xu et al., 2026b] and anchored e-BH [Ramdas, 2026]. Two are randomized: U-eBH [Xu and Ramdas, 2023] and J-eBH (Section 4.2). There are K = 100 hypotheses, of which 10, 50 or 90 are false. For each hypothesis we observe a Gaussian statistic Zj with unit variance, mean 0 under the null and a common signal mean between 1 and 4 otherwise; the statistics are equicorrelated with correlation ρ ∈ { 0, 0.5, 0.9}. All methods use the same e-values, the likelihood ratios Ej = exp(2 Zj − 2) of N (2 , 1) against N (0 , 1), at level α = 0 .1. We report power, the expected fraction of false nulls that are rejected, and the FDR, each averaged over 50 ,000 data sets. Figure 3 shows all settings. The standard errors are very small, at most 0 .0023 for power and 0 .0013 for the FDR, so they are not shown. With 10 false nulls, RU-eBH and RC-eBH are the most powerful methods in every panel, and they nearly coincide. At signal mean 2 .5 with independent statistics, for example, RU-eBH has power 0 .32, compared with 0 .22 for U-eBH and J-eBH and at most 0 .06 for the deterministic procedures. Its FDR stays close to the bound α|T |/K = 0 .09 of Proposition 4.1 with independent statistics, whereas the FDR of U-eBH is between about 40% and 70% of this bound, which reflects the budget that U-eBH leaves unused. J-eBH has nearly the same FDR as RU-eBH but much lower power, so the gain of RU-eBH also comes from how it allocates the budget rather than from how much of the budget it spends. With more false nulls, RU-eBH remains at least as powerful as U-eBH and J-eBH, but the deterministic procedures based on larger e-collections catch up. With 50 false nulls and independent statistics, closed and anchored e-BH are more powerful than RU-eBH at strong signals, and with 90 false nulls, closed e-BH is more powerful at moderate and strong signals unless the correlation is high. This is consistent with the theory: the optimality of RU-eBH is relative to EeBH , which normalizes by K, whereas closed e-BH is certified by the mean e-collection, which normalizes by |T | and is much larger when few hypotheses are null. RC-eBH, which is certified by the same e-collection as closed e-BH, removes this gap. As part (ii) of Proposition 4.2 guarantees, it is more powerful than RU-eBH in every setting, and the gain grows with the number of false nulls; with 90 false nulls, independent statistics and signal mean 2, its power is 0 .64, compared with 0 .52 for RU-eBH and 0 .55 for closed e-BH. It is the most powerful of all eight methods, except at the strongest signals with independent statistics, where several methods reject nearly all false nulls and it trails the best of them by less than 0 .005. Its FDR stays below α but exceeds α|T |/K when many hypotheses are false, as the mean e-collection permits.

23 5 Discussion 

In both applications, recertification enlarges the menu of certified actions, and we selected its most liberal element – the least conservative controller in CRC, and the boosting rule that rejects the most hypotheses in multiple testing. Nothing in the framework requires this choice. By Theorem 2.1, every data-dependent selection from a certified menu is valid, so one may instead select the action that maximizes a utility, for instance a posterior expected utility under a prior. Such a selection is Bayes optimal among selections from the menu, and its validity does not depend on the prior. This differs from the constrained problems of Wald [1950] and Hodges and Lehmann [1952], which optimize a single rule subject to the risk constraints and, by Theorem 2.1, amount to optimizing the e-collection and the selection jointly. Similarly, our admissibility results compare procedures on every calibration sample or conditionally on the e-values; they do not address classical risk admissibility, which allows a rule to trade larger losses at some observations for smaller losses at others. In summary, this paper formulates the e-closure principle for general decision problems and uses the resulting correspondence constructively. Recertification starts by measuring the budget that a valid proce-dure leaves unused, spends it through larger e-values, and inverts them into an improved procedure with the same guarantee. The source of this budget differs between our two applications. In conformal risk control, exchangeability makes the budget computable on every hypothetical augmented sample, and recertification yields pointwise improvements of CRC and an admissible limit. In multiple testing, the e-values themselves supply the budget once randomized procedures are treated as randomized actions, and recertifying U-eBH yields two closed-form procedures, RU-eBH and RC-eBH, each optimal among the procedures certified by its e-collection. The two applications also differ in cost. RU-eBH and RC-eBH require only sorting the e-values and, for RC-eBH, a convex minorant, whereas repeated recertification of CRC trades computation for a uniform improvement, with diminishing returns, and can be expensive when the controller depends on individual scores. Exploiting the structure of the loss and the data to compute stronger certificates economically remains an important practical question, and whether RC-eBH is admissible among all FDR procedures is open. More broadly, any procedure whose validity proof leaves an identifiable budget unused is a candidate for recertification, and we expect further applications of this kind, especially those with more complex states, actions or loss structures.
