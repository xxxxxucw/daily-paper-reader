Abstract
Relational foundation models are increasingly pretrained on synthetic databases, yet downstream benchmarks
reveal little about why one synthetic corpus produces a better model than another. In particular, strong
performance may arise from realistic row-level statistics without the model ever learning to use relational
structure. We study this as a data-attribution problem: which property of synthetic pretraining data induces
relational computation? Using four Relational Transformer checkpoints trained with the same architecture,
initialization, objective, and compute budget on corpora produced by four relational data generators, we trace a
measurable property of the data to learned computation and downstream behavior. We hypothesize that
relational mechanisms emerge when cross-table information is predictively necessary for the masked-cell
pretraining objective. RelDiff exhibits by far the largest predictive gain from foreign-key-linked parents, and
its corresponding model is uniquely sensitive to foreign-key interventions on unseen databases. This
dependence survives a random-initialization control, grows monotonically with the fraction of corrupted links,
and localizes to a serial cross-table pathway. Finally, disrupting the same mechanism during downstream
inference removes RelDiff’s advantage on relational tasks while leaving structure-insensitive models nearly
unchanged. These results connect a property of synthetic training data to a learned mechanism and, through
intervention, to downstream behavior
Keywords: Relational Foundation Models; Synthetic Pretraining; Data Attribution; Mechanistic
Interpretability; Relational Learning

Introduction
Relational databases encode information at two levels: in the attributes of individual rows and in the relationships
that connect entities across tables. A customer’s properties, for example, may be informative on their own, but many
prediction problems additionally depend on related transactions, products, accounts, or organizations. Recent work has
therefore moved beyond single-table prediction [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] toward relational deep learning and relational foundation
models (RFMs), including Relational Deep Learning [6], RelBench [7], Griffin [8], KumoRFM [9], and the Relational
Transformer (RT) [10]. A central goal of these models is to transfer across databases and prediction tasks while
exploiting foreign-key (FK) structure, rather than requiring a separately trained model and manually engineered joins or
aggregations for every task.
Pretraining such models creates a fundamental data problem. Large and diverse collections of real relational databases
are difficult to obtain, frequently proprietary, and heterogeneous in both schema and semantics. This has motivated the
use of synthetic data for foundation-model pretraining. In the single-table setting, O’PRIOR [11] shows that the design
of the synthetic pretraining distribution itself can be a first-order determinant of downstream accuracy and robustness,

even when model architecture and training budget are held fixed. This question becomes especially important in
the relational setting, where a synthetic generator must determine not only feature distributions but also schemas,
cardinalities, foreign-key structure, and statistical dependencies across related tables. RelDiff [12] and graph-conditional
relational diffusion models (GRDM) [13] generate data conditioned on real database structure, whereas PluRel [14] and
RDB-PFN [15] construct relational pretraining distributions without requiring a reference database. These approaches
differ substantially in how schemas, cardinalities, attribute distributions, and dependencies between related tables are
generated.
A natural way to compare such generators is to pretrain the same model on each resulting corpus and evaluate the
checkpoints on downstream tasks. This establishes which synthetic pretraining distribution produces the strongest
model, but it does not establish why. A generator may improve downstream performance because it better reproduces
single-column marginals, because it produces more diverse schemas, because its rows contain more predictable within-
table structure, because its foreign-key relationships carry useful signal, or because of some combination of these
factors. A benchmark score alone cannot identify which property of the synthetic data produced a particular learned
capability. Our central question is therefore not simply which generator performs best?, but rather:
What property of a synthetic relational pretraining distribution causes a model to learn to use
relations?
We view this as a problem of data attribution. Classical influence functions [16], TracIn [17], datamodels [18], and
TRAK [19] attribute predictions or model behaviors to individual training examples or subsets. Our attribution target is
different: a synthetic generator induces an entire distribution over relational databases. Attributing a capability directly
to generator identity is therefore too coarse. We instead seek an intermediate property of the generated distribution that
explains why a particular computation becomes useful during pretraining.
Our hypothesis is motivated by RT’s masked-cell reconstruction objective. If a masked value is already predictable
from information in its own row, a model can reduce pretraining loss without making meaningful use of related tables.
If FK-linked rows contain additional predictive information unavailable locally, however, exploiting relational context
becomes useful for solving the objective. We refer to this property as relational predictive necessity. Importantly, this
distinguishes architectural capacity from learned reliance: providing a model with cross-table computational pathways
does not imply that pretraining will make those pathways functionally important. Section 2 formalizes this hypothesis
and describes the relevant RT mechanisms.
We test this idea by tracing a sequence from the training data to model behavior. First, we measure relational predictive
signal directly in the generated corpora, without using RT. We then test whether the corresponding pretrained checkpoints
functionally depend on FK structure during masked reconstruction. Next, we intervene on RT’s internal cross-table
computation to localize the mechanism responsible for this dependence. Finally, we perturb the same relational
information during downstream RelBench inference to determine whether the identified mechanism is responsible for
the observed downstream behavior.
Our contributions are threefold:
1. A data-side measure of relational predictive necessity. We quantify the predictive gain provided by
FK-linked information beyond features available in the target row, giving a model-independent measure of
whether a synthetic pretraining corpus rewards cross-table computation.
2. A mechanistic analysis of relational reliance. Using structural corruption, a random-initialization
control, graded interventions, and pathway-specific ablations, we show that the RT checkpoint pretrained on
the corpus with the strongest parent-linked predictive signal develops substantially stronger functional reliance
on relational structure. We further localize this dependence to a specific serial cross-table computation.
3. A causal connection between mechanism and downstream behavior. We rerun downstream
inference while disrupting FK structure or the corresponding internal computation. On a task where relational
structure provides a large advantage, disrupting the identified mechanism removes that advantage, whereas
checkpoints that do not rely on relational structure remain nearly unchanged. We also identify cases in which
disrupting relational information improves performance, showing that learned relational priors can help or hurt
depending on how well they match the downstream database.
The causal scope of these results is important. Our inference-time interventions establish a causal link between
the identified relational computation and model behavior while holding the trained checkpoint fixed. The synthetic
pretraining corpora themselves, however, are not produced by independently randomizing relational predictive necessity
while holding every other property constant. We therefore treat the relationship between corpus predictive structure and
the learned mechanism as evidence for our hypothesis, rather than as a universal causal law. A stronger data-side causal

Problem Setup and Hypothesis
2.1
Controlled Comparison of Synthetic Pretraining Distributions
We study four checkpoints of the Relational Transformer (RT) [10], a relational foundation model pretrained by
masked-cell reconstruction on multi-table databases. Given a partially masked database, RT predicts the missing cell
values from the remaining cells and the database’s relational structure.
The four checkpoints use the same model architecture: 12 transformer blocks, hidden dimension 256, and approximately
19.2M parameters. They also share the same masked-value reconstruction objective, initialization, and training budget.
The experimental variable is the synthetic corpus used for pretraining. Each checkpoint is trained on 1,000 generated
databases produced by one of four relational data generators: RelDiff [12], GRDM [13], PluRel [14], or RDB-PFN
[15].
These generators represent two broad approaches to relational synthesis. RelDiff and GRDM are reference-conditioned:
their generated databases are tied to structural properties of real reference databases. PluRel and RDB-PFN are
reference-free: they sample relational databases from designed synthetic priors rather than conditioning generation on a
particular reference database. The resulting corpora consequently differ not only in relational dependencies, but also in
properties such as feature distributions, schema structure, cardinalities, and data types.
This distinction is important for the interpretation of our experiments. We do not treat generator identity itself as
the causal variable. A statement such as "RelDiff teaches relational reasoning" would conflate all properties that
differ between the generated corpora. Instead, we measure a specific property of those corpora—whether FK-linked
information provides predictive signal beyond information available within a row—and ask whether that property is
associated with the relational computation learned during pretraining.
2.2
Cross-Table Computation in RT
RT represents database cells as tokens and applies separate attention operations along different axes of the relational
input. Our analysis focuses on two operations through which information can cross table boundaries.
Parent access (feat).
The feature-attention stream normally operates over cells associated with the target row, but
its attention mask additionally exposes cells from rows referenced through foreign keys. Consequently, when predicting
a cell in a child row, the model can directly access attributes of the corresponding FK-linked parent row. We refer to
this operation as parent access.
Child aggregation (nbr).
The neighbor-attention stream operates in the complementary direction. It allows a
representation associated with a parent entity to aggregate information from rows that reference that entity through a
foreign key. We refer to this operation as child aggregation.
These two routes are architecturally available in every checkpoint. Thus, architecture alone cannot explain differences
in relational behavior: all four models have the capacity to exchange information across tables. The relevant question is
whether their respective pretraining distributions create sufficient optimization pressure for those pathways to become
functionally important.
Importantly, we do not assume a priori that parent access and child aggregation constitute independent or serial compu-
tations. Their functional interaction is determined experimentally in Sec. 3.3 through pathway-specific interventions.
2.3
Evaluation Setting
Model-side analyses are performed on real RelBench databases [7, 20]: rel-stack, rel-arxiv, and rel-ratebeer.
All checkpoint comparisons use identical preprocessed inputs and, within each experiment, identical masked cells,
batches, and interventions. This paired design ensures that differences in reconstruction loss arise from the checkpoints’
responses to the same inputs rather than from different sampled examples.
Our most controlled mechanistic experiments—including the random-initialization baseline, graded FK corruption, and
pathway-specific ablations—use fixed rel-stack batches so that the interventions can be compared directly under a

2.4
Relational Predictive Necessity
Our hypothesis follows directly from the masked-cell reconstruction objective. Let y denote a masked target value,
xrow the information available from the target row, and xrel information available through FK-linked rows. If relational
information contributes no additional predictive signal, then
p(y | xrow, xrel) ≈p(y | xrow).
In this regime, a model can reduce reconstruction loss primarily from within-row statistics. Cross-table pathways may
exist architecturally but are not required to solve the pretraining task.
Conversely, when
p(y | xrow, xrel)̸ = p(y | xrow),
FK-linked information contains predictive signal unavailable locally. Exploiting relational context can then reduce
the pretraining loss beyond what is achievable from the target row alone. We call this condition relational predictive
necessity.
We do not attempt to estimate the conditional distributions above directly. Instead, Section 3.1 operationalizes predictive
necessity through out-of-sample predictive gain: we compare a predictor that uses only target-row information with the
same predictor augmented by information from related tables. This measurement is performed entirely on the synthetic
corpora and does not involve RT, preventing the quantity being explained from also defining the explanatory variable.
The hypothesis generates three linked empirical predictions:
H1: Predictive structure in the pretraining data.
Synthetic corpora differ in the amount of target information
available through FK-linked rows beyond what can already be recovered from the target row. A corpus that creates
stronger relational predictive necessity should therefore exhibit larger out-of-sample predictive gains when relational
information is added.
H2: Learned relational reliance.
If relational predictive necessity creates pressure to learn cross-table computa-
tion, the corresponding pretrained checkpoint should become functionally dependent on correct relational structure.
Corrupting FK assignments or disabling the relevant internal pathways should therefore increase reconstruction loss
substantially more for that checkpoint than for models whose pretraining objectives could be solved primarily from
row-local information.
H3: Relational mechanism and downstream behavior.
If the learned cross-table computation is responsible
for a downstream capability, intervening on that computation while holding the trained checkpoint fixed should produce
a corresponding change in downstream performance. Conversely, checkpoints that do not functionally rely on relational
structure during reconstruction should be comparatively insensitive to the same intervention.
Together, these predictions define the empirical chain studied in the remainder of the paper:
predictive structure in data −→relational reliance −→localized cross-table mechanism −→downstream behavior
The arrows have different causal status. The inference-time interventions used to test H2 and H3 manipulate the trained
model or its relational inputs while holding the checkpoint fixed, permitting causal conclusions about the dependence
of model behavior on the intervened mechanism. By contrast, the four pretraining corpora were not generated by
independently manipulating relational predictive necessity while holding all other distributional properties fixed. The
data-to-mechanism link in H1–H2 is therefore a controlled observational comparison. Establishing that link causally
would require retraining on corpora in which cross-table predictive coupling is selectively manipulated while other
corpus properties, initialization, objective, and training budget are held constant.

Experimental Evaluation
We evaluate the relational-predictive-necessity hypothesis through four increasingly model-dependent stages. First,
we measure whether FK-linked information is predictively useful in the synthetic pretraining corpora themselves,
without using RT. Second, we test whether the corresponding trained checkpoints functionally depend on relational
structure during masked-cell reconstruction. Third, we intervene on RT’s internal cross-table pathways to localize the
computation responsible for that dependence. Finally, we apply the same structural and internal interventions during
downstream RelBench inference.
This progression separates three questions that are often conflated in evaluations of synthetic pretraining data: whether
relations contain useful signal in the generated corpus, whether a model actually learns to use that signal, and whether
the resulting computation matters for downstream prediction.
3.1
Predictive Structure in Synthetic Pretraining Data
Our first experiment tests H1 directly at the data level. The goal is to determine whether a target value in each synthetic
corpus becomes more predictable when information from FK-linked tables is made available, independently of any
representation learned by RT.
Protocol.
For each eligible target column y, we construct three prediction problems using the same LightGBM
learner. The first uses only features available in the target row. The second augments those features with attributes from
the FK-linked parent row. The third instead augments the target row with simple statistics of linked children: mean,
standard deviation, and count. Let the corresponding held-out scores be
Srow = R2(y | xrow),
Sparent = R2(y | xrow, xparent),
and
Schild = R2(y | xrow, a(xchild)) ,
where a(·) denotes the child-aggregation features. We define
∆parent = Sparent −Srow,
∆child = Schild −Srow.
Positive gain therefore means that relational information predicts the held-out target beyond what is available from the
target row alone.
We sample 40 generated databases from each corpus. Individual held-out R2 values are clipped to [−1, 1] before
differences are computed, preventing a small number of pathological fits from dominating the aggregate. Table 1 reports
medians over target-column prediction problems; parent gains additionally include 95% bootstrap confidence intervals.
Full task-level distributions are shown in Appendix B.1.
RelDiff contains substantially stronger parent-linked predictive signal.
Table 1 reveals markedly different
pretraining regimes. In RelDiff, adding linked-parent attributes improves median held-out R2 by 0.072. The corre-
sponding gain is 0.009 for PluRel and approximately zero for RDB-PFN. RelDiff’s parent gain is therefore about eight
times PluRel’s and more than two orders of magnitude larger than the rounded RDB-PFN gain.
The contrast with row-local predictability is also informative. RDB-PFN has by far the largest row-only score
(R2 = 0.436) while exhibiting essentially no gain from parent information. Its generated values are therefore
comparatively predictable without cross-table context. RelDiff lies at the opposite extreme: row-local predictability is
weak (R2 = 0.022), while linked parents provide substantial additional signal.

3.2
Functional Reliance on Relational Structure
The corpus experiment establishes that relational information can be predictive, but this does not show that RT actually
learned to use it. We therefore intervene directly on relational structure during masked-cell reconstruction.
3.2.1
Structural Interventions
Our primary controlled probe uses 24 fixed rel-stack batches, corresponding to 384 masked cells at context length
1,024. Every checkpoint is evaluated on exactly the same examples and masks under four conditions:
• Original: ordinary inference on the unmodified database;
• FK shuffle: FK-neighbor assignments are permuted among a selected set of row positions while cell values
are left unchanged;
• FK removal: relational indices are removed;
• Neighbor ablation: the input database is unchanged, but the output of RT’s nbr stream is set to zero.
For intervention c, we report ∆Lc = Lc −L0, where L0 is the original masked-cell reconstruction loss. Positive values
therefore indicate that the perturbed information or computation was useful.
RelDiff is substantially more dependent on correct FK structure.
Full FK shuffling raises RelDiff’s loss
from 0.241 to 0.443, an absolute increase of 0.202 or 83.6%. GRDM, PluRel, and RDB-PFN increase by only 5.3%,
6.1%, and 3.4%, respectively. FK removal produces the same qualitative ordering. The result is therefore not specific to
one particular corruption procedure.
Internal intervention gives a complementary result. Zeroing nbr increases RelDiff’s loss by 0.127, whereas the same
ablation has negligible effect on the other trained checkpoints. Thus, the difference is not merely that RelDiff reacts
strongly to an unusual input perturbation: an internal cross-table computation is itself load-bearing.
3.2.2
Architectural Floor and Graded Corruption
FK shuffling also changes the relational masks supplied to RT, so some response can arise from the architecture even
in the absence of learning. We quantify this floor using a randomly initialized RT with exactly the same architecture.
Full FK shuffling increases its loss by 4.9%, comparable to the 3.4–6.1% changes of the three weakly reliant trained
checkpoints but far below RelDiff’s 83.6% increase. RelDiff’s relative response is approximately 17× the random-
initialization effect.

3.2.3
Reliance Across Real Databases
The preceding experiments deliberately use one fixed rel-stack protocol so that binary corruption, graded corruption,
pathway ablation, and the random-initialization control are directly comparable. We additionally ask whether the
qualitative checkpoint ordering survives across real databases.
We therefore evaluate the original structural-intervention protocol over 27 fixed probe batches drawn from rel-stack,
rel-arxiv, and rel-ratebeer. Because this is a different probe suite, its absolute loss values should not be compared
directly with Table 2.
The broader evaluation reproduces the aggregate separation: RelDiff is the only checkpoint for which FK corruption
and internal neighbor ablation both produce substantial mean degradation. Importantly, however, the sign is not
uniform across databases. For RelDiff, full FK shuffling changes reconstruction loss by +0.165 on rel-stack, +0.067
on rel-ratebeer, and −0.112 on rel-arxiv. Thus, the learned model is sensitive to relational structure across
databases, but that structure is not always beneficial. On rel-arxiv, corrupting the learned relational prior improves
reconstruction.
This distinction will matter again downstream: relational reliance should be interpreted as a learned inductive bias, not
as a guarantee that the bias is correct for every target database.

3.3
Mechanistic Localization of Cross-Table Computation
Structural corruption establishes dependence on relational information but perturbs every operation that consumes FK
indices. We next intervene directly on RT’s internal cross-table pathways to determine how the relational computation
is organized.
3.3.1
Pathway-Specific Interventions
The pathway experiment uses 12 fixed rel-stack batches, corresponding to 192 masked cells at context length 1,024.
We compare four conditions:
1. Original: both cross-table pathways operate normally;
2. No child aggregation: the nbr output is zeroed;
3. No parent access: the feat attention mask is restricted to same-row cells;
4. Both: child aggregation and parent access are disabled simultaneously.
These interventions distinguish two possible organizations. If nbr and feat provide independent routes by which
relational information reaches the target, ablating both should be more damaging than ablating either route alone. If they
instead form a serial computation, disabling a downstream route should make removal of the upstream route redundant.
3.3.2
A Serial Relational Pathway
Figure 2 supports the serial interpretation. RelDiff’s original loss is 0.258. Removing child aggregation raises it to
0.386 (∆L = +0.128), while removing parent access raises it to 0.453 (+0.195). Crucially, disabling both routes also
yields 0.453, identical to parent-access ablation alone to six decimal places.
The equality is not caused by accidentally silencing nbr. Its activation magnitude remains essentially unchanged when
parent access is blocked. The neighbor computation is therefore still performed, but its output can no longer influence
the masked target. The functional pathway supported by these interventions is
linked child rows
nbr
−−−→parent representation
feat
−−−→masked target cell.
Thus, nbr should not be interpreted as an independent direct route from children to the target. It first enriches the
representation of the linked parent, and feat subsequently exposes that representation to the queried cell.
3.3.3
Fine-Grained Functional Specialization Across Heads
The pathway intervention establishes that the nbr stream is functionally important for RelDiff, but does not reveal
whether this dependence is spread uniformly across its attention heads. We therefore ablate the eight nbr heads
individually and measure the resulting change in reconstruction loss.

3.4
Causal Link to Downstream Performance
The preceding experiments connect a property of the synthetic corpora to a learned cross-table computation. We now
ask whether this computation explains downstream predictive behavior.
3.4.1
Downstream Evaluation and Corrected Context Scaling
We evaluate the four checkpoints using RT’s in-context inference protocol on nine RelBench tasks across context
lengths {100, 200, 512, 1024, 30000}. Six tasks are classification problems and are summarized by AUROC.

3.4.3
When Relational Priors Hurt
Relational reliance is not uniformly beneficial. On rel-ratebeer user-churn at context 1,024, FK shuffling improves
RelDiff from 0.612 to 0.673 (+0.061 AUROC) and GRDM from 0.627 to 0.691 (+0.064), while PluRel and RDB-PFN
change by at most 0.007.
The same phenomenon appears in Table 4: on rel-stack user-engagement at 30k, shuffling FK structure improves
GRDM from 0.520 to 0.622. These sign reversals are useful controls because they show that the intervention is not
merely removing information that must always help. A learned relational computation acts as an inductive bias. When
the learned relation between topology and attributes matches the downstream problem, preserving relational structure
can be beneficial; when it is mismatched, destroying that structure can improve prediction.
This behavior parallels the masked-reconstruction probe, where RelDiff is harmed by FK corruption on rel-stack
and rel-ratebeer but improves on rel-arxiv. Relational reliance and relational usefulness are therefore distinct: a
checkpoint may strongly use relational structure even when the particular structural prior it applies is poorly matched to
a target database.
4
Discussion and Limitations
Our results distinguish architectural access to relational information from learned functional reliance on relations.
All four checkpoints use the same FK-aware RT architecture and therefore have access to the same cross-table
computational routes, yet they respond very differently when relational structure is corrupted or internally ablated.
The checkpoint trained on the corpus with the strongest measurable parent-linked predictive signal also develops the
strongest dependence on correct FK structure. That dependence can be localized to a concrete serial cross-table pathway,
and intervention on the same computation substantially changes downstream behavior. The strongest causal evidence in
our study therefore concerns the mechanism-to-behavior link: when the trained checkpoint is held fixed, disrupting the
relational structure or the corresponding internal computation changes the prediction.
The data-side analysis suggests a broader design principle for synthetic relational pretraining. Structural complexity
alone is not sufficient. A generated corpus may contain many tables, foreign keys, diverse schemas, or heterogeneous
cardinalities while still allowing the pretraining objective to be solved largely from row-local regularities. To create
optimization pressure for cross-table computation, relational context must provide predictive information that is not
already available locally. Our diversity analysis supports this distinction: corpus diversity does not reproduce the
ordering of functional relational reliance across checkpoints (Appendix B.2). This suggests that future relational data
generators should be evaluated not only by how much structural variation they produce, but also by whether that
structure carries predictive information relevant to the model’s pretraining objective.
The results also emphasize that relational reliance and relational usefulness are not equivalent. On some databases
and tasks, corrupting relational structure improves reconstruction or downstream accuracy. The learned relational
computation should therefore be viewed as an inductive bias rather than an intrinsically beneficial capability. When
the statistical dependencies learned during pretraining align with the target database, the bias can improve prediction;
when they are mismatched, the same mechanism can be harmful. This distinction is important for evaluating relational
foundation models: sensitivity to structure demonstrates that the model is using relations, but does not by itself establish
that the learned relational prior transfers correctly to every schema or task.
Our study has several limitations. First, we analyze a single relational architecture and a single masked-cell reconstruc-
tion objective. The specific nbr→feat computation identified here is therefore RT-specific, although the data-side

notion of relational predictive necessity is not tied to a particular architecture and may generalize to other relational
pretraining settings. Second, each generator is represented by one trained checkpoint. We can therefore measure paired
intervention effects within each checkpoint, but we cannot estimate variability across training seeds, independently
generated corpora, or repeated pretraining runs. Third, the four synthetic corpora differ along multiple dimensions
simultaneously. The association between corpus predictive structure and learned relational reliance is therefore a
controlled observational attribution rather than a randomized intervention on the training distribution. A stronger
causal test would begin from the same generated corpus, selectively destroy FK-conditioned predictive coupling while
preserving marginal and row-local statistics, retrain from the same initialization and budget, and test whether relational
reliance disappears. The complementary sufficiency test would inject such coupling into a weakly relational corpus and
ask whether reliance emerges. Finally, the available GRDM corpus contains the semantic-type preprocessing confound
described in Section 2.3. We therefore retain GRDM as an informative comparison but avoid interpreting its absolute
ranking as an isolated estimate of the quality of its relational generation procedure.
Conclusion.
Synthetic-data benchmarks can reveal which pretraining corpus produces the strongest model, but not
what properties of that corpus produced a capability. We trace one such capability across four levels of analysis: relational
predictive signal is measurable directly in the synthetic pretraining distribution; the corresponding checkpoint develops
load-bearing dependence on FK structure; that dependence can be localized to a specific cross-table computation; and
intervening on the same mechanism changes downstream behavior. More broadly, our results motivate attributing the
effects of synthetic pretraining not only to generator identity, but to measurable properties of the generated distribution
that create optimization pressure for particular computations to be learned.

on how well the learned prior matches the target database and task.

Implementation and Reproducibility Details
This section records the evaluation protocols underlying the primary results. Within each intervention experiment, all
checkpoints are evaluated on identical examples, masks, and randomly selected corruption positions so that changes are
paired at the example level.
Predictive-necessity analysis.
For each of the four synthetic corpora, we sample 40 generated databases. Each
eligible target column defines a held-out prediction problem. Identical LightGBM procedures are used for the row-only,
row-plus-parent, and row-plus-child-aggregate feature sets. Child features consist of mean, standard deviation, and
count aggregates over linked rows. Held-out R2 scores are clipped to [−1, 1] before relational gains are computed.
Tables report medians across target-column tasks, and parent gains include 95% bootstrap confidence intervals.
Structural interventions.
All checkpoints receive identical preprocessed inputs. FK shuffling permutes FK-
neighbor index assignments among the selected row positions while leaving cell values unchanged. FK removal sets
the corresponding relational indices to the missing-link value. Internal neighbor ablation leaves the input database
unchanged and sets the output of the nbr module to zero during inference.
Random-initialization and graded-corruption experiment.
The controlled rel-stack experiment uses 24
fixed batches containing 384 masked cells at context length 1,024. The random-initialization control uses the same
RT architecture without pretraining. Graded corruption evaluates ρ ∈{0, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1} on exactly the same
examples; ρ denotes the fraction of FK assignments selected for corruption.
Pathway intervention.
The pathway-specific experiment uses 12 fixed rel-stack batches containing 192 masked
cells at context length 1,024. Child aggregation is disabled by zeroing the output of nbr. Parent access is disabled by
modifying the feat attention mask so that only same-row cells remain accessible. The joint condition applies both
interventions simultaneously.
Individual-head intervention.
For the head-level experiment, each of the eight nbr attention heads is ablated
individually while all other model components remain unchanged. Functional importance is measured by the resulting
increase in masked-cell reconstruction loss relative to the same intact batches. When reporting the fraction of RelDiff’s
positive head-attributable effect, negative knockout changes are excluded from the denominator rather than treated as
negative importance.
Broader real-database probe.
The cross-database structural-reliance experiment uses 27 fixed probe batches
drawn from rel-stack, rel-arxiv, and rel-ratebeer. This probe suite is distinct from the 24-batch controlled
rel-stack experiment; absolute loss values between the two protocols are therefore not directly compared.
Downstream evaluation.
Downstream experiments use RT’s standard in-context inference protocol on nine
RelBench tasks and context lengths 100, 200, 512, 1024, and 30000. Classification performance is measured by
AUROC. The corrected 30k evaluations of the two rare-label rel-stack tasks use n = 2048 examples. For every
downstream intervention, the original, FK-shuffled, and nbr-ablated conditions are evaluated on identical examples.
Scope of statistical comparisons.
The predictive-necessity experiment reports bootstrap uncertainty across
target-column prediction problems. The mechanistic intervention experiments instead use paired, fixed-batch compar-
isons: the same masked cells are evaluated before and after each intervention. Because each generator is represented
by one pretrained checkpoint, these experiments quantify within-checkpoint intervention effects rather than variance
across independent pretraining seeds.
