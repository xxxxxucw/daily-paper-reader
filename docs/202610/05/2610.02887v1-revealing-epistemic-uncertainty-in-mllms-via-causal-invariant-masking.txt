Abstract
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) suffer from hallucinations, creating
a critical need for Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) to ensure reliable deployment.
However, existing approaches struggle to detect uncertainty caused by superfi-
cial associations, especially when the query-relevant signal is weak. We mainly
attribute this issue to their bias toward aleatoric uncertainty arising from data
ambiguity, overlooking epistemic uncertainty stemming from model limitations.
To further decompose uncertainty types for a comprehensive UQ, we propose
Causal-Invariant Masking (CIM), which measures the semantic shift between the
original predictions and those conditioned on a causally-focused view. Based on
this framework, we introduce Semantic Divergence as our core metric for UQ and
provide theoretical evidence that it converges to the variance of model’s sensitivity
to non-causal correlations, establishing its ability to capture MLLM’s limitation. To
accelerate UQ in MLLMs, we further propose Expected Embedding Drift (EED),
a fast geometric proxy metric that estimates semantic shift directly within the
hyperspherical embedding space. Experiments show that our method achieves
state-of-the-art performance on various benchmarks, while the proposed EED
accelerates by nearly 50% with comparable performance.

Introduction
Large Language Models (LLMs) [51, 32] and Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) [47, 4]
have significantly altered the landscape of artificial intelligence. By connecting visual perception
with textual semantics, MLLMs are able to demonstrate consistent success in tasks ranging from
visual question answering to complex reasoning [28, 37, 50]. However, their reliable deployment in
real-world applications requires solving the severe problem of hallucination [5, 17], where MLLMs
confidently generate factually incorrect but grammatically plausible content. Especially for VLMs,
non-existent objects, attributes, and events may unreasonably occur in the responses when misleading
factors, either in vision or language, dominate the query. It suggests that MLLMs do not internally

realize their visual capability boundary and highlights a critical need for robust safety measures
targeting generative trustworthiness.
With this aim, numerous Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) methods have been developed. By es-
timating the confidence level of a model, systems can abstain from answering or flag potential
hallucinations when encountering high uncertainty. Therefore, precise UQ becomes a proxy for
hallucination detection. Traditional UQ approaches typically measure the entropy of the predictive
distribution, often aggregated at the token level [46, 42]. Recent advancements [21, 20, 34] have
shifted towards measuring semantic uncertainty using sampling-based methods, such as Semantic
Entropy (SE) [13], which aggregates probabilities over clusters of similar generations to calculate the
response variety.
However, these approaches exhibit limited discrimination under hallucinative visual content, espe-
cially when the query-irrelevant elements generate spurious hints. For instance, background objects
with prominent colors can dominate the response to queries on the foreground object, making con-
sistent mistakes in the sampled output. Thus, a more comprehensive UQ is necessary for MLLMs.
In fact, both epistemic and aleatoric uncertainties play important roles in UQ, where epistemic un-
certainty reveals insufficiency in the model’s perception or comprehension and aleatoric uncertainty
reflects the poor data quality or incomplete information [19]. Quantifying both of them is crucial in
UQ [41, 1], however, existing approaches fail to do so because they typically assume that all forms of
uncertainty can be equally estimated. In this paper, we show that they are biased towards quantifying
mostly aleatoric uncertainty in MLLMs, leaving epistemic uncertainty underestimated.
We introduce Causal-Invariant Masking (CIM), a framework that disentangles intrinsic epistemic
instability from data ambiguity by probing semantic consistency under causally invariant transforms.
Our core insight is that robust reasoning must remain invariant when task-irrelevant features are
removed. By constructing a Region-of-Interest (RoI) view that retains only causal features, we
measure the shift in the model’s belief distribution. We quantify this using Semantic Divergence,
defined as the shift from the semantic distributions of the original to the focused inputs. Supported
by theoretical derivation, this metric targets the model stability against irrelevant distractions to
effectively isolate epistemic uncertainty, thereby alleviating the biased quantification in previous
works. To address the computational costs of discrete semantic clustering, we then propose a
fast proxy called Expected Embedding Drift (EED). Leveraging the hyperspherical geometry of
the embedding space, EED estimates semantic shift directly from token embeddings, eliminating
costly discrete operations. Experiments demonstrate that our method significantly outperforms
baselines, achieving state-of-the-art hallucination detection of 76.8% AUROC on the challenging
AdVQA and superior results on POPE. Furthermore, EED reduces computational overhead by 50%
without compromising performance, facilitating applications such as hallucination mitigation and
adaptive-resolution inference.

Related Work
2.1
Uncertainty Estimation in LLMs
UQ in LLMs is pivotal for reliability and hallucination detection, generally categorized into intrinsic,
verbalized, and consistency-based methods. Intrinsic metrics like perplexity [41] measure token-level
confidence, while verbalized methods [45, 43] prompt models to explicitly express uncertainty,
often yielding better calibration than raw probabilities. Consistency-based works are currently
dominant approaches. Semantic Entropy [13] and Semantic Dispersion [27] detect hallucinations by
aggregating multiple sampled responses based on meaning rather than exact phrasing. Recent works
further exploit internal representations as uncertainty indicators by utilizing activation covariance
and propose EigenScore [10] or geometric convex hull analysis [9] to quantify dispersion in high-
dimensional embedding spaces. However, these methods target general UQ for LLMs and can be
challenged in vision-language tasks.
2.2
Uncertainty Estimation in MLLMs
Extending UQ to MLLMs involves challenges from combined visual and textual modalities, often
focusing on hallucination detection in vision-language tasks. Similar to those for LLMs, consistency-
based sampling techniques are explored, e.g., [23] develops UMPIRE, a training-free UQ framework
2


that estimates consistency in multiple sampled responses by a coherence-adjusted semantic volume.
Perturbation-based methods introduce variations in inputs to probe uncertainty. VL-Uncertainty [48]
and Uncertainty-o [49] measure hallucination in LVLMs by computing entropy over clustered
responses, but using semantic-equivalently perturbed visual and textual queries, supporting tasks of
hallucination mitigation and uncertainty-aware reasoning. However, these approaches typically rely
on global perturbations under the full context, without explicitly isolating the model’s reliance on
critical and irrelevant features. CIM is also a perturbation-style test in its implementation, but its
perturbation is query-conditioned and RoI-preserving. The goal is to compare the original belief with
a view that keeps the answer evidence while suppressing irrelevant context, rather than purely testing
variance under general information loss.

3.2
Epistemic Uncertainty Isolation via Causal Masking
Reliable MLLM deployment requires separating aleatoric uncertainty, which reflects data ambiguity,
from epistemic uncertainty, which reflects model limitation. Existing metrics often blend the two and
therefore skew toward aleatoric noise. As illustrated in Fig. 1 middle, Semantic Entropy rises quickly
under blurring for aleatoric uncertainty, but is much less sensitive to background mixup for epistemic
uncertainty, suggesting that it treats causal and non-causal features alike. Robust reasoning instead
should satisfy causal invariance: predictions should stay stable when task-irrelevant context changes,
whereas uncertain responses should not.

Theoretical Analysis
We justify why Usem isolates epistemic uncertainty by framing the MLLM’s semantic output as an
Energy-Based Model (EBM) [8, 24, 12]. This is compatible with autoregressive decoding: prior
work interprets pre-softmax logits as token energies [33], connects left-to-right language models
and EBMs through a function-space equivalence [6], and uses energy functions for constrained text
decoding [39]. Because MLLM decoding remains autoregressive after conditioning on images, the
4


same energy view applies. Under standard causal assumptions, the divergence in Eq. (1) converges to
the variance of sensitivity to spurious features.
Standard causal theory [36, 35] models the input v as causal variables vc and non-causal variables vs.
An oracle predictor would satisfy P(s|vc, vs) = P(s|vc), but real models often fail to be invariant.
We therefore introduce a unified assumption on the masking operation and the energy landscape:

4.2
Discussions
Causal Masking.
Our theoretical framework proposes that the visual input operator T(·) must
focus on isolating causal features xc to separate epistemic from aleatoric uncertainty. To validate
such a design choice of xc-focused RoI Cropping, we compare it against: 1) Random Crop, which
crops an arbitrary region, 2) Reversed Crop, which masks the detected RoIs and keeps only the
background, and 3) Ground-Truth RoIs, which are from the box-annotated GQA benchmark. The
ablation results are presented in Table 3. We also compare with the commonly used gaussian noise
perturbation in Appendix C.3. From the results, Random Crop yield inferior performance compared
to our targeted approach. This aligns with our hypothesis that random perturbations degrade the
aleatoric and epistemic signals indiscriminately. Crucially, Reversed Crop performs significantly
worse than Random Crop in most tested cases. This is because feeding only the background forces
the model to hallucinate or guess, but does not provide an anchor point for the correct reasoning. In
contrast, our method of RoI Crop achieves significantly larger AUROC across all settings (e.g., 81.3%
on VQAv2 for InternVL). By preserving necessary evidence while maximally removing spurious
context, our measurement effectively grounds the MLLM’s confidence in causal signals, providing
the cleanest isolation of epistemic uncertainty.
While the SE component is included to retain aleatoric sensitivity for hallucination detection, it can
dilute the random-vs-RoI gap on epistemic UQ. To isolate the epistemic component, the AUROCEPI
metric reports AUROCs in a pure SD setup without the auxiliary SE score. Under this setting, the
separation becomes much larger: Our RoI Crop improves over Random Crop by 6.5/9.2 AUROC on
InternVL VQAv2/AdVQA or by 11.6/14.0 AUROC on QwenVL VQAv2/AdVQA, and is even more
superior to the Reversed Crop. We additionally include GQA [18] because it provides ground-truth
object annotations, enabling a controlled comparison with GT RoIs. On GQA, our operation is
comparable to GT RoIs Crop and both clearly outperform the others, validating that CIM relies on
preserving query-relevant causal evidence rather than arbitrary perturbation, and exact crops are less
critical than retaining causal evidence.
Number of Samples.
In Fig. 3, we show the optimal sampling allocation under a fixed total
number K + K′ between estimating the original P(s|v), i.e., for SE and Usem, and measuring the
masked P(s|v′) only for Usem. We observe a consistent middle-peaking trend across benchmarks.
The maximal performance is achieved at a balanced sampling budget of 40%-60% each. Biased
allocations degrade performance, as insufficient samples for either distribution lead to mis-estimation
of one type of uncertainty. Furthermore, while increasing the total budget universally improves
AUROC, the optimal allocation proportion remains stable, suggesting its robustness in such scaling.

Beyond detection, a practical uncertainty
metric should enable the model to correct its own errors.
We study whether CIM can act as a filter to improve factual quality. In this setting, we perform a
reject-and-refine strategy: if the estimated uncertainty Usem exceeds a threshold, we instruct the model
to re-generate the answer. Accuracy improvements on VQAv2, OKVQA, and AdVQA are reported in
Fig. 5, which demonstrates the effectiveness of such intervention. By using our uncertainty estimates
to trigger re-answering, we observe consistent accuracy gains across all benchmarks. Importantly, on
the difficult AdVQA benchmark, the performance of InternVL improves from 47.9% to 51.1%, and
QwenVL from 47.5% to 50.0%. This significant increase indicates that our method successfully flags
epistemic failures where the model was previously hallucinating due to adversarial visual contexts.

5
Conclusion
This work addresses the critical bias in existing MLLM uncertainty quantification methods toward
aleatoric uncertainty, which often ignores hallucinations driven by spurious correlations. We proposed
CIM, a framework that isolates epistemic uncertainty by measuring the semantic shift under causally
invariant perturbations with theoretical guarantee. Our proposed Semantic Divergence effectively
targets reliance on non-causal features, achieving state-of-the-art performance. Furthermore, the
Expected Embedding Drift (EED) reduces computational latency by 50% without compromising
accuracy. By enabling robust hallucination detection and efficient adaptive inference, CIM enables a
rigorous path toward trustworthy multimodal reasoning.
Limitations.
CIM depends on the quality of the RoIs and equivalence estimation, so failures of
them can affect the results. Nevertheless, we empirically show their impact is limited.

B
Detailed Experiment Setup
Computational Resources
We carried out all experiments on 4 NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs.
Model Parameters
The implementation uses an inference temperature of 0.1, while the sampling
temperature is 0.5. The RoI transform uses GroundingDINO with detection threshold 0.3, which
filters boxes labeled as the whole image when they cover more than 95% of the image area and uses a
central crop fallback when no valid detection remains.
Prompts
The dataset question is passed to the evaluated MLLM with this prompt:
[Question] Answer this question shortly.
The prompt used to extract key objects for grounding is:
What are the key objects in this question-answer pair?
Q: [Question]
A: [Answer]
Output in json format: {"key_objects": ["key object 1", "key object 2", ...]}
where the placeholders are filled with the original question and the model’s original answer, and the
judge LLM is queried with temperature 0.1. The prompt used for pairwise semantic clustering of
sampled answers is:
Question: [Question] True Answer: [Answer A] Is the following answer correct?
Answer: [Answer B] Respond with ’no’ if this is totally different from the
True Answer. Respond with ’yes’ when they are of the same meaning.
This clustering prompt is filled with the text query and the answer pairs, using a temperature of 0.1.
Causal-Invariant Transform.
We use GroundingDINO as a lightweight RoI generator for the
causal-invariant transform T(·). When no valid RoI is found, we use a central crop as the fallback
view. This choice is not essential to the framework: task-specific or query-conditioned detectors can
be substituted when they are available.
Dataset Configuration.
To keep evaluation consistent and computationally feasible, we fix the
evaluation subsets across all methods. For hallucination-targeted benchmarks, we use the full test sets:
3,000 POPE examples across the random, popular, and adversarial splits, and 2,374 MME examples.
For VQAv2, OKVQA, AdVQA, and GQA, we use the first 2,000 validation examples from each
dataset.
Sampling Budget and Fair Comparison.
For all sampling-based methods, we fix the total decod-
ing budget to Ktotal = 32 sequences per query.
• Baselines: SE, EigenScore, and UMPIRE sample only from the original distribution P(y|v),
so we set K = 32.
• Ours (CIM): CIM samples from both the original distribution P(y|v) and the causally-
filtered distribution P(y|v′). We use K = 16 original-view samples and K′ = 16 filtered-
view samples, keeping K + K′ = 32.
Metric Interpolation.
The K = 16 original-view samples used by CIM can also estimate standard
Semantic Entropy without extra decoding. We therefore report a mixed score Ufinal = λUours,K′=16 +
(1 −λ) · USE,K=16, where Uours is either Usem or UEED. Here, λ is set to 0.05. This score retains
sensitivity to aleatoric uncertainty while adding the epistemic signal measured by CIM.

EED Proxy and Distance Metrics
Figure 9 left validates the connection between EED and Semantic Divergence by grouping samples
according to Usem and plotting the corresponding EED values. The mean EED increases with Semantic
Divergence, supporting EED as an efficient proxy for semantic distribution shift.

KL performs best in our experiments, consistent with its role as an information-gain measure from
the causally-filtered view to the original view.
