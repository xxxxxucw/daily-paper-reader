ABSTRACT
Causal representation learning for time-series data aims to identify latent states
and their causal relations from observations. In this setting, an important chal-
lenge is to model both lagged causal relations across observation intervals and
faster causal effects that appear as instantaneous relations within an interval, while
accounting for nonstationarity in time-series data. However, methods that jointly
handle these causal relations and nonstationarity remain limited. To address this
gap, we establish sufficient conditions for identifying latent states up to component
permutation and component-wise invertible transformations, and their instanta-
neous and lagged causal structures up to the same permutation, using an observed
auxiliary variable, such as time or a condition label, associated with changes in
transition-noise distributions. Based on these results, we propose iCReN, a frame-
work that uses contrastive learning with discrete or continuous auxiliary variables
to learn latent representations and estimate their instantaneous and lagged causal
structures. Experiments demonstrate accurate recovery of latent states and both
instantaneous and lagged causal structures on synthetic data and the utility of the
learned representations for downstream forecasting on real-world data.

INTRODUCTION
Time-series analysis is used for a wide range of tasks (Yue et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2023), includ-
ing weather forecasting (Bi et al., 2023; Lam et al., 2023) and human motion analysis (B¨utepage
et al., 2017; Martinez et al., 2017). Many time-series methods model dependencies among ob-
served variables, while learning the factors underlying the observations and their causal relations
may additionally support generalization under distribution shifts (Sch¨olkopf et al., 2021; Shirahmad
Gale Bagi et al., 2023; Song et al., 2026). When these factors are directly observed, causal discov-
ery methods can be used to infer their causal structure (Shimizu et al., 2006; Zheng et al., 2018;
Runge, 2020), whereas in many applications, the relevant factors are not directly observed but are
instead entangled in observations such as sensor measurements or images. Causal representation
learning (CRL) therefore seeks to identify these factors and their causal relations in learned repre-
sentations (Komanduri et al., 2024). However, different latent representations can produce the same
distribution of observations, so identifying the underlying factors requires additional conditions or
information (Hyv¨arinen & Pajunen, 1999; Khemakhem et al., 2020). Consequently, a central ques-
tion in CRL is under what conditions and with what additional information the underlying factors
and their causal relations become identifiable (Moran & Aragam, 2026).
Two important aspects of CRL for time-series data are modeling the underlying factors as latent
states and their causal relations within and across time steps, and accounting for nonstationarity of
the data distribution. First, causal interactions occurring within an observation interval are repre-
sented as relations among variables at the same time step, termed instantaneous causal relations,
whereas interactions across time steps are represented as lagged causal relations. While earlier CRL
work for time-series data largely focused on lagged causal relations (Yao et al., 2022b;a), recent

work explicitly models both instantaneous and lagged relations (Lippe et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025b;a;
Song et al., 2025). These relations can be represented using structural causal models (SCMs) (Pearl,
2009), where each component of the latent state is modeled as a function of its instantaneous and
lagged parents and exogenous noise (Hyv¨arinen et al., 2010; Peters et al., 2013). Second, the distri-
bution of time-series data can change across seasons, times of day, operating conditions, or exper-
imental conditions; accounting for such nonstationarity is important for time-series modeling (Liu
et al., 2022; Ditzler et al., 2015; Song et al., 2023). Within this formulation, one way to model non-
stationarity is to allow the distribution of the exogenous noise—which we call transition noise—to
vary over time or across conditions. Prior work has used such transition-noise variation (Yao et al.,
2022b) or, more generally, nonstationarity in the transitions between latent states (Yao et al., 2022a;
Song et al., 2023; 2024) to identify latent states and their temporal relations. However, methods that
explicitly model such nonstationarity and use it to identify latent states and their instantaneous and
lagged causal structures remain underexplored.
To address this gap, we propose iCReN, a framework for identifying latent states and their instanta-
neous and lagged causal relations using nonstationarity of transition-noise distributions. We model
nonstationarity through variation in transition-noise distributions associated with an observed auxil-
iary variable, while the structural functions governing transitions between latent states and the map-
ping from latent states to observations remain invariant. Under this setting, our main contributions
are as follows. First, we establish sufficient conditions for identifying latent states and their instanta-
neous and lagged causal structures. Second, we develop iCReN-T and iCReN-G, which instantiate
iCReN using time-contrastive learning (TCL) and generalized contrastive learning (GCL), respec-
tively. Third, we demonstrate accurate recovery of latent states and causal structures on synthetic
data and predictive utility on real-world data.

RELATED WORK
CRL methods for time-series data differ in what causal relations they identify and what informa-
tion they use to do so. While causal discovery methods for time-series data identify instantaneous
and lagged causal relations among directly observed variables under assumptions based on non-
Gaussianity, conditional independence, or regime-switching nonstationarity (Hyv¨arinen et al., 2010;
Runge, 2020; Hulsman et al., 2026), CRL must identify both the latent states underlying the ob-
servations and their causal relations, requiring further assumptions or information. Several CRL
approaches identify latent states and often their lagged causal relations using nonstationarity in
transition-noise or latent transition distributions (Yao et al., 2022b;a; Song et al., 2023; 2024; Chen
et al., 2024) or component-level interventions (Lippe et al., 2022). In particular, similar to our
approach, LEAP (Yao et al., 2022b) exploits variation in transition-noise distributions while assum-
ing invariant structural functions and an invariant observation process, but excludes instantaneous
parents. Among methods that allow instantaneous causal relations, iCITRIS (Lippe et al., 2023) as-
sumes known intervention targets and interventions that remove each targeted variable’s dependence
on its instantaneous causal parents. G-CaRL (Morioka & Hyv¨arinen, 2024) can also be applied to
time-series data with both instantaneous and lagged causal relations; when applied in this temporal
setting, however, it identifies only lagged causal relations. IDOL (Li et al., 2025b) identifies latent
states and their instantaneous and lagged causal relations from stationary time-series data without
auxiliary information, using sufficiently diverse dependencies on past states together with sparsity
of the causal structure. More broadly, prior work has studied how sparse dependencies can support
identification of latent variables (Lachapelle et al., 2022). Our work identifies latent states and both
instantaneous and lagged causal structures when an observed auxiliary variable is associated with
changes in the transition-noise distributions.
The methods above also differ in how representations are learned. LEAP and IDOL use variational
inference with variational autoencoders (VAEs), while iCITRIS also offers a normalizing-flow vari-
ant. In contrast, our identification framework uses contrastive learning without requiring a varia-
tional posterior or decoder. Our approach builds on independent innovation analysis (IIA) (Morioka
et al., 2021), which uses distributional changes associated with an auxiliary variable to identify tran-
sition noise up to a component permutation and component-wise invertible transformations. Its TCL
and GCL variants accommodate discrete and general auxiliary variables, respectively, and identify
the transition-noise components by exploiting sufficient variability in the associated distributional
changes. However, IIA alone identifies neither the latent states nor their causal structure. Under

In this section, we establish sufficient conditions for identifying the latent states and for identifying

extend the results to any finite maximum lag L in Appendix A.5.

transition-noise distributions across auxiliary-variable values, including changes in their mean or

Condition (iii) requires linear independence to prevent cancellation among the relevant cross-time
variation vectors and uses differences in past or future conditional-dependence relations to eliminate
mixing between intimate neighbors. Complete proofs are given in Appendices A.1 and A.2.

IDENTIFICATION OF THE CAUSAL GRAPH
We then establish conditions for identifying the causal graph. For the learned representation, let ˆr be
a learned inverse transition function that estimates transition noise as ˆϵt = ˆr(ˆzt, ˆzt−1). To express
direct causal edges through derivatives, we regard each fi as a function of all candidate instanta-
neous and lagged variables and characterize its parent sets by the variables whose corresponding
partial derivatives are not identically zero. Theorem 2 builds on the latent-component identification
in Theorem 1 and component-wise identification of transition noise to establish a correspondence
between Jacobian entries of ˆr that are not identically zero and direct causal edges.

learned.

We generate synthetic data with nonlinear structural and observation functions defined by three-layer

noise distributions vary discretely across environments or continuously over time. The main com-

We measure latent-state recovery by the mean correlation coefficient (MCC) and graph recovery by
instantaneous and lagged F1 scores. Each ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating better
recovery. Details of data generation and the evaluation protocol are provided in Appendix C.1.
We evaluate iCReN-T and iCReN-G under both transition-noise variation settings. For discrete-
variation data, both use the observed environment labels; for continuous-variation data, iCReN-G

comparable MCC and lagged F1 scores, but its instantaneous F1 is lower on discrete-variation data.

than iCReN-T and that its lower instantaneous F1 on discrete-variation data is partly a finite-sample
effect. Reducing the depth of the observation function from three layers to one increases mean MCC
for every evaluated method, with the largest increases for IDOL and LEAP, which remain below both
proposed methods, as shown in Appendix C.5. Appendix C additionally reports sensitivity analyses
of latent causal structure and latent dimension, along with ablations of the objective components.

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
The following discussion compares our framework with related methods and considers the limi-
tations of our analysis and experiments. Our framework requires an observed auxiliary variable
associated with sufficient variation in transition-noise distributions, but not the intervention-target
labels required by iCITRIS (Lippe et al., 2023). IIA-HMM (Morioka et al., 2021) may offer a route
to relaxing the requirement for an observed auxiliary variable by modeling nonstationarity with a
hidden Markov model. For latent-component identification, our analysis draws on the identification
argument of IDOL (Li et al., 2025b) by combining variation across auxiliary-variable values and
past states; transition-noise identification builds on IIA (Morioka et al., 2021). The full-rank re-
quirement in Condition (ii) does not cover models with instantaneous relations when the transition
noise can be expressed as a sum of functions of the current and past latent states, including linear
transition models. Consistent with this limitation, Appendix C.5 shows lower instantaneous F1 for
linear transitions. Finally, because ground-truth latent states and causal structures are unavailable for
the real-world datasets, we evaluate predictive utility rather than recovery of these quantities. Vali-
9


dating the learned states and causal relations on real-world data would require additional evidence,
such as domain knowledge or interventional information.
We established sufficient conditions for identifying latent states and their instantaneous and lagged
causal structures from time-series observations, using an observed auxiliary variable associated with
changes in transition-noise distributions. Based on these results, we developed iCReN, a contrastive
learning framework that accommodates discrete and continuous auxiliary variables. Experiments
demonstrated accurate recovery of latent states and their causal structures on synthetic data and the
utility of the learned representations for downstream forecasting on real-world data. Our results
show how transition-noise nonstationarity associated with observed auxiliary variables can support
CRL with both instantaneous and lagged causal relations.

This section establishes an isomorphism between the true and learned instantaneous Markov net-

is independent of time and the value of the auxiliary variable:
zt = H(ˆzt),
zt−1 = H(ˆzt−1).
We use nonedges in the learned instantaneous network to derive constraints on JH and combine them
with instantaneous edge-count minimality to establish an isomorphism between the true and learned
instantaneous Markov networks. Let Mz and Mˆz denote the pairwise Markov networks over the
components of zt and ˆzt, respectively, defined by the instantaneous conditional-independence rela-
tions in the true and learned transition distributions, and write their edge sets as
Einst := E(Mz),
bEinst := E(Mˆz).

{ˆzt,k, ˆzt,l} /∈bEinst
⇐⇒
ˆzt,k ⊥ˆzt,l | ˆzt,\{k,l}, ˆzt−1, ut.

A.2
IDENTIFICATION OF LATENT COMPONENTS
This section builds on the local isomorphism between the instantaneous Markov networks estab-
lished in Appendix A.1 to identify the latent components. We first show that any remaining com-
ponent mixing is restricted to intimate neighbors in the instantaneous Markov network. When such
neighbors exist, we eliminate the remaining mixing using cross-time relations and their variation
across auxiliary-variable values. Finally, we show that the resulting local permutations reduce to a
single permutation independent of state and time, yielding component-wise identification. Our ar-
gument draws on the intimate-neighbor proof strategy of IDOL (Li et al., 2025b), while treating the
instantaneous and cross-time relations separately and using changes across values of the auxiliary
variable for the latter.
Throughout this section, suppose that the true and learned representations are related by the same
C2 diffeomorphism H, independent of time and the value of the auxiliary variable, and that As-
sumption 1 holds.

on contiguous sequences, we do not remove individual windows from its training or validation
sequences. At test time, however, we evaluate all methods only on windows satisfying the threshold.
C.1.2
TRAINING CONDITIONS AND BASELINES
Model architecture.
We use the model and objective defined in Appendix B with the dimensions
and auxiliary variables specified for these experiments. The encoder and learned inverse transi-
tion function are five-layer Leaky-ReLU MLPs, and the history feature map is a three-layer Leaky-
ReLU MLP; all hidden widths are 4dz. The main comparison and all additional analyses except the
reconstruction-loss ablation use no decoder or reconstruction loss. iCReN-T classifies 256 discrete
environments using the Gaussian sufficient statistics (ˆϵt,i, ˆϵ2
t,i) for each estimated transition-noise
component, together with the history-feature vector ht and its element-wise square. iCReN-G ap-
plies the same element-wise transformations to the estimated transition noise and history feature.
For discrete-variation data, each coefficient µik(u), the coefficients multiplying the transformed his-
tory features in φ, and the offset in φ are represented by separate parameters for each of the 256
environments. For continuous-variation data, these coefficients and the offset are linear functions of
a Fourier basis consisting of 64 pairs of sine and cosine functions. Its auxiliary-variable-independent
term B(ˆϵt, ht) in equation 34 is implemented as the sum of two MLP outputs, one for ˆϵt and one
for ht, each with hidden width 32. Although the theory assumes C2 mappings, the experimental
implementation uses Leaky-ReLU activations, which are only piecewise smooth. This mismatch
at the activation level can be removed by replacing Leaky-ReLU with a smooth, strictly increasing
activation applied element-wise.

Baselines and their inputs.

Method-specific sequence handling.
For G-CaRL, CtrlNS, and LEAP, we adapt the inputs to the
sequence structure of the default data. G-CaRL receives (xt, xt−1) as current- and past-time groups
through a shared encoder, and we report past-to-current lagged F1. CtrlNS receives no auxiliary
variable; its gating network estimates at most 256 regimes from consecutive learned representations.
The 256 time bins are used only for chunk sampling and are not provided as labels. For LEAP,
we compute the total-correlation loss from the residual components of each transition separately,
rather than jointly across an entire residual sequence. For continuous-variation data, we divide the
single sequence into the 256 pseudo-label bins and present each bin to LEAP as a separate sequence;
consequently, transitions across bin boundaries are excluded.

C.5
SENSITIVITY TO DATA-GENERATING FUNCTION COMPLEXITY
We vary the depth of the true structural or observation function to examine its effect on latent-state
and causal-graph recovery by iCReN-T. We use the default dimensions dx = dz = 5 and the default
instantaneous and lagged graphs. First, we fix the observation function at three layers and vary
the structural-function depth from one to five layers. One layer gives linear structural functions,
whereas two to five layers are Leaky-ReLU MLPs with increasing numbers of hidden layers; all
conditions use the same 10 seeds. As shown in Table 8(a), MCC and both F1 scores remain high
for the nonlinear two- to five-layer structural functions. For the linear functions, instantaneous F1
decreases to 0.275 ± 0.275, whereas MCC and lagged F1 remain high. An additively separable
linear transition cannot satisfy the sufficient-variability rank condition when instantaneous edges
are present (Remark 1). The reduced instantaneous F1 is consistent with this limitation.
Next, we fix the structural functions at three layers and vary the observation-function depth from
one to five layers. Table 8(b) shows that iCReN-T has mean MCC and instantaneous F1 that tend
to decrease as the observation-function depth increases, whereas lagged F1 shows no clear trend.
We also compare iCReN-T, iCReN-G, and the baselines using one- and three-layer observation
functions while keeping the structural functions at three layers. Within each seed, the two settings
share the same latent states, transition noise, environment labels, and time indices. Table 9 shows
higher mean MCC at one layer for every evaluated method, with the largest increases for IDOL and
LEAP; both proposed methods have higher mean MCC than all baselines at both depths.

REAL-WORLD EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
We use iCReN-T in both real-world experiments because it generally achieved higher mean re-
covery scores in the synthetic comparisons.
Its classifier applies the element-wise map a 7→
(a, a2, log(1+a2)) to both the estimated transition noise and the history feature. Inputs are standard-
ized using statistics computed only from the training data, and each method’s pretraining checkpoint
is selected using its method-specific validation objective. After pretraining, each encoder is frozen,
and a forecasting head with the same architecture across methods is trained, selected using val-
idation data, and evaluated on test data. Each random seed defines one complete run, including
representation pretraining, training of the forecasting head, and evaluation.
D.1

Dataset and preprocessing.

including sequences recorded at Slow, Comfortable, and Fast walking speeds. From the three-
dimensional coordinates of markers on the left and right sides of the pelvis, thigh, knee, ankle,
heel, and toe, we construct 36-dimensional sequences. We align the coordinate axes, convert the
coordinates from millimeters to meters, center each frame by subtracting the midpoint of the left

a sampling rate of 12.5 Hz. We split the 30 participants into 19 for training, 6 for validation, and
5 for testing. We estimate the normalization statistics using only sequences recorded at the Slow
and Fast walking speeds from the training participants and apply them unchanged to validation and
test data. We use Slow and Fast for training and model selection and Comfortable only for final test
evaluation.

Downstream forecasting.
After pretraining, we freeze each selected encoder and train the same
two-layer forecasting head with hidden width 64 and Gaussian error linear unit (GELU) activations
for all methods to directly predict the marker coordinates for the following 24 frames (1.92 seconds)
from the latent representations of the preceding 12 frames (0.96 seconds). For downstream learn-
ing, we further divide the 19 training participants into 15 for fitting the forecasting head and 4 for
selecting its checkpoint; both subsets contain only the Slow and Fast walking speeds. We train the
head for at most 100,000 steps using Adam with learning rate 10−3 and batch size 256 and select
the checkpoint with the lowest mean marker error averaged over all 24 prediction horizons on the
four-participant selection subset. We then evaluate the selected encoder and forecasting head on
the Comfortable walking speed from the five test participants without further parameter updates or
model selection.

D.2
WEATHER FORECASTING
Dataset and preprocessing.
We construct regional weather time series from the ERA5 global
climate reanalysis dataset (Hersbach et al., 2020; Copernicus Climate Change Service, 2023) for the
eight regions listed in Table 14. For each of the five cities in a region, we use the nearest 0.25◦grid
point and sample the data at three-hour intervals. At each time point, the encoder input comprises 2-
m temperature (t2m), mean sea-level pressure (msl), 10-m zonal and meridional wind components
(u10 and v10), and total precipitation over the preceding three hours (tp) for all five cities. We
convert the three-hour precipitation totals to millimeters and apply a log1p transformation. We use
only windows consisting of consecutive three-hour observations. Within each month of the 1981–
2013 pretraining period, we divide the days into four periods (1–7, 8–14, 15–21, and 22 onward) and
reserve the final day of each period for validation. The preceding L time points used as context for
these validation windows are also excluded from training and from the computation of normalization
statistics. We standardize each input variable separately for each city using only the remaining 1981–
2013 training samples and apply the resulting statistics unchanged to the pretraining validation and
later downstream data.
Pretraining.
We use a calendar index as the auxiliary variable during pretraining. The index
combines month (12 categories), the four within-month periods defined above, and UTC time (8
three-hour categories), yielding 384 classes. We exclude February 29 and do not include the year,
so the same 384 classes recur across years. Only iCReN and LEAP use this observed auxiliary
variable during pretraining; the other methods receive no calendar label. We pretrain each method
for at most 10,000 steps on data from 1981–2013, use latent dimension 5, and set the maximum lag
to 8 for the dynamic methods, covering the preceding 24 hours at the three-hour sampling interval.
For iCReN, the pretraining objective combines reconstruction and TCL classification losses with
sparsity regularization on the input gates, using λrec = 1, λIIA = 0.005, and λsparsity = 5 × 10−5.
A fixed lower-triangular mask is used for the instantaneous structure, so no DAG penalty contributes
to the objective. Table 15 summarizes the pretraining objectives and auxiliary information.
Downstream forecasting.
After pretraining, we freeze each selected encoder and train the same
two-layer forecasting head with hidden width 256, GELU activations, and dropout 0.1 for all meth-
ods. The head uses the latent representations at the preceding eight time points to predict the relative
humidity of all five cities three hours ahead. We fit the head on data from 2014–2016 for at most
5,000 steps using Adam with learning rate 10−3 and select its checkpoint by standardized MSE
on 2017 validation data. We then evaluate the selected encoder and head on data from 2018–2020
without further parameter updates or model selection. The calendar index is not provided to the
forecasting head.

from 2-m temperature and dew-point temperature and evaluate the three-hour-ahead forecasts using
