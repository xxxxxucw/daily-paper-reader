ABSTRACT
Tabular foundation models (TFMs) achieve strong predictive performance through
in-context learning, yet repeatedly conditioning on labeled data makes inference
expensive. Knowledge distillation can reduce this cost by transferring their pre-
dictive ability to lightweight, dataset-specific students. However, the dependence
of TFM predictions on both a labeled context and a query introduces two design
questions: how to construct teacher supervision and whether expanding query
coverage improves distillation. We examine these questions across two TFMs
and both neural and tree-based students, and derive an effective distillation recipe.
The recipe uses the full labeled training set as teacher context and trains students
solely on teacher predictions for observed and synthetic queries. On TabArena, the
resulting students outperform their supervised trained tuned-and-ensembled coun-
terparts by 57–98 Elo points. Applied unchanged to TALENT, the same recipe im-
proves matched default students on 236–258 of 300 datasets and reduces median
primary error by 4.0–6.4%. The distilled students also achieve median inference
speedups of 3.0–21.6× over their teachers, offering a practical trade-off between
predictive performance and repeated inference cost. Code is available at link.

INTRODUCTION
Tabular machine learning has been dominated by supervised methods such as tree models (Chen &
Guestrin, 2016; Ke et al., 2017; Prokhorenkova et al., 2018) and multi-layer perceptrons (Holzmüller
et al., 2024; Gorishniy et al., 2025; 2026). These methods support fast inference once trained but
require dataset-specific fitting and often extensive hyperparameter tuning. Recently, tabular founda-
tion models (TFMs) (Hollmann et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2025; 2026) have changed
this paradigm with strong out-of-the-box performance without per-dataset updates. The central idea
of TFMs is in-context learning, which enables zero-shot inference by conditioning predictions on
labeled samples in the new dataset, not on the training samples observed earlier.
However, the in-context learning mechanism of TFMs does not eliminate the computational costs
but rather shifts them from training to inference because the context must be processed every time a
prediction is made. Repeated TFM inference is therefore much more costly than using a trained su-
pervised method. Several recent works reduce this burden through context selection (Thomas et al.,
2024; Liu & Ye, 2025), routing queries to specialized local contexts (Xu et al., 2025), compressing
contexts into latent representations (Zabërgja et al., 2026), or caching reusable context representa-
tions (Grinsztajn et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2026). These approaches improve the overall efficiency of
inference but retain context-dependent computation at serving time.
Knowledge distillation (KD) (Bucilu˘a et al., 2006; Hinton et al., 2015) gives a complementary ap-
proach that transfers the predictive ability of a TFM to a lightweight, dataset-specific student. Once
created, a distilled student supports instant inference as many times as we want, saving huge compu-
tational costs. However, because of the unique properties of TFMs as in-context learners, distilling
a TFM introduces two key essential design choices: (i) supervision construction asks how teacher
supervision should be formed from training data, while (ii) query coverage asks whether and how
expanding teacher queries improves distillation.
Supervision construction. What we distill from a TFM to a student is not its full capacity; we distill
its predictive power conditioned on a specific labeled context. For every labeled observation in the
training data, we can either (i) include it in the context to make the TFM’s predictions more focused

synthetic queries. Our distilled students with single default student configurations outperform tuned-
and-ensembled students on TabArena (Erickson et al., 2025) by 57–98 Elo points. Moreover, the
same recipe improves students across binary classification, multiclass classification, and regression
on 300 TALENT datasets (Liu et al., 2025). The distilled students trade a one-time distillation cost
and some accuracy relative to their teachers for faster repeated inference, achieving median speedups
of 3.0–21.6×, with the inference advantage growing as the teacher context size increases.

RELATED WORK
Tabular foundation models.
Prior-data fitted networkss (PFNs) learn to approximate posterior
predictions by pretraining over synthetic tasks and conditioning on labeled samples at inference
time (Müller et al., 2022). TabPFN introduced this paradigm to tabular prediction (Hollmann et al.,
2023; 2025), and recent TFMs have substantially improved predictive performance and scalability
(Qu et al., 2025; 2026; Grinsztajn et al., 2025; 2026; Cho et al., 2026). Unlike supervised models,
which fit a separate predictor to each dataset, they adapt through in-context learning: predictions
are created by conditioning on labeled examples from the target dataset without dataset-specific
parameter updates. This enables strong out-of-the-box prediction, but it also means that inference
continues to depend on the labeled context even after the model is fully deployed.

Improving context efficiency.
The context dependence of TFMs has motivated several ap-
proaches to improve efficiency. One direction improves the efficiency of the context itself. Lo-
CalPFN constructs local contexts through nearest-neighbor retrieval (Thomas et al., 2024), while
MixturePFN routes queries to specialized experts with cluster-specific contexts (Xu et al., 2025).
BETA combines lightweight encoder adaptation with bootstrapped sampling to scale contextual
prediction (Liu & Ye, 2025). TACO jointly trains a context compressor and a predictor, enabling
predictions to condition on a compact latent representation of the training data (Zabërgja et al.,
2026). TabPFN-2.5 and TabICLv2 accelerate repeated inference by caching reusable context rep-
resentations (Grinsztajn et al., 2025; Qu et al., 2026). These approaches reduce inference cost, but
predictions still depend on context examples or their cached representations at serving time.
Knowledge distillation of TFMs.
A complementary direction transfers the information of a TFM
into a dataset-specific predictor that can be served independently. MotherNet generates the weights
of a compact neural network from a dataset (Müller et al., 2025), while knowledge distillation
trains a lightweight student from teacher predictions. Classical distillation may combine teacher
predictions with ground-truth labels (Bucilu˘a et al., 2006; Hinton et al., 2015). For tabular models,
FAST-DAD distills ensemble teachers into individual predictors using observed labels together with
teacher predictions on synthetic inputs generated by Gibbs sampling. (Fakoor et al., 2020). Re-
cent TFM distillation methods likewise instantiate particular choices of teacher supervision: Pocket
Foundation Models combines out-of-fold teacher predictions with direct label supervision (Tanna
et al., 2026). In concurrent work, GEAR combines distillation on synthetic samples with sophis-
cated generators and that combines direct label supervision and out-of-fold teacher supervision (Qin
et al., 2026).

DESIGNING TFM DISTILLATION
We study two design choices for distilling a pretrained TFM into a lightweight, dataset-specific
student. The first, supervision construction, concerns how teacher predictions are generated for
training datas and whether they are combined with direct label supervision. The second, query
coverage, concerns whether and how querying additional inputs improves distillation.
Experimental setup. Our design studies use 51 TabArena datasets with three official outer splits.
We consider all four teacher–student pairs formed by two strong TFM teachers, TabICLv2 (Qu et al.,
2026) and TabPFN v3 (Grinsztajn et al., 2026), and two structurally distinct students, the neural
model TabM (Gorishniy et al., 2025) and the tree-based model XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin, 2016).
Our experiments build on the official TabArena training and evaluation pipeline, retaining its default
student configurations, preprocessing, and protocol. Configurations are compared using aggregate
TabArena Elo, calibrated jointly within each comparison. Further details appear in Appendix A.1.

One simple way to make fuller use of the teacher is to expand query coverage beyond the real
training rows. By querying synthetic inputs, we can obtain additional supervision without collecting
new labels. We ask whether this expansion improves distillation when the queries are generated by
simple perturbations or recombinations of real sample’s features. For each synthetic input ˜x, we

Four simple query generators.
We evaluate four task-agnostic, non-parametric generators at a
common budget of one synthetic query per real row. CUTMIX independently selects each feature
from one of two sampled rows with equal probability. MIXUP interpolates numerical features be-
tween two rows using feature-wise mixing coefficients, while categorical features select between the
corresponding values. FULLMIX independently draws each feature from a randomly selected real
row. PERTURB adds standard-deviation-scaled Gaussian noise to numerical features and resamples
categorical values from their empirical marginals.

Final recipe.
Together, these studies define a single distillation recipe that we fix for all sub-
sequent experiments. We use the full labeled training set as teacher context and train the student
solely on teacher predictions and CUTMIX synthetic queries at a 2:1 synthetic-to-real ratio.

EVALUATION
Section 3 fixes a single recipe for TFM distillation. We now evaluate this recipe on TabArena (Er-
ickson et al., 2025) and TALENT (Liu et al., 2025).
Experimental setup.
As in Section 3, we use TabICLv2 (Qu et al., 2026) and TabPFN v3 (Grin-
sztajn et al., 2026) as teachers, and TabM (Gorishniy et al., 2025) and XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin,
2016) as students. For KD, we’ll use the selected recipe in Section 3 without further reselection and
use the default student configurations throughout. On TabArena (Erickson et al., 2025), we evaluate
on all 51 datasets under the official benchmark protocol. We compute aggregate TabArena scores
jointly over the 72 official baseline configurations and our four KD configurations, using default
student configurations and a 2:1 synthetic-to-observed query ratio (r = 2). On TALENT (Liu et al.,
2025), we evaluate on all 300 datasets using the provided train/validation/test splits and five training
seeds. We measure error by 1 −ROC-AUC for binary classification, log loss for multiclass clas-
sification, and RMSE for regression. For each dataset, we first average errors over splits or seeds
and then compute matched relative-error reductions. Aggregate results weight datasets equally, with
uncertainty estimated by paired dataset-bootstrap 95% confidence intervals. Appendix A.2 provides
full protocols, statistical details, and additional analyses.
Baselines.
On both benchmarks, we compare KD against architecture-matched default students
trained directly without distillation. On TabArena, we additionally compare against tuned and tuned-
and-ensembled versions of the same students, all 15 conventional supervised model families in the
benchmark pool, including RealMLP (Holzmüller et al., 2024), LightGBM (Ke et al., 2017), and
CatBoost (Prokhorenkova et al., 2018), as well as the teacher models.
4.1
DISTILLATION IMPROVES THE SAME STUDENT BEYOND DIRECT OPTIMIZATION
KD outperforms tuned-and-ensembled direct training for both student architectures. Across the four
teacher–student pairs, distilled students gain 56.7–97.9 Elo over the matched tuned ensemble, with
all paired 95% confidence intervals above zero. They also win on 33–37 of 51 datasets, with median
relative-error reductions of 1.30–1.91% and positive confidence intervals for every pair.
The distilled students also rank strongly against the broader TabArena pool. All four outperform
the strongest configuration of 14 of the 15 conventional supervised model families. RealMLP is the
only exception, although TabPFN v3–TabM also exceeds its tuned and ensembled variant. Table 2
reports selected baselines; the full comparison is in Appendix B.
4.2
TEACHER ADVANTAGE TRANSFERS THROUGH KD
To assess whether KD actually transfers the teacher’s predictive advantage, we measure how much
of the teacher–student error gap is recovered by the distilled student and whether larger teacher
advantages translate into larger KD gains. Across datasets where the teacher reduces raw-student
error by more than 1%, KD recovers a median 67.9–70.9% of the teacher–student error gap (Table 3).
Thus, when the TFM is meaningfully stronger than the student, roughly two-thirds of its predictive
advantage is retained in the lightweight student.

ONE-TIME DISTILLATION COST VS. INFERENCE EFFICIENCY
We finally examine whether the one-time cost of distillation can be amortized through faster repeated
inference, and in which serving regimes this trade-off is most favorable. Table 4 quantifies the
deployment trade-off across all 51 TabArena datasets using 32-row requests on the same H200
host with 32 pinned CPU cores. Serving measurements start from resident models and exclude
initial loading, whereas offline cost includes teacher-target generation, student fitting, and associated
overhead (Appendix A.3). Across the four teacher–student pairs, dataset-paired median inference
speedups range from 3.0 to 21.6×, while the distilled students incur median relative primary-error
increases of 1.72–2.76% and median one-time costs of 48.0–192.4 seconds.

EVALUATION ON TALENT
We use TALENT as an external validation benchmark to test whether the fixed distillation recipe
could be generalized. We apply the recipe unchanged to all 300 TALENT datasets. Across all four
teacher–student pairs, KD improves over supevised trained students on 236–258 datasets, reducing
median primary error by 4.02–6.37%, with all paired 95% confidence intervals above zero.
The improvement is not concentrated in a particular task. Across binary classification, multiclass
classification, and regression, all 12 teacher–student–task combinations show positive median im-
provements with 95% confidence intervals above zero. Together, these results show that the fixed
recipe transfers across a substantially broader collection of datasets without adapting new supervi-
sion design.
5
CONCLUSION
We studied supervision construction and query coverage as two key design choices for distilling
TFMs into lightweight, dataset-specific students. Across all four teacher–student pairs, training
solely on full-context teacher predictions achieves the best aggregate performance among the tested
supervision configurations. All four simple, task-agnostic query generators further improve aggre-
gate performance for every pair, showing that useful teacher supervision can extend beyond observed
training rows.
These findings yield a fixed recipe that trains students solely on full-context teacher predictions
for observed and synthetic queries. With default student configurations, this recipe outperforms
architecture-matched tuned-and-ensembled students on TabArena and improves matched default
students on TALENT without reselecting its target-generation settings. Predictive gains vary across

labels. The distilled students can be served independently of the labeled context, trading a one-time

EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
A.1
SECTION 3 DETAILS
Training protocol.
We use TabArena pinned to commit eb5bc081 (16 June 2026), and its 72-
method official comparison preset. Section 3 use TabArena splits 0–2 on 51 datasets with eight-fold
student bagging. Full-context targets use all fitting rows of each bagging member as teacher context;
validation and test labels are excluded. OOF targets use up to eight inner folds within that fitting set.
Implementation.
We retain benchmark-default students and preprocessing, use eight teacher es-
timators, and apply no additional distillation temperature scaling. Classification uses soft cross-
entropy and regression uses squared error to teacher predictions. TabM and XGBoost balance ob-
served and synthetic queries through the sum of their group-mean losses.
Synthetic queries.
The operator comparison uses one synthetic query per observed row. Cut-
Mix replaces each feature with probability 0.5; Mixup uses independent feature-wise Unif(0, 1)
coefficients. Perturb uses numerical noise scaled by the column standard deviation and categorical
replacement probability 0.5; FullMix samples each feature independently from observed rows. All
source rows are sampled from the fitting set, and synthetic targets come only from the teacher. The
TabPFN v3–XGBoost budget comparison uses 152 matched dataset–split pairs because one r = 4
result is unavailable; the other curves use 153.
A.2
SECTION 4 DETAILS
Evaluation protocol.
TabArena evaluation covers all 816 official dataset–split pairs: nine splits
for 34 datasets and 30 for 17, including design splits 0–2. Appendix C repeats the analyses in Sec-
tions 4.1 and 4.2 after excluding design splits 0–2. TALENT uses its fixed train/validation/test par-
titions and seeds 0–4, with training-only teacher context and no outer bagging or seed ensembling.
The released TALENT adapter uses the same eb5bc081 dependency with a TabM compatibility
layer. The official tuned TabArena baselines select from one default and 200 random configurations;
tuned ensembles use 40 ensemble-selection iterations.
Statistics.
We average errors over matched splits or seeds before computing dataset-level relative
gains, then report their median. Paired bootstrap intervals resample datasets, using 20,000 replicates
with joint recalibration of all 76 configurations for Section 4 Elo contrasts and 10,000 for TALENT
median gains. TALENT relative gains exclude reference errors at most 10−12, leaving 297 datasets;
win/tie/loss counts use all 300, with absolute differences at most 10−12 treated as ties. Each dataset
receives equal weight.
A.3
TIMING DETAILS
Serving measurements.
We measure all 51 TabArena datasets at outer split 0 on one H200 host
with 32 pinned CPU cores. The reported latencies use resident models, 32-row requests, and teacher
context recomputation without persistent KV caching. After three warm-up calls, we record 7–30
GPU-synchronized calls, targeting two seconds of measured time, and take the median latency per
dataset. Initial model loading is excluded.
Cost aggregation.
Offline cost includes teacher-target generation, student fitting, and training
overhead. Speedups and break-even request counts are computed per dataset before taking me-
dians, retaining infinite break-even values when the student is no faster. In Figure 4, generation time
is the mean recorded cold target-generation cost across completed splits, excluding student fitting.
