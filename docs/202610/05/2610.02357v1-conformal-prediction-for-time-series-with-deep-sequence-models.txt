# Conformal Prediction for Time Series with Deep Sequence Models

Abstract 

Recent advances in deep learning for time series prediction have amplified the need for reli-able uncertainty quantification. Conformal prediction has gained attention as a distribution-free framework for constructing prediction intervals with coverage guarantees. However, its coverage guarantees rely on data exchangeability, an assumption generally violated in time series. Active research has focused on developing conformal prediction methods for time series that overcome this limitation. While deep sequence models, such as recurrent neural networks and Transform-ers, have often been used in conformal prediction for time series, limited work has systematically studied how deep sequence models can be utilized in conformal prediction for time series. In this work, we systematically investigate the use of deep sequence models in conformal prediction for time series through three approaches: conditional quantile regression, conditional quantile function estimation, and localized conformal prediction. We provide a theoretical analysis es-tablishing asymptotic conditional coverage guarantees for all three approaches under suitable assumptions. Through comprehensive experiments on real-world datasets, we demonstrate the effectiveness of leveraging deep sequence models into conformal prediction for time series. 

## 1 Introduction 

Time series prediction aims to forecast future outcomes from past sequential observations of features and outcomes [1], and has been widely applied across a broad range of downstream tasks, such as climate modeling, biological science, and finance [2]. Recent advances in deep learning have led to the development of various foundation models for time series prediction [3, 4, 5, 6]. The growing use of such foundation models highlights the need for reliable uncertainty quantification in time series prediction. Conformal prediction has been actively adopted in time series prediction as a reliable and distribution-free framework for uncertainty quantification, constructing prediction sets with desired coverage guarantees [7]. Conformal prediction provides prediction intervals with coverage guarantees under the assump-tion that the data are exchangeable, without requiring additional distributional assumptions [8, 9].

Consequently, substantial research has focused on extending conformal prediction to time series by developing methods that account for temporal dependence and the resulting violations of exchangeability [10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. Within this line of research, deep sequence models, such as recurrent neural networks [15, 16, 17] and Transform-ers [18], have been increasingly adopted due to their ability to capture complex temporal dependen-cies [19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. However, a systematic understanding of how deep sequence models can be leveraged and their practical effectiveness in conformal prediction for time series remains limited. To address this gap, we make the following contributions: 

• We propose three methods that leverage deep sequence models for conformal prediction for time series: conditional quantile regression, conditional quantile function estimation, and localized conformal prediction. 

• We establish asymptotic conditional coverage guarantees for all three methods under suitable assumptions. 

• We conduct comprehensive experiments on real-world datasets, demonstrating the competitive performance of the proposed methods and providing practical insights into their use. 21.1 Related Works 

Conformal prediction beyond the exchangeability assumption. A line of work on confor-mal prediction under distribution shift focuses on adaptively updating the calibration procedure online. Gibbs and Candes [25] address distribution shifts in sequential data by adaptively updat-ing a coverage level based on past coverage errors, and Zaffran et al. [26] extend this framework to time series by proposing an aggregation-based approach to improve its adaptivity. More recent work along this line includes Bhatnagar et al. [27], Angelopoulos et al. [28, 29], Gibbs and Cand` es [30], Areces et al. [31]. Another line of work on conformal prediction under distribution shift be-tween the calibration and test set is by reweighting the data points. Tibshirani et al. [32] address covariate shift by reweighting calibration samples according to the likelihood ratio between the test and training covariate distributions, requiring this ratio to be known or accurately estimated. Bar-ber et al. [33] uses predefined weights, such as exponentially decaying weights that assign greater importance to more recent observations. However, determining appropriate weights remains chal-lenging when the underlying shift is unknown, and inaccurate weight estimates or poorly chosen weighting schemes may lead to coverage degradation or inefficient prediction intervals. Although not specifically designed to address non-exchangeability, localized conformal prediction [34] uses similarity-based weights to emphasize calibration samples near the test point and adapt prediction intervals to local distributional characteristics. 

Conformal prediction for time series. Early approaches combine ensemble-based predictors with sequential conformal calibration. Xu and Xie [10, 11] use bootstrap ensembles with a rolling window of prediction residuals, and Jensen et al. [35] combine ensemble quantile regression with conformal calibration to adapt prediction interval widths. Related to the idea of localized conformal prediction [34], a growing body of work reweights calibration observations based on their relevance to the current prediction. Auer et al. [12] use modern Hopfield networks to assign greater weight to ob-servations from similar temporal regimes, and Lee et al. [13] employ a reweighted Nadaraya–Watson estimator to obtain data-adaptive weights for quantile estimation of prediction residuals. Chen et al. [20] use deep sequence models to learn latent representations for attention-based reweighting of cal-ibration samples. Neglia et al. [23] use similarities between reservoir states to reweight historical residuals, capturing local temporal dynamics without training a calibration network. Another important body of work estimates conditional quantiles of future prediction residuals given historical observations. Xu and Xie [36] use quantile random forests to exploit dependencies among residuals, and Lee et al. [19] extend this approach using a Transformer to leverage the past residuals and covariates. Cini et al. [22] combine sequence models with graph neural networks to estimate residual quantiles across correlated time series while learning the relational structure from data. Menadjiev et al. [14] introduce DistMatch, which groups residual histories into distributionally similar bins using Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistics and performs bin-specific quantile estimation. 31.2 Problem Setup

## 2 Methods 

In this section, we propose three approaches to leverage deep sequence models in conformal prediction for time series. Figure 1 provides an overview of the three proposed methods. The three methods use a sequence encoder but use its encoded representation in different ways: as a finite collection of quantile heads, a head that also takes the quantile level as input, or kernel weights for observed calibration residuals. Given the context zt at time t, an encoder Enc θ′ (·) maps zt to a latent representation ht that summarizes the relevant contextual information, as ht = Enc θ′ (zt), where θ′

denotes the parameters of the encoder. We write Ht = Enc θ′ (Zt) for the corresponding random representation. 42.1 Conditional Quantile Regression using Deep Sequence Models 

Conditional quantile regression with deep sequence models has been explored in probabilistic time series forecasting [38, 39, 40]. Building on this idea, a straightforward way to leverage deep sequence models in conformal prediction for time series is to model the conditional quantiles of prediction residuals given their historical context. Specifically, a deep sequence model encodes the historical time series context into a latent representation, which is then used as input to multiple prediction heads, one for each target quantile level. The resulting quantile estimates are used to construct prediction intervals. Let T = {τ1 < . . . < τ J } ⊂ (0 , 1) denote the set of quantile levels at which the conditional quantiles of the prediction residuals are estimated. We assume that the number of quantile levels 

J is fixed throughout training and inference. For notational simplicity, we omit the subscript when referring to a generic quantile level τ ∈ T . In an unconstrained multi-head parameterization, for each τ ∈ T the corresponding quantile-specific prediction head gθτ (·) takes ht as input and produces an estimate of the conditional τ -quantile of the prediction residual bqT,τ , given by 

bqT,τ (zt) = gθτ (ht) = gθτ (Enc θ′ (zt)) ,

where θτ denotes the parameters of the prediction head associated with quantile level τ .Rather than employing a separate encoder for each quantile level in T , a common architec-ture uses a single shared encoder together with multiple quantile-specific prediction heads [38, 40]. Transformer [18] and recurrent neural networks [16, 17] are common choices for the encoder. Although the model is trained using quantile loss, quantile crossing may occur due to estima-tion, approximation, or optimization errors [41]. To prevent quantile crossing, we parameterize the prediction heads using nonnegative increments. The head corresponding to the lowest quantile level predicts its quantile value directly, while each subsequent head produces a nonnegative increment that is added to the preceding quantile estimate. Specifically, we define 

bqT,τ 1 (zt) = gθτ1 (ht),

bqT,τ j (zt) = bqT,τ j−1 (zt) + σ(gθτj (ht)) , j = 2 , . . . , J, 

where σ : R → [0 , ∞) is a nonnegative activation function, such as ReLU or softplus. This construc-tion guarantees 

bqT,τ 1 (zt) ≤ · · · ≤ bqT,τ J (zt),

for every context zt. We refer to this conditional quantile regression approach using a deep sequence encoder as DSCP-QR throughout the paper. 

2.2 Conditional Quantile Function Estimation using Deep Sequence Mod-els 

Conditional quantile regression with deep sequence models provides a direct way to estimate pre-diction residual quantiles given historical context. However, the multi-head formulation requires a 5separate prediction head for each target quantile level, restricting estimation to a predefined set of levels. To address this limitation, we consider conditional quantile function estimation, in which the quantile level is provided as an additional model input alongside the encoded historical con-text. This allows a single prediction head to estimate arbitrary quantile levels. Similar formulations have been explored in distributional reinforcement learning [42, 43], uncertainty estimation [44], and probabilistic time series forecasting [45]. Rather than specifying a finite set of target quantile levels, we allow the quantile level τ to vary continuously over (0 , 1). The prediction head combines ht with the requested scalar level τ to estimate the corresponding conditional quantile of the prediction residual: 

bqT,τ (zt) = gθ′′ (ht, τ ) = gθ′′ (Enc θ′ (zt), τ ) ,

where θ′′ denotes the prediction-head parameters. Unlike the finite-head approach, this construction does not restrict the endpoints to quantile levels specified during model construction. By varying the requested levels, the same trained model can produce intervals with different nominal coverage levels and different allocations of nominal miscoverage between the lower and upper tails, without additional prediction heads or retraining. Similar to conditional quantile regression, quantile crossing may occur when estimating quantiles using the learned conditional quantile function. However, the nonnegative-increment parameteri-zation used in § 2.1 is not applicable here, since the conditional quantile function is designed to estimate quantiles at arbitrary levels rather than at a finite set of prespecified quantile levels. To prevent quantile crossing, we therefore construct the prediction head as a neural network that is partially monotonic with respect to the quantile level τ , while remaining unrestricted with respect to the context representation ht. Specifically, the prediction head is constrained to satisfy 

gθ′′ (ht, τ 1) ≤ gθ′′ (ht, τ 2), for any τ1 < τ 2.

Consequently, the estimated conditional quantile function is nondecreasing in the quantile level, thereby preventing quantile crossing. The details of the partially monotonic prediction head are provided in Appendix B.3. We refer to this conditional quantile function estimation approach using a deep sequence encoder as DSCP-QFE throughout the paper. 

2.3 Localized Conformal Prediction using Deep Sequence Models 

Localized conformal prediction [34] assigns greater weight to calibration samples that are similar to the test sample, rather than weighting all calibration samples equally. Building on this idea for time series prediction setup, we use a sequence model to encode historical contexts into latent representations and compute similarity-based calibration weights in the learned representation space. These weights are then used to estimate the conditional cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the prediction residuals. We train the encoder using quantile losses at multiple fixed quantile levels. This encourages the learned representation to retain information about the context for conditional distribution of 6the residuals. Consequently, contexts with similar conditional distribution are expected to exhibit similar learned representations, making distances in the representation space informative for identi-fying relevant calibration samples. The resulting representations therefore can be used to construct similarity-based calibration weights, assigning greater weight to calibration contexts that are closer to the test context in the learned representation space. For this method, the latent representation serves as a learned state variable for local calibration. ## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Setup 

Datasets. We evaluate the proposed methods and baselines on three real-world datasets that are commonly used as benchmarks in conformal prediction for time-series. The Air Quality dataset [46] contains air quality measurements and 10 associated features collected from 12 locations in Beijing, China. The Solar dataset [47] contains half-hourly measurements of solar radiation along with eight environmental features at 50 different locations. The Sapflow dataset, obtained from the SAPFLUXNET database [48], contains measurements of water movement within plants. Since the individual sequences in the Sapflow dataset vary in length, we use a subset of 24 time series, each containing between 15,000 and 20,000 observations with varying sampling frequencies, following Auer et al. [12]. Additional details of the datasets are provided in Appendix B.1. 

Base predictors. We consider three base predictors: linear regression, a recurrent neural network (RNN), and Chronos [49, 50]. For linear regression, we use a sliding window of the past k time steps of features and outcomes to predict the outcome at the next time step, and train a separate model for each individual time-series sequence. For the RNN, we use LSTM cells to perform the same one-step-ahead prediction using the past k time steps of features and outcomes. Unlike linear regression, a single RNN model is trained jointly using the training splits of all time series sequences. Since Chronos is a pretrained foundation model for time series forecasting, we use its zero-shot forecasts without fine tuning. Proposed and baseline methods. For the three proposed methods, we consider LSTM and Transformer as the sequence encoder. Based on the literature review in § 1.1, we consider several representative conformal prediction methods as baseline methods, with an emphasis on methods developed for time series or distribution shift: NexCP [33], SPCI [36], HopCPT [12], KOWCPI [13], ResCP [23], and DistMatch [14]. For each time-series sequence, the first 33% is used as the training set. The first 67% of the remaining sequence are used for calibration, and the rest are used for testing, resulting in an approximate 33:45:22 split. The training set is used to train the base predictor, the calibration set is used to calibrate the prediction intervals, and the test set is used for evaluation. We consider a one-step-ahead prediction setting where the prediction interval for time 

t+1 is constructed using the historical context at time t. Implementation details and hyperparameter 11 selection procedures for the proposed methods and baselines are provided in Appendices B.3 and B.4, respectively. 

Evaluation metrics. We evaluate all methods using three metrics: coverage gap, prediction interval width, and Winkler score [51]. The coverage gap measures the difference between empirical and target coverage. Prediction interval width measures the size of the prediction interval. The Winkler score jointly accounts for interval width and coverage violations. Each metric is computed over the test set and then averaged across sequences within each dataset. Detailed definitions are provided in Appendix B.5. 

4.2 Results 

Deep sequence models provide efficient prediction intervals for time series. Table 1 reports the average coverage gap, interval width, and Winkler score for all methods across three datasets at a target coverage level of 0 .9. The results for Split CP and NexCP suggest that relying on exchangeability or simply assigning greater weights to recent observations may be insufficient to construct efficient prediction intervals for time series. For each proposed method, we report the best results obtained using either an RNN or a Transformer encoder. DSCP-Local achieves the best overall performance while maintaining stable coverage across all three datasets. DSCP-QR also performs well, often outperforming the strongest baselines, although it occasionally exhibits undercoverage. Similar trends hold at higher and lower target coverage levels of 0 .95 and 0 .8, as shown in Tables 15 and 16, respectively. We observe that the RNN encoder generally outperforms the Transformer encoder in our ex-periments. One possible explanation is that perhaps relatively short-term temporal dependencies are sufficient for modeling the conditional residual distributions in these settings, although further analysis is needed to assess this hypothesis. DSCP-QFE exhibits undercoverage, possibly because its partially monotonic prediction heads limit model expressiveness. The ablation study below, in which this architectural constraint is removed, provides empirical support for this interpretation. 

The effect of architectural constraints for preventing quantile crossing on prediction interval efficiency. Table 17 reports ablation results for DSCP-QR and DSCP-QFE without their respective architectural constraints for preventing quantile crossing. For DSCP-QR, removing the incremental prediction heads has marginal effect on performance but leads to quantile crossing. In contrast, removing the partially monotonic heads substantially improves the performance of DSCP-QFE. 

## 5 Conclusion 

In this study, we propose three methods that leverage deep sequence models for conformal prediction in time series. We establish asymptotic conditional coverage guarantees for all three methods un-der suitable assumptions. Experiments on three real-world datasets against a broad set of baseline 12 methods demonstrate the effectiveness of deep sequence models for conformal prediction for time series. We believe that our theoretical analysis and empirical findings provide useful insights and practical guidance for future research. Future work should address the limitations of this study by developing experimental protocols to assess conditional coverage empirically and designing architec-tures that prevent quantile crossing without compromising prediction interval efficiency. Another promising direction is to develop novel methods that more effectively leverage deep sequence models for time-series conformal prediction.

## B Additional Details of Experiments 

B.1 Datasets 

We evaluate the proposed methods and baselines on three real-world datasets that are commonly used as benchmarks in conformal prediction for time series. This section provides details of the datasets, and Table 2 summarizes their key characteristics. 

Air quality dataset. The Air Quality dataset [46] comprises 35,064 air quality measurements collected from 12 locations in Beijing, China. It includes two air quality variables, PM10 and PM2.5. We use PM10 as the outcome variable. PM2.5 is excluded from the feature set. The dataset also contains ten additional environmental variables that are used as features. This dataset has also been used in prior work on conformal prediction for time series [12, 23]. 

Solar dataset. The Solar dataset is obtained from the U.S. National Solar Radiation Database [47] and contains half-hourly measurements of solar radiation, along with eight environmental features, at 50 locations. We use diffuse horizontal irradiance (DHI) as the outcome variable and the eight environmental variables as input features. We consider three years of data and resample the original half-hourly measurements to an hourly frequency. This results in 50 time series, each consisting of 26,304 hourly observations resampled from 52,609 half-hourly measurements. This dataset has also been used in prior works [10, 11, 12, 23]. 

Sapflow dataset. Sap flow refers to the movement of water within a plant and is commonly used as a proxy for plant transpiration in environmental science. The Sapflow dataset is obtained from the SAPFLUXNET database [48]. Following Auer et al. [12], we use a subset of 24 time series, each containing between 15,000 and 20,000 observations. The sequences have varying sampling frequencies, and each sequence contains 10 features. 39 Table 2: Summary statistics of the three real-world datasets, including the number of features, sequence length, and number of time series.

B.2 Base predictors 

Linear regression. We use LinearRegressionModel from the Python Darts [60] library to im-plement the linear regression base predictor. Each time series sequence is chronologically split into training and held-out sets according to the specified training ratio. A separate model is trained for each sequence. Predictions are made sequentially using a rolling window. At each prediction step, the model uses the most recent past window outcomes and covariates as input features to predict the next prediction step outcomes. The window is then advanced by prediction step time steps, incorporating the new true outcomes and covariates to make the next prediction. Covariates at the forecasting timestamps are not provided. prediction step corresponds to output chunk length 

hyperparameter in LinearRegressionModel . If prediction step > 1, Darts fits a separate linear regressor for each prediction step. Training is conducted by minimizing the mean squared error between the predicted and true outcomes on the training set. After training, the model forecasts the held-out set of the sequence. We make the true covariates and outcomes available for use as windowed features when forecasting the held-out set. MSE and MAE are computed over each se-quence’s held-out set and then averaged across sequences. In all experiments, we set past window 

to 100 and prediction step to 1. Table 3 reports the MAE and RMSE of the linear regression base predictor on all datasets. 

Recurrent neural networks. The recurrent neural network (RNN) base predictor is implemented as a global model trained jointly across all time series sequences in each dataset. Each time series sequence is chronologically split into training and held-out sets according to the specified training ratio, with the last 10% of the training set is used as the validation set for model selection. The covariates and outcomes are then normalized using the training sets across all sequences in the dataset. Predictions are made sequentially using a rolling window. At each prediction step, the model uses the most recent past window outcomes and covariates as input features to predict the next prediction step outcomes. The window is then advanced by prediction step time steps, incorporating the new true outcomes and covariates to make the next prediction. Covariates at the forecasting timestamps are not provided. The model consists of a linear input embedding layer, followed by a multi-layer LSTM [16], and a final fully connected layer that maps the hidden representation to the predicted outcomes. During training, the model is trained by minimizing the mean squared error using the Adam optimizer [61]. After training, the model forecasts the held-out set of each sequence, and the predictions are transformed back to the original scale. DSCP-QR DSCP-QR consists of a linear projection layer that maps the input to the model dimension, a backbone sequence encoder, and quantile-specific prediction heads. Each head gθτj is a fully connected neural network with one or more hidden layers and ReLU activations. To prevent quantile crossing across the ordered target levels 0 < τ 1 < · · · < τ J < 1, we parameterize successive quantiles using nonnegative increments: ˆqT,τ 1 (zt) = gθτ1 (ht),

ˆqT,τ j (zt) = ˆ qT,τ j−1 + σ(gθτj (ht)) , j = 2 , . . . , J,

> †https://github.com/amazon-science/chronos-forecasting

bqT,τ 1 (zt) ≤ · · · ≤ bqT,τ J (zt)for every context zt. Without this constraint to prevent quantile crossing, each prediction head directly estimates its corresponding quantile: 

bqT,τ j (zt) = gθτj (ht), j = 1 , . . . , J. 

We use the first 75% of the calibration set for training and the remaining 25% for validation. Training is performed for at most 50 epochs with early stopping using a patience of three epochs, and the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss is selected. The validation loss is the same quantile loss used for training, evaluated at the target quantile levels. We use AdamW [62] with β1 = 0 .9, 

β2 = 0 .999, ϵ = 10 −8, and a weight decay of 0 .01. The initial learning rate is set to 10 −3 for the RNN encoder and 10 −4 for the Transformer encoder. To construct prediction intervals with target coverage levels of 0 .8, 0 .9, and 0 .95, we use the target quantile sets {0.10 , 0.90 }, {0.05 , 0.95 }, and 

{0.025 , 0.975 }, respectively. The best hyperparameter configuration is selected through a grid search over the search space specified in Table 4. Each configuration is trained and evaluated separately using a subset of se-quences, with 20% of the sequences in each dataset used for hyperparameter selection. For each selected sequence, we use a chronological split, with the first 85% of the training data used for model fitting and the remaining 15% used for validation. Because we prioritize coverage, we adopt a two-stage hyperparameter selection procedure. First, we filter configurations based on empirical coverage. A configuration is considered eligible only if the difference between its empirical and nominal coverage is at least −0.01 for every target quantile pair and every selected sequence. Among the eligible configurations, we select the one with the lowest average Winkler score. The Winkler score is first averaged over evaluation observations within each sequence and then averaged equally across sequences. This coverage-first selection procedure is similar to the hyperparameter selection criterion used by Auer et al. [12]. The best hyperparameter configurations selected for each dataset and base predictor are presented in Tables 5–7.

B.4 Implementation Details of Baseline Methods 

NexCP [33] We use the calibration set to calibrate the prediction intervals. For hyperparameter selection, we further split the calibration set into training and validation subsets using an 85% / 15% split. The best hyperparameter configuration is selected using the same grid-search procedure as for the proposed methods, over the search space specified in Table 8. The best hyperparameter configurations selected for each dataset and base predictor are presented in Tables 9–11. 

HopCPT [12] Because the datasets used in the original HopCPT experiments include the three datasets used in our study (Air, Solar, and Sapflow), we follow the hyperparameter selection proce-dure described in the original paper. We split the calibration set equally into training and validation subsets. 

SPCI [36] We calibrate SPCI prediction intervals using the calibration set. For hyperparameter selection, we divide this set into training and validation subsets containing 85% and 15% of the observations, respectively. The best hyperparameter configuration is selected using the same grid-search procedure as for the proposed methods, over the search space specified in Table 8. The best hyperparameter configurations selected for each dataset and base predictor are presented in Tables 9–11. In its original formulation, SPCI retrains its random forest regressor at each prediction step, which can be computationally prohibitive for larger datasets. Auer et al. [12] identify the same computational bottleneck and adopt a modified implementation that trains the regressor only on the calibration set, reporting only a small performance decrease in experiments on their smallest dataset. We therefore adopt the same modification and keep the regressor fixed during prediction. This choice is also consistent with the other methods in our comparison, whose models are not retrained after calibration. 

KOWCPI [13] We use calibration set to calibrate the prediction intervals. For hyperparameter selection, we divide this set into training and validation subsets containing 85% and 15% of the ob-servations, respectively. We use Epanechnikov kernel for all experiments. The best hyperparameter configuration is selected using the same grid-search procedure as for the proposed methods, over the search space specified in Table 8. The best hyperparameter configurations selected for each dataset and base predictor are presented in Tables 9–11. 

ResCP [23] We calibrate the prediction intervals using the calibration set. Because the Air and Solar datasets were also evaluated in the original publication, we adopt the hyperparameter configurations reported for these datasets and perform hyperparameter selection only for Sapflow. For this selection, we divide the Sapflow calibration set into training and validation subsets containing 85% and 15% of the observations, respectively. We select the best configuration using the same 46 grid-search procedure as for the proposed methods, over the search space specified in Table 8. The hyperparameter configurations used for each dataset and base predictor are reported in Tables 9–11. 

DistMatch [14] We tune the KS matching threshold γ ∈ { 0.01 , 0.05 , 0.1} while fixing the residual patch length at w = 100, following the authors experimental setup. The number of matching trees, bootstrap sampling ratio, and minimum node-size parameter are fixed at 10, 0 .9, and 0, respectively, as specified in Appendix of Menadjiev et al. [14]. We select the best configuration using the same grid-search procedure as for the proposed methods, over the search space specified in Table 8. The hyperparameter configurations used for each dataset and base predictor are reported in Tables 9–11.
