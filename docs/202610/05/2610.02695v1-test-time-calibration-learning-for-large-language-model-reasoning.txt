ABSTRACT 

Reliable large language models (LLMs) must not only produce accurate answers but also express confidence that faithfully reflects their probability of being correct. Such calibration is essential for identifying uncertain predictions and supporting reliable decision-making in real-world deployment. Recent studies incorporate calibration learning into reinforcement learning (RL), jointly optimizing answer correctness and verbalized confidence using ground-truth correctness supervision. However, their reliance on labeled data limits their applicability in practical test-time settings, where ground-truth labels are unavailable and calibration may need to adapt to newly encountered target tasks. To address this challenge, we propose 

Test-Time Calibration Learning (TTCL) , a label-free framework that jointly adapts reasoning accuracy and verbalized confidence directly on unlabeled target-task data. Specifically, TTCL derives self-supervision signals for both correctness and calibration from multiple model-generated responses, enabling calibration learning at test time without ground-truth labels. Theoretical analysis further establishes TTCL as a bounded surrogate for the ideal calibration objective. Extensive experi-ments on mathematical reasoning and factual question answering demonstrate that TTCL consistently improves both accuracy and calibration across diverse models and tasks. On base models, TTCL achieves an average relative accuracy improve-ment of +40 .13% and an ECE reduction of +70 .80% across eight benchmarks. Moreover, TTCL can further improve both accuracy and calibration for already calibrated models under domain shift, particularly when source-domain calibration transfers poorly to target tasks. In the math-to-factQA setting, TTCL achieves an average relative accuracy gain of +20 .35% and reduces ECE by +53 .83% . The source code is released at https://github.com/tmlr-group/TTCL .

1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in user-facing and decision-support systems (Zhang et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026), where the outputs may directly influence real-world

Such miscalibration makes errors difficult to detect and can lead to severe consequences in high-stakes settings (Ji et al., 2023). Ideally, a well-calibrated model should produce confidence estimates that faithfully reflect its empirical likelihood of being correct. To achieve this, researchers have proposed calibration learning (Damani et al., 2026; Bani-Harouni et al., 2026), which combine calibration-oriented reward with answer correctness reward during reinforcement learning process to consistently improve LLM reasoning performance and confidence calibration behavior. Despite the promising results achieved by previous research, we observe that existing calibration learning methods typically rely on large amounts of labeled data to provide supervision for calibration, incurring substantial annotation costs (Damani et al., 2026; Tan et al., 2026; Zollo et al., 2026). More importantly, a model calibrated on a source domain may not remain well-calibrated when testing on a different target domain. As shown in Fig. 2, a model calibrated by existing method RLCR (Damani et al., 2026) on DAPO-Math-14K (Yu et al., 2025) transfers well to the math-related AMC benchmark (Yang et al., 2024), but remains limited calibration behavior on the factual NQ benchmark (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019), highlighting the sensitivity of learned calibration to domain shift. These limitations pose a key challenge: how can we adapt a model’s calibration behavior directly to an unseen target task at test time, without relying on ground-truth labels? 

To address this, we propose Test-Time Calibration Learning (TTCL), which provides a label-free surrogate for calibration learning. Specifically, TTCL derives two self-supervision signals from multiple sampled responses: a correctness reward based on the majority answer and a calibration reward based on the empirical support of each answer. The two rewards are separately applied to the answer and confidence tokens, allowing the model to improve answer correctness and confidence calibration without undesirable interference. To stabilize learning, we further introduce a target cache that maintains temporally lagged supervision targets, reducing the risk of reinforcing the model’s own instantaneous predictions. To demonstrate the effectiveness of our method, we conduct extensive experiments on mathematical reasoning and factual question-answering benchmarks with multiple model scales and series. The results show that TTCL can consistently improve both reasoning accuracy and confidence on target task. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • Conceptually, we propose Test-Time Calibration Learning, a new calibration paradigm that adapts LLM confidence directly to the target task at test time without relying on ground-truth labels, enabling calibration of confidence estimation under unseen test distributions (Section 3.1). • Technically, we develop a label-free TTCL framework that constructs self-supervised signals for correctness and calibration rewards from multi-rollout frequency (Section 3.2 & 3.3). • Theoretically, we show that TTCL essentially serves as a bounded surrogate for the ideal calibration learning objective, theoretically grounding its label-free learning mechanism (Section 4). • Experimentally, extensive experiments across models and benchmarks show consistent improve-ments of TTCL in both reasoning and calibration performance (Section 5). 

2 PRELIMINARY 

In this section, we first introduce the problem setup of calibration learning and the corresponding calibration measurement, and then review Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a widely used RL algorithm in eliciting LLM reasoning capability. 

Problem setup of calibration learning. Let πθ denote an LLM parameterized by θ. Given a question 

x, the model generates a response y ∼ πθ (·| x) containing a reasoning trajectory and an extracted final answer a = Ans( y). Given the ground-truth (GT) answer a∗, the correctness indicator is defined as z(y, a ∗) = I[a ≡ a⋆] ∈ { 0, 1}. A confidence estimator c(x, y ) ∈ [0 , 1] quantifies the model’s belief that the generated response y is correct. Let C and Z denote the corresponding confidence and correctness random variables. A perfectly calibrated model satisfies: 

Pr( Z = 1 |C = c) = c, (1) indicating that predictions with confidence c should be correct about c fraction of the time. In practice, calibration learning typically optimizes c(x, y ) against observed correctness z(y) using a proper scoring rule (Gneiting & Raftery, 2007). A common choice is the Brier score (Brier, 1950), 

LBrier = ( c(x, y ) − z(y, a ∗)) 2, which encourages the predicted confidence to match the probability of correctness. Building on this, recent methods (Damani et al., 2026; Bani-Harouni et al., 2026; Ma 2Figure 2: Calibration performance of Qwen3-8B and its RLCR-calibrated model on DAPO-Math-14K. Left: Base Qwen3-8B exhibits over-confidence on both AMC and NQ, with predicted confidence exceeding empirical accuracy. Right: RLCR on DAPO-Math-14K transfers well to AMC but remains limited on NQ, where further TTCL adaptation markedly improves calibration. et al., 2026) jointly optimize reasoning correctness and confidence calibration during RL. Specifically, RLCR (Damani et al., 2026) trains the LLM to verbalize a confidence score using reward as: 

rRLCR = z(y, a ∗) − (c(x, y ) − z(y, a ∗)) 2 , (2) where the first term is a binary correctness reward, while the second is a Brier-score calibration reward. However, both objectives require the ground-truth answer a∗, which limits their applicability when annotated labels are not available. In this work, we focus on test-time calibration learning, where the model adapts its confidence estimation on unlabeled test data. 

Calibration measurement. Expected Calibration Error (ECE) is a widely used metric for evaluating calibration by measuring the discrepancy between predicted confidence and empirical accuracy (Guo et al., 2017). Given N predictions partitioned into M confidence bins {Bm}Mm=1 , ECE is defined as:

3 TTCL: T EST -TIME CALIBRATION LEARNING 

In this section, we first present the motivation of the need for TTCL (Sec. 3.1), and then introduce the technical details of our proposed TTCL method (Sec. 3.2 and Sec. 3.3). 3.1 WHY TEST -TIME CALIBRATION LEARNING (TTCL)? Despite their strong reasoning capabilities, current LLMs often remain over-confident on downstream tasks. As illustrated in the left part of Fig. 2, Qwen3-8B exhibits clear over-confidence on both the mathematical benchmark AMC (Yang et al., 2024) and the factual benchmark NQ (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019), with estimated confidence systematically exceeding empirical accuracy and resulting in larger ECE values. Moreover, calibration learned on one task may not transfer well to others. As shown in the right part of Fig. 2, Qwen3-8B calibrated with RLCR on DAPO-Math-14K (Yu et al., 2025) achieves strong calibration on AMC, yet remains poorly calibrated on NQ. These observations motivate direct test-time calibration of LLMs and lead to two key principles of TTCL: 

> Key Principles of TTCL

• Label-free calibration learning: derive supervision signals for calibration learning from unlabeled test-time data, removing the dependence on annotated ground-truth labels. • Target-task adaptation: adapt confidence estimation directly to newly encountered tasks at test time, alleviating the limited cross-task generalization of calibration learning. Compared with existing calibration learning methods (Damani et al., 2026; Bani-Harouni et al., 2026), the first principle, label-free calibration learning , removes the dependence on annotated correctness labels, which are often costly to obtain or unavailable at test time. The second principle, target-task adaptation , enables the model to recalibrate its confidence directly on the deployment task, mitigating the limited cross-task transferability of calibration learned from source tasks. A target cache stabilizes these signals and is updated via EMA across epochs. Correctness and calibration rewards are optimized separately, with the former updating reasoning and answer tokens and the latter updating only confidence tokens. further illustrates that TTCL can adapt calibration directly to a new target task at test time. Starting from the RLCR-calibrated Qwen3-8B, applying TTCL on unlabeled NQ data reduces the ECE from 0.730 to 0.290, without requiring additional ground-truth annotations. The self-supervision signals above are constructed from the empirical answer distribution q(a | x) of the current rollout group. However, directly using this instantaneous distribution can tightly couple the policy with its self-generated targets, leading to reward hacking. As majority-based supervision encourages answer consistency, their empirical frequencies rise, further increasing the associated confidence targets. 4 THEORETICAL ANALYSIS OF TTCL 

In this section, we theoretically connect TTCL to the calibration definition in Eq. (1). 

Analysis: TTCL provides a label-free surrogate for calibration learning, with its deviation from the ideal calibration objective bounded by the frequency-based estimation error. 

Let p⋆(x, y ) = Pr( Z = 1 | X = x, Y = y) denote the conditional probability that response y is cor-rect for question x. For TTCL, we denote its frequency-based confidence target and the corresponding estimation error as s(x, y ) = q(Ans( y) | x) and ϵ = E[|s(X, Y ) − p⋆(X, Y )|] respectively. 

Theorem 4.1 (TTCL as a Surrogate for the Ideal Calibration Objective ).

Let JTTCL (c) = E(c(X, Y ) − s(X, Y )) 2 denote the calibration objective optimized by TTCL, and let Jideal (c) = E(c(X, Y ) − p⋆(X, Y )) 2 denote the ideal calibration objective. Then, for any confidence estimator c(X, Y ) ∈ [0 , 1] , |J TTCL (c) − J ideal (c)| ≤ 2ϵ.

Theorem 4.1 demonstrates that TTCL optimizes a bounded surrogate of the ideal calibration objective. The objective gap is controlled by the estimation error between the frequency-based target and the latent conditional correctness probability. A detailed proof is provided in Appendix D.3. We next present two supporting results: the empirical probability q(Ans( y) | x) of the answer frequency can serve as a proxy for the latent conditional correctness probability p⋆(x, y ), while 

p⋆(x, y ) constitutes the ideal target for calibration learning. 

Proposition 4.2 (Answer Frequency as a Proxy for Conditional Correctness ).

Let qπθ (a | x) denote the population probability of answer a under the model. The empirical answer probability estimated from G independent responses is an unbiased and consistent estimator of 

qπθ (a | x). When answer distribution reflects model’s uncertainty over the correct answer, qπθ (a | x)

serves as a proxy for the probability that response y is correct, i.e., p⋆(x, y ), where a = Ans( y).

Proposition 4.2 provides the statistical justification for using answer frequency as the label-free confidence target in TTCL. A detailed proof is provided in Appendix D.1. We next show that the conditional correctness probability is the ideal target for calibration learning. 

Proposition 4.3 (Conditional Correctness as the Ideal Calibration Target ).

If the confidence estimator satisfies c(X, Y ) = p⋆(X, Y ), then the resulting confidence is perfectly calibrated, i.e., Pr( Z = 1 | C = c) = c. Minimizing the expected Brier score yields the optimal confidence estimator c⋆ = arg min c JBrier (c) = p⋆.

This proposition connects Brier score minimization to perfect calibration through the conditional correctness probability. A detailed proof is provided in Appendix D.2. Together with Proposition 4.2, this forms a theoretical bridge from answer frequency to conditional correctness probability and ultimately to calibrated confidence, supporting the surrogate guarantee in Theorem 4.1.

The mathematics includes MATH500 (Lightman et al., 2024), AMC (Yang et al., 2024), AIME24 (Zhang & Math-AI, 2024), AIME25 (Zhang & Math-AI, 2025) and AIME26 (Zhang & Math-AI, 2026), while the factual QA includes SimpleQA (Wei et al., 2024), ChineseSimpleQA (He et al., 2025), NQ (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019), HotpotQA (Yang et al., 2018) and TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017). We apply TTCL to each benchmark independently without access its ground-truth labels, and evaluate the resulting model on the same benchmark. More details are provided in Appendix E.3. 

Implementation details. We implement TTCL based on the VERL framework (Sheng et al., 2025). For each question, we sample G = 32 rollouts with a temperature of 0.7. We train TTCL with the learning rate of 5 × 10 −7. The target cache is updated after each epoch with an EMA rate of η = 0 .01 .Following prior studies (Damani et al., 2026), we evaluate answer correctness using Accuracy (ACC), calibration using Expected Calibration Error (ECE), and confidence discrimination using AUROC. More implementation and metric details refer to Appendix E.2 and Appendix E.4. 

Baselines. Besides the corresponding starting models, we compare TTCL with several label-free calibration methods, including P(True) (Kadavath et al., 2022), Verbalization and Elicitation (Tian et al., 2023), and BaseCal (Tan et al., 2026). These methods mainly rely on post-hoc confidence estimation strategies to obtain more accurate confidence scores for given responses, without training the model. More details of the baselines are introduced in Appendix E.1. 5.2 MAIN EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS

TTCL can further adapt calibrated models to new target tasks. From Tables 3 and 4, TTCL remains effective on RLCR-calibrated models. More importantly, RLCR calibration transfers imperfectly across domains: math-calibrated models remain poorly calibrated on factual QA tasks, and vice versa. In both math-to-FactQA and FactQA-to-math settings, TTCL consistently improves ACC and ECE, showing that it can refine calibration directly on unlabeled target-task data when transfer is limited. 

TTCL consistently outperforms existing label-free calibration strategies. From Table 5, TTCL generally achieves higher ACC and AUROC and lower ECE than existing label-free baselines. As these methods mainly perform post-hoc confidence estimation without model training, their ACC remains unchanged, while TTCL further improves accuracy through target-task adaptation. RLCR performs strongly under label-leakage settings with access to ground-truth correctness labels, reflecting the benefit of explicit supervision. 6 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we introduce Test-Time Calibration Learning (TTCL) , a label-free framework for adapting LLM calibration directly to target tasks at test time. Unlike existing methods relying on labeled supervision, TTCL derives correctness and calibration self-supervision from multiple model-generated responses, enabling calibration learning without access to ground-truth labels. Theoretical analysis shows that TTCL provides a label-free surrogate for the ideal calibration objective. Extensive experiments on mathematical reasoning and factual QA demonstrate consistent improvements in ac-curacy and calibration. TTCL can further adapt pre-calibrated models under domain shift, improving 9calibration when source-domain transfer is limited. We hope this work inspires further research on test-time calibration for reliable confidence estimation in unlabeled deployment settings.

B RELATED WORK 

In this section, we mainly review related work from three perspectives: LLM reasoning, label-free RL methods for LLM reasoning, and existing LLM calibration methods, to better position TTCL within the broader research landscape and demonstrate its distinctions from prior studies. 

Large language model (LLM) reasoning. The reasoning capabilities of LLMs have advanced rapidly in recent years. Early studies primarily elicit reasoning capability through prompting and inference-time strategies, such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting (Wei et al., 2022), Tree-of-Thoughts (ToT) (Yao et al., 2023), or self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023). More recently, reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as an effective paradigm for further eliciting and strengthening LLM reasoning capabilities (Shao et al., 2024; OpenAI, 2024), with reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) becoming a widely adopted approach in modern LLM post-training pipelines (Guo et al., 2025; Team et al., 2026; Xiao et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2025). However, improvements in reasoning accuracy do not necessarily translate into reliable uncertainty estimation. Existing studies have shown that LLMs can exhibit substantial over-confidence (Groot & Valdenegro-Toro, 2024; Leng et al., 2025), while RL training that mainly rewards answer correctness may further exacerbate such miscalibration (Kalai et al., 2025; Bereket & Leskovec, 2025). This poses substantial risks for deploying LLMs in high-risk scenarios, where confidently incorrect predictions may lead to severe consequences. Therefore, beyond improving reasoning accuracy, reliable reasoning models should also faithfully reflect the uncertainty of their predictions through well-calibrated confidence estimates. 

Label-free RL methods for LLM reasoning. Recently, emerging studies have explored RL without annotated ground-truth answers to improve LLM reasoning capabilities, reducing reliance on labeled supervision (Yang et al., 2026). According to the source of self-supervised signals, these methods can be broadly divided into two categories. One line of work mainly encourages self-consistency among model outputs (Zuo et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025a), for example, using majority-voted answers as pseudo labels to supervise RL optimization (Zhang et al., 2026; Shafayat et al., 2025). Another line constructs rewards from the model’s internal signals (Zhang et al., 2025c; Wen et al., 2026), such as minimizing prediction entropy (Zhang et al., 2025b; Prabhudesai et al., 2025) or maximizing self-certainty (Zhao et al., 2026), to sharpen the model’s predictive distribution. Although these methods enable label-free improvement of reasoning performance, they primarily focus on answer correctness and lack an explicit optimization objective for calibration learning. As a result, the resulting models may still fail to express confidence that faithfully reflects uncertainty over their own predictions. In contrast, our work further explores label-free learning for LLM calibration, enabling models to adapt both reasoning capability and confidence estimation on target tasks at test time. 

Existing LLM calibration methods. Existing approaches to LLM calibration can be broadly catego-rized into post-hoc calibration and calibration learning. Post-hoc methods estimate confidence for model-generated responses using signals from multiple perspectives, such as token probabilities (Tan et al., 2026), verbalized confidence (Tian et al., 2023), self-evaluation (Kadavath et al., 2022), sam-pling consistency (Lyu et al., 2025), or semantic uncertainty (Farquhar et al., 2024; Kuhn et al., 2023). 14 While effective in many settings, these methods primarily derive confidence through external signals or post-processing, rather than explicitly training the LLM to develop the capability of estimating the confidence of its own generated responses. In contrast, recent calibration learning studies directly train LLMs to express their calibrated confidence, typically by incorporating calibration-aware objec-tives into existing LLM training objectives (Ma et al., 2026; Seo et al., 2026; Finlay et al., 2026). For example, RLCR (Damani et al., 2026), BCRL (Wu et al., 2025), and Rewarding Doubt (Bani-Harouni et al., 2026) leverage proper scoring rules (Gneiting & Raftery, 2007) to introduce calibration rewards into RL training, jointly optimizing answer correctness and confidence estimation. These methods explicitly encourage calibration to become an intrinsic capability of the model. However, their calibration supervision generally relies on ground-truth correctness labels during training (Tan et al., 2026), incurring additional annotation costs, while the learned calibration behavior may transfer poorly when the target distribution differs from the training distribution (Jang et al., 2026). In this work, we instead introduce Test-Time Calibration Learning (TTCL) , which directly adapts model calibration on the target task at test time using label-free supervision without requiring ground-truth labels, thereby enabling calibration learning under unseen test distributions.

F.4 REWARD HACKING WITHOUT TARGET CACHE 

To examine the failure mode that motivates the target cache, we train Qwen3-4B on AIME24 using supervision computed directly from the current rollout group. Fig. 13 shows four questions for which the rollouts increasingly concentrate on an incorrect answer. As more rollouts produce that answer, its frequency-based probability rises toward one. Because this probability also serves as the confidence target, the supervision encourages the model’s confidence to rise toward 1.0 despite the answer being wrong. This is a form of reward hacking: the model can obtain a higher self-generated reward by consistently producing an incorrect answer with high confidence, without improving correctness or calibration. The target cache is motivated by this failure mode; by fixing targets within each epoch, it weakens the immediate feedback between the current policy and its self-supervision signals. F.7 CALIBRATION RESULTS OF EXISTING LABEL -FREE RL M ETHODS 

To examine whether existing label-free RL methods can improve model calibration while enhancing reasoning accuracy, we evaluate several representative approaches, including Self-certainty Max-imization (Zhao et al., 2026), Entropy Minimization (Prabhudesai et al., 2025), Majority-voting for pseudo-label construction (Zuo et al., 2025), and Co-rewarding (Zhang et al., 2026). These methods are primarily designed to improve reasoning performance without ground-truth labels, but lack an explicit calibration objective. Thanks to the Co-rewarding work, which releases checkpoints trained on DAPO-Math-14K with these different label-free RL methods, we directly evaluate these checkpoints for calibration performance. Since most of them are trained from pretrained models and have limited ability to follow verbalized-confidence instructions, we use the mean token probability of the generated response as the confidence estimate (Malinin & Gales, 2020; Orgad et al., 2025). As shown in Table 16, although these label-free RL methods improve accuracy to some extent compared with base models, their calibration remains relatively poor. We observe that the ECE values are generally high, while the mean confidence consistently exceeds 0.8 and is typically above 0.9 for the Qwen models, indicating clear over-confidence. These results suggest that accuracy-oriented label-free RL does not naturally lead to well-calibrated confidence estimates when an explicit calibration objective is absent. These observations are consistent with our discussion in Appendix B Related Work and further motivate our exploration of label-free test-time calibration learning framework. F.8 CASE STUDY
