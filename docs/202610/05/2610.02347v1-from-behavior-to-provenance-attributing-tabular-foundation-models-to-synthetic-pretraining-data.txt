Abstract
Training-data attribution aims to identify which training examples shape model behavior, yet validating such
claims is difficult because causal training influence is rarely observable. We argue that controlled synthetic
pretraining makes attribution experimentally testable. Using O’PRIOR, a provenance-rich synthetic task
generator for tabular foundation models, we construct a testbed in which every pretraining task carries explicit
lineage over structural mechanisms, missingness, confounding, shortcuts, and distribution shift. We combine
behavior-conditioned attribution with counterfactual retraining and provenance-aware interventions to test both
task-level faithfulness and mechanism-level consistency. On held-out real tasks, removing the top-attributed
5% of synthetic tasks decreases mean ROC-AUC by 0.013, compared with 0.002 ± 0.004 under random
removal, while removing bottom-attributed tasks improves performance by 0.003. Within shortcut-provenance
tasks, targeted removal yields an effect of 0.043 versus 0.016 for matched random removal. Provenance
discrimination is more modest by ranking AUROC (0.55–0.62), despite substantial top-k enrichment,
revealing that provenance association and interventional faithfulness need not coincide. Our results establish
synthetic provenance as a controlled setting for verifiable contributive attribution
Keywords: Training Data Attribution; Synthetic Priors; Data Provenance; Tabular Foundation Models

Introduction
Training-data attribution seeks to explain model behavior in terms of the data that shaped it. Given a prediction,
capability, or failure, contributive attribution methods aim to identify the training examples that influenced that behavior.
Existing approaches include influence functions, gradient-based tracing, data-models, and scalable estimators such
as TRAK [1, 2, 3, 4]. Yet a fundamental difficulty remains: how can we tell whether an attribution is faithful? The
contribution of a training example is not directly observed, and provenance metadata, when available, records where
data came from rather than how the learned model would have behaved had that data been absent. Attribution is
therefore often evaluated through plausibility or indirect proxies rather than controlled interventions, limiting its use for
auditing and accountability.
We argue that synthetic pretraining turns this validation problem into an experimental opportunity. When training data
is produced by a controllable generator, each training task can carry an explicit record of how it was generated, while
the training corpus itself can be manipulated. We study this setting in tabular foundation models (TFMs) [5, 6, 7, 8, 9],
whose predictive behavior is largely acquired from synthetic supervised tasks. In particular, we build on O’PRIOR
[10], a compositional synthetic prior in which each task records its structural mechanism together with interventions for
missingness, confounding, spurious correlation, and distribution shift. While O’PRIOR was originally introduced to

study how prior design affects downstream TFM quality, we use its controllable lineage for a different purpose: as a
provenance-rich testbed for evaluating training-data attribution.
Crucially, known provenance is not itself causal ground truth. A task may carry a particular generative mechanism
without being important for a given downstream behavior, and two tasks with identical provenance may have very
different training effects. Our framework therefore connects three quantities that are normally unavailable together:
the attribution assigned to individual synthetic tasks, the known provenance of those tasks, and the behavioral effect
of removing them and retraining. At the task level, we test interventional faithfulness by asking whether removing
highly attributed tasks changes a downstream behavior more than removing equally sized random or weakly attributed
subsets. At the provenance level, we ask whether influential tasks concentrate on generative mechanisms associated
with the behavior being explained, and whether this association changes coherently under controlled interventions. This
separation lets us distinguish plausible provenance association from attribution that survives intervention on the training
corpus.
Our experiments reveal a clear distinction between provenance association and interventional faithfulness. Influence
is the most removal-faithful estimator: removing its top-attributed 5% of tasks decreases mean ROC-AUC by 0.013,
versus 0.002 ± 0.004 under random removal, while bottom removal slightly improves performance. Within shortcut
provenance, Influence also yields the largest top-vs-random counterfactual gap (0.030). Yet provenance discrimination
remains modest (macro ROC-AUC 0.55–0.62), and TracIn achieves higher shortcut Lift@5% than Influence (1.81×
vs. 1.60×) despite weaker interventional faithfulness. Provenance-level attribution is also slightly more stable than
task-level rankings (0.86 vs. 0.82). Together, these results show that provenance association, behavioral influence under
retraining, and attribution stability are related but distinct properties.
Contributions.
We make three contributions:
1. Provenance-grounded attribution: we formulate synthetic-data attribution hierarchically, connecting
downstream behavior to influential pretraining tasks and those tasks to their known generative provenance.
2. Interventional validation: using O’PRIOR’s frozen synthetic corpus, we test attribution through counter-
factual task removal and provenance-conditioned interventions rather than attribution plausibility alone.
3. A systematic study of faithfulness, provenance association, and reliability: we compare attribu-
tion methods against intervention-derived effects, study attribution under controlled failures and distribution
shifts, and show that these evaluation axes need not rank attribution methods in the same way.

Provenance-Grounded Attribution
Training-data attribution seeks to estimate how training examples contribute to learned model behavior, yet the causal
quantity of interest is generally unobserved. Given a model fθ trained on D, an attribution method returns scores
intended to identify which elements of D contributed to a behavior B. Whether these scores reflect genuine training
influence is difficult to establish: the causal contribution of an example is unobserved, and dataset provenance, when
available, does not reveal how model behavior would change had that example been absent. Our framework is built
around removing that obstacle by keeping three quantities explicitly separate: the known generative provenance zi of
a synthetic training task, an attribution method’s estimate a(B)

τB(S) of actually removing a set of tasks S and retraining. Provenance is recorded lineage, not assumed causal ground
truth; whether it agrees with a(B) and τB is the empirical question the rest of the paper answers.

Together, the framework makes three quantities jointly accessible that are usually separated in training-data attribution:
estimated task attribution a(B), observed behavioral change under counterfactual removal τB, and known generative
provenance Z. Their combination lets us distinguish three properties that need not coincide: removal faithfulness,
whether attributed training tasks actually affect the behavior they are credited with; provenance association, whether
highly attributed tasks are enriched for behavior-relevant generative mechanisms; and attribution reliability, whether
these conclusions persist across runs and perturbations. The contribution is therefore not a new attribution estimator,
but a controlled evaluation paradigm in which apparent provenance alignment can be tested against intervention-derived
training effects.

Experiments
3.1
Experimental protocol
Frozen synthetic corpus.
We instantiate O’PRIOR once to obtain a fixed corpus D = {Di}N
i=1 of N =
5,000 synthetic classification tasks. Each task contains an in-context support/query split, a persistent identifier, and
provenance zi recording its structural mechanism and active missingness, confounding, shortcut, and distribution-
shift interventions. Crucially, the corpus is frozen before training: no new synthetic tasks are sampled during either
pretraining or counterfactual retraining, and a removed task remains unavailable throughout the corresponding retraining
run. Individual synthetic tasks therefore remain well-defined units of both attribution and intervention.
Model and downstream behaviors.
We train NanoTabPFN [12, 13] for 15 epochs with 2,000 optimization
updates per epoch and four synthetic tasks per batch. Evaluation uses 22 held-out OpenML-CC18 classification datasets
[14]; no real dataset is used during synthetic pretraining. The default behavior Biid is mean ROC-AUC across these
real tasks. We additionally construct controlled missingness, covariate-shift, and shortcut behaviors to test whether
attribution changes with the behavior being explained. When the reported behavior is non-differentiable, we use
cross-entropy only as the differentiable attribution objective while retaining ROC-AUC for behavioral evaluation.

3.2
Task-level faithfulness and provenance association
E1: Task-level removal faithfulness.
We first test the most direct claim made by contributive attribution: whether
tasks assigned high influence exert larger behavioral effects when they are made unavailable to learning. We rank all
5,000 synthetic tasks with respect to Biid and retrain after removing top-attributed, bottom-attributed, or size-matched
random subsets.
At r = 5%, removing the top-attributed tasks decreases mean ROC-AUC by ∆B = 0.013, compared with 0.002±0.004
under random removal (Fig. 1a). Removing the bottom-attributed subset instead gives ∆B = −0.003, i.e., a small
improvement, yielding a top-vs-random faithfulness gap of 0.011. The contrast is therefore visible against both controls:
the highly attributed subset is substantially more consequential than random removal, whereas the lowest-ranked tasks
are not required to preserve the baseline behavior.
The effect persists at r = 10%: top removal gives ∆B = 0.012 versus 0.004 for random removal, corresponding to a
gap of 0.008. Separation from the bottom subset is weaker at this larger intervention, so we regard the 5% experiment

effect is not confined to a single removal fraction. Importantly, these results validate the attribution ranking through the
learning procedure itself rather than through similarity to downstream examples or provenance labels.

gap (+0.008), compared with +0.002 for TracIn and +0.001 for TRAK; representation similarity produces a negative

some attributed tasks matter, but does not explain what properties characterize them. Because every O’PRIOR task

Provenance association alone, however, does not establish that the ranking distinguishes consequential tasks within a

The result is not universal across all behavior–mechanism combinations. Other targeted pairs show heterogeneous or
inconclusive separation. We therefore treat within-provenance faithfulness as an empirical property to be tested for each
behavior rather than assuming that known provenance is itself causal ground truth.

3.3
Behavior specificity and estimator disagreement
E3: Attribution tracks the behavior being explained.
A behavior-conditioned attribution should not be
invariant to a controlled change in the downstream behavior. We therefore keep the pretrained model and synthetic
corpus fixed while varying the severity of missingness, covariate shift, and shortcut interventions at evaluation time and
recompute attribution for each resulting behavior.
Across these graded interventions, attribution to behavior-matched provenance changes systematically with intervention
severity. The corresponding Spearman correlations satisfy ρsev ≥0.63 over at least four distinct severity levels (Fig. 2b).
Thus, the provenance associated with highly attributed training tasks changes monotonically with the strength of the
downstream failure being explained rather than remaining fixed across evaluation conditions.
The response is heterogeneous across mechanisms: some controlled behaviors produce clear matched-provenance shifts
whereas others are weaker or indistinguishable from controls. This negative evidence is important. Known provenance
makes such failures visible and prevents behavior-specific attribution from being judged only by examples for which
the expected mechanism happens to appear.
E4: Provenance association and interventional faithfulness can disagree.
Comparing estimators exposes
a distinction that is difficult to observe in standard attribution benchmarks. Influence gives the largest global top-vs-
random removal gap and the largest within shortcut counterfactual gap (Table 1). Provenance enrichment gives a
different ordering: under Bshortcut, TracIn achieves Lift@5% of 1.81×, exceeding Influence at 1.60× (Fig. 2a), even
though its within-provenance faithfulness gap is smaller (+0.023 vs. +0.030).
This reversal shows that the two evaluations measure genuinely different properties. A method can preferentially
retrieve tasks carrying the right provenance label without identifying the subset whose absence has the largest effect on
learning. Conversely, intervention-derived faithfulness does not require perfect recovery of the corresponding generative
mechanism. We therefore treat provenance association as supporting evidence about where attribution concentrates and
counterfactual retraining as evidence about whether the nominated training data is behaviorally consequential under the
specified learning intervention.

Figure 3 shows that provenance-level summaries are more stable for every estimator considered. Influence increases

encode different notions of training influence. Reliability under repeated estimation and agreement across attribution
assumptions are therefore separate properties.
4
Conclusion
We introduced a provenance-grounded framework for evaluating contributive training-data attribution in synthetically
pretrained foundation models. The key idea is to keep three quantities explicitly separate: the attribution assigned to
individual training tasks, their recorded generative provenance, and the behavioral effect of removing those tasks and
retraining. This makes it possible to test attribution claims through intervention rather than treating either attribution
scores or provenance labels as ground truth.
Across our experiments, these quantities provide complementary but distinct signals. Influence exhibits the strongest
counterfactual removal faithfulness, both globally and within shortcut-provenance strata, while attribution scores
recover generative provenance only moderately at the level of the full ranking. At the same time, highly ranked
tasks are enriched for behavior-matched provenance, and this attribution changes systematically under controlled
failures and increasing intervention severity. Crucially, the methods need not be ordered consistently across these
criteria: TracIn shows stronger shortcut-provenance enrichment than Influence (1.81× vs. 1.60×) despite a smaller
within-provenance counterfactual gap (0.023 vs. 0.030). This reversal demonstrates that provenance association and
interventional faithfulness are genuinely different properties. We additionally find that aggregating task-level attribution
onto generative mechanisms yields modestly more reproducible summaries, suggesting that provenance can provide a
useful intermediate level of analysis between individual training examples and downstream behavior.
Taken together, these results suggest a broader role for controlled synthetic pretraining in attribution research. Known
synthetic provenance should not be viewed as causal ground truth: it records how a task was generated, not whether that
task caused a particular learned behavior. Its value instead comes from providing a structured reference that can be
combined with counterfactual retraining, behavior-specific evaluation, and reliability analysis. In this sense, synthetic
provenance turns attribution from a primarily plausibility-based exercise into one for which competing claims can be
experimentally tested.
Limitations.
Our study is deliberately controlled, and several limitations bound the scope of its conclusions. First,
the experiments use a finite O’PRIOR corpus of 5,000 synthetic tasks and a NanoTabPFN-scale tabular foundation
model; the same relationships may change with substantially larger models, larger synthetic corpora, or natural-data
pretraining. Second, the removal effect τB(S) is causal only with respect to the specific finite-corpus intervention and
training algorithm considered here: it depends on the optimizer, training budget, initialization, and retraining procedure,
and should not be interpreted as an intrinsic influence value independent of learning dynamics. Third, removing sets of
tasks can induce interaction and redundancy effects, so the effect of a subset need not equal the sum of the effects of its
members. Fourth, O’PRIOR mechanisms are compositional and can co-occur; consequently, provenance enrichment
provides evidence of association but does not isolate a single generative mechanism as the unique cause of a behavior.
Our within-provenance controls mitigate this issue but do not remove all dependencies between mechanisms. Fifth,
attribution for non-differentiable behaviors relies on a differentiable surrogate objective, which may not perfectly align
with the reported downstream metric. Finally, counterfactual retraining is computationally expensive, which limits the
number and scale of interventions that can be evaluated exhaustively.
Future work should extend this evaluation paradigm to larger foundation models, richer and multi-generation provenance
graphs, and settings where synthetic and natural pretraining data coexist. An important direction is to develop cheaper
validation procedures that retain the evidential value of retraining-based interventions while scaling to substantially
larger training corpora.
