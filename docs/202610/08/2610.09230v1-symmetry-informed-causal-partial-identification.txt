ABSTRACT
Partial identification (PI) entails estimating bounds on causal effects by encoding
different assumptions on data generation as a constrained optimization problem.
Such bounds can suffice to inform policy decisions even if the causal effect itself
is not identifiable. Often vacuous in practice, practitioners seek to exhaustively
encode domain knowledge as additional constraints to make the PI bounds more
informative. We introduce known data symmetries—invariance of the causal effect
under certain data transformations—as a new source of constraints to inform PI.
We operationalize this as a shape constraint on the causal function, and via a
change of measure against which PI is posed using simple data pre-processing.
Both approaches are shown to sharpen bounds under two canonical PI models.
This is shown both theoretically for the population case, and via experiments in the
finite-sample case. More broadly, our framework establishes data symmetries as a
natural, underutilized source of background knowledge for robust causal inference.

INTRODUCTION
Interventions are the gold standard for answering causal queries (Pearl, 2009; Peters et al., 2017).
Absent interventions, observed associations may be due to hidden confounders rather than causal
effects, so researchers resort to adjusting via auxiliary variables, which rest on strong, untestable
assumptions that may simply fail (Zhang et al., 2023). Partial identification (PI) offers a principled
relaxation to this problem. Rather than insisting on assumptions sufficient for point identification,
PI returns bounds on the causal effect under a milder set of assumptions (Padh et al., 2023). These
bounds are constructed by considering all possible data-generating processes (DGPs) compatible
with both the observed data and the chosen assumptions. Consequently, they are only as informative
as the strength of said assumptions, and can often be near-vacuous. To make PI actionable for
decision-making, practitioners must exhaustively incorporate credible domain knowledge into the PI
solver to restrict the set of compatible DGPs, thereby sharpening the bounds.
While existing PI families encode domain knowledge by bounding the strength of hidden confound-
ing (Tan, 2006; Rosenbaum, 2002), positing a causal graph (Meresht et al., 2022), or imposing shape
restrictions on the class of causal functions (Chetverikov et al., 2018), a natural and accessible source
of prior knowledge remains underutilized: known data symmetries. Symmetries describe properties
of a system that remain invariant under certain data transformations; for example, image content may
not change under rotation/reflection/translation (Bronstein et al., 2021). Readily used in statistical
regularization (Chen et al., 2020; Lyle et al., 2020), we propose re-purposing known symmetries as a
natural shape restriction on the set of candidate causal functions to sharpen PI bounds.
Contributions.
We give two operationalizations: an invariance shape constraint on the causal
functions added to white-box PI solvers (§3), and a change-of-measure approach where off-the-shelf
or black-box solvers are simply fed pre-processed data via symmetry transformations (§4). We license
the latter by showing it realizes a soft intervention: preserving the causal mechanism and graph up to
relabeling, while diluting confounding. Thus, validity is preserved on aforementioned PI families. We
then develop observable conditions for bound sharpening, and a simple pad-and-intersect protocol to
guard against misspecified symmetry under two canonical PI models: bounded confounding (Cinelli
& Hazlett, 2020; Chernozhukov et al., 2026) and leaky instruments (Deaner, 2022; Cinelli & Hazlett,
2025). Finally, we validate both approaches on simulated and real data.

2.1
STATISTICAL VS. CAUSAL INFERENCE
Given n samples D := {(xi, yi, zi)}i∈[n] from the observational distribution pXY Z of treatment
X ∈X ⊆Rdx, outcome Y ∈Y ⊆R and pre-treatment co-variates Z ∈Z ⊆Rdz, we aim to find
the function h⋆∈H := {h : X →R} defining the causal effect of X on Y under the following DGP:
Y = h⋆(X) + U + ξ,
E[U ] = E[ξ ] = 0,
ξ ⊥⊥(U, X, Z).
(1)
The unobserved noise (ξ, U) is assumed mean-centered w.l.o.g., with the intercept absorbed into h⋆.
Independence of noise U ⊥⊥X implies E[Y |x] = h⋆(x). Statistical inference entails identifying
precisely the Bayes optimal predictor E[Y |x] from D via empirical risk minimization (ERM): for a
proper, convex loss ℓ: R×R →R+, minimize over h ∈H an empirical version of the statistical risk,
Rerm(h) := E[ℓ(Y, h(X))].
(2)
Then, for a sufficiently rich hypothesis class, the minimizer herm provides an unbiased estimate of h⋆.
However, independence of X and the unobserved noise U may not hold in general, in which case
E[U |x]̸ = 0 and the conditional E[Y |x] now gives a biased estimate of h⋆(x) (Pearl, 2009; Peters
et al., 2017). This dependence arises due to unobserved common causes U of X and Y , known as
confounders. We say that X and Y are confounded and refer to the resulting bias as confounding
bias (Pearl, 2009). The observed pre-treatment co-variates Z may contain auxiliary information, e.g.
proxies for such confounders, that can help to mitigate this bias. This encompasses causal inference
which seeks to identify h⋆under hidden confounding, or at the very least account for it by finding
bounds on h⋆should point-identification not be possible. Both approaches are outlined below.
2.2
POINT IDENTIFICATION VIA INTERVENTION AND ADJUSTMENT
We can make treatment X and the confounder U independent via an intervention do(X = x) that
sets X to a fixed value x in Eq. (1) during data generation. Under such a treatment intervention,
henceforth denoted as do(x) for brevity, outcome Y follows the interventional distribution pY |do(x).
The loss from Eq. (2) evaluated under pY |do(x) now defines the causal risk R (Kania & Wit, 2023),
Rx(y) := E[ℓ(Y, y)|do(x)],
R(h) := EX∼pX[RX(h(X))].

which removes the irreducible noise from Eq. (3) and measures how far h is from h⋆, with E(h⋆) = 0.

Choice of constraints.
The construction of Ppı depends on domain knowledge. At the agnostic
extreme, Manski’s (1990) “no-assumption” bounds use only a bounded support of Y to constrain
Hpı(x) for binary x. Other approaches bound the strength of unmeasured confounding through odds
ratios (Rosenbaum, 2002), propensity scores in the Marginal Sensitivity Model (MSM) (Tan, 2006), or
the share of variance explained by hidden confounders in the partial r2 model (Cinelli & Hazlett, 2020;
Chernozhukov et al., 2026). Robust optimization (RO) formulations instead take Qpı to be a total-
variation (Bertsimas et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2022) or Wasserstein (Meresht et al., 2022) ball around
pXY Z. Alternatively, one may directly model the response function H⋆(·) := h⋆(·) + U + ξ (Padh
et al., 2023), e.g., with GANs matched to pXY Z (Hu et al., 2021). Many methods further sharpen
bounds via conditional independences on auxiliary variables (Ailer et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2025). A
notable example is IV-based PI (Balke & Pearl, 1997), where U may affect Y non-additively, unlike

Eq. (1). Neural variants extend this to continuous, high-dimensional treatments and IVs (Kilbertus
et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2021; Padh et al., 2023; Meresht et al., 2022; Gunsilius, 2020).
Perhaps the closest to our work are shape constraints (Chetverikov et al., 2018), which sharpen PI by
restricting the class of structural or response functions h⋆or H⋆. Examples include positivity (Leamer,

the same vein, we propose known invariances of h⋆as a natural shape constraint.

Variance inflation.
In IV methods, “strong” IVs that induce more exogenous variation in X yield
sharper PI bounds than weak ones (Kilbertus et al., 2020; Padh et al., 2023). So we pose the question:
Can the synthetic exogenous variation introduced by DA likewise sharpen PI?
We answer yes. Fundamentally, PI uncertainty is an attribution problem; the solver must split
observed (X, Y ) covariation between h⋆and U. Only variation in (X, Y ) whose relation to U
is known, e.g. an IV Z, can break the tie. DA adds exactly such variation since T is exogenous
by construction and cannot be attributed to U. This reduces ambiguity in PI, giving sharper, more
informative bounds. This effect is formalized in §4.1, here we give a notion of variance inflation itself.

EXPERIMENTS
We test three claims in finite samples: symmetry-informed PI (i) stays valid, (ii) is sharper and
more informative than baseline PI, and (iii) via DA remains usable where the shape constraint is
not. We compare baseline pı with the shape constraint pı + ınv (§3), DA pre-processing epı (§4.1)
and T-as-IV epı + eıv (§4.2), each an instance of one common PI program (Table 3). To isolate
symmetry-driven sharpening from mere statistical efficiency, the augmented dataset is the same
size as the original. Setup details are in §E.1, and our implementation is publicly available at
https://github.com/uzairakbar/symmetry4CausalBounds.
Optical device.
In Janzing & Schölkopf (2018)’s benchmark, 3 × 3-pixel images X shown on a
screen drive voltage readings Y across a photodiode, while a physical latent U drives two LEDs, one
lighting the webcam that records X, the other the photodiode. Since image orientation should not
affect the irradiance, we augment X by flips, 90◦rotations, and sum-zero translations. We obtain h⋆
by back-door adjustment on U, withheld from all methods. Figure 5 shows that symmetry-informed
bounds stay valid while reducing width and worst-case error, sometimes by an order of magnitude.
US cigarette demand. We give an illustrative example of a textbook symmetry in a canonically
causal setting; scaling all prices and income by a common factor (e.g., inflation) should leave demand
unchanged. Following Baltagi & Levin (1986), we take a panel of 48 US states and DC (1970–2019)
with Y the log cigarette sales per capita and X the state’s own price, cheapest neighbor’s price,
income and the consumer price index (CPI). We assume the latter two are exogenous, whereas the
former two are confounded, with the state’s cigarette tax as a (possibly leaky) instrument Z. With
h⋆(·) := θ⊤
⋆log(·), the symmetry implies 1⊤θ⋆≈0 and DA multiplies X by a random scalar T
with log T Gaussian. Figure 6 shows that imposing the symmetry makes the neighbor-price effect
clearly positive unless the IV is heavily leaky, suggestive evidence of cross-border shopping.

6
DISCUSSION
Limitations. Our proposed framework provides a practical hierarchy of symmetry constraints for PI:
should an invariance shape constraint pose implementation challenges, practitioners can resort to the
DA operationalization. If they suspect misspecification by some ε, they need only to ε-pad bounds.
Which leads us to the main limitation of our work: while the soft intervention framing in Lem. 1
makes composability of DA with a broad class of disparate PI solvers plausible under well-specified
symmetries, it is yet unclear whether this should also be the case under ε-misspecification. Having
addressed this question under two canonical, arguably the most popular PI classes (Asm. 2 bounded
confounding and Asm. 3 leaky IVs), further investigation of additional models is left as future work.
Broader applicability. In principle, symmetry as a shape constraint is compatible with all PI models.
Furthermore, since DA dilutes confounding, it also extends to other bounded-confounding models,
e.g. MSM (Tan, 2006), Wasserstein (Meresht et al., 2022), total-variation (Bertsimas et al., 2023; Guo
et al., 2022) and f-divergence (Jin et al., 2022) balls. All of these, like Asm. 2, admit conditioning on
pre-treatment Z (Cinelli & Hazlett, 2020), omitted here only for clarity of exposition. Under well-
specified symmetry, DA leaves h⋆unchanged, so other shape constraints remain valid. And as DA
preserves the causal graph up to relabeling, Balke & Pearl (1997) style graphical constraints carry over
too; with response-function invariance H⋆(x) =d H⋆(τx), this covers IVs with latent, non-additive
U (Kilbertus et al., 2020), response-function models (Padh et al., 2023), proxy variables (Ghassami
et al., 2026; Mastouri et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2025) and others (Zhang & Bareinboim, 2021).
Related work. Dance & Bloem-Reddy (2025) use symmetry to improve estimation of an identifiable
target, and concurrent work uses symmetry as a point-identification assumption (Weinstein & Blei,
2026; Rabel & Runge, 2026). Counterfactual DA likewise imposes causal priors via augmentation
for point-estimation (Ilse et al., 2021; Mohebbi et al., 2026), or uses causal structure to inform
DA design (Gajewski et al., 2026). All of these are target-identifiable settings; we consider the
weaker setting where point-identification itself fails. Closer to our work is Akbar et al. (2025),
where additive Gaussian DA under a stylized, linear-Gaussian DGP improves non-identifiable point-
estimation. Proposition 1 shows that this is generally fragile, which motivates the PI lens taken here.
Future work. We aim to generalize our change-of-measure framework to (i) general causal queries on
pY | do(x) (Ailer et al., 2024), (ii) equi-variance in the response function H⋆(τx) =d β(x, τ)H⋆(x)
under cocycle β (Dance & Bloem-Reddy, 2025), and (iii) latent U under a ℘-Wasserstein notion of
equi-variance misspecification supx,τ W℘(pH⋆(τx), β(x, τ)#pH⋆(x)) ≤ε (Chen et al., 2020). Also,
it may be interesting to demonstrate application across causal settings like genomics (Weinstein &
Blei, 2026) and medicine (Müller et al., 2021; Elaldi et al., 2023; Slote et al., 2025; Cheema, 2026).
Conclusion. Researchers are advised to incorporate established domain symmetries as a standard
component of their partial-identification toolkit. Integration is effortless with DA pre-processing.
