Abstract 
Uncertainty quantification in machine learning models is essential for nuclear energy applications. 
While machine learning models can make both uncertainty and point predictions, these models are 
often significantly more difficult to train jointly than models that make point predictions alone. 
Split conformal prediction is a family of methods for computing distribution-free prediction 
intervals by calculating nonconformity scores corresponding to a target quantile in a calibration 
dataset. Although conformal prediction only guarantees marginal coverage, normalizing 
nonconformity scores enables predictive interval widths to be calculated on a per-sample basis, 
providing an approximation to conditional coverage. In this work, we connect uncertainty 
predicting neural networks to pre-trained networks that estimate shielding factors in multigroup 
neutron cross sections. We train the uncertainty models on the same OpenMC data used by the 
pre-trained models, whose weights and biases remain fixed. The outputs of the uncertainty models 
are the standard deviations of the residual, separated into contributions from the model and tally 
uncertainty. Their corresponding variances are combined in quadrature to provide a normalizing 
factor for conformal prediction, providing interval widths that are adaptive on a per-sample basis. 
Baselines for comparison are established using Mondrian conformal prediction, and by using the 
ground truth OpenMC uncertainties as normalizers in the conformal framework. We use predicted 
uncertainty interval widths and the severity of predictions that overshoot the interval widths as 
bases for comparing our normalizer with the baselines. We find that our method dramatically 
reduces both interval widths and the degree to which predictions fall outside these widths.

1. Introduction  
Uncertainty quantification (UQ) in modeling and simulation (M&S) of nuclear reactors 
and fuel cycles is well established with a long history and comprehensive literature. While machine 
learning (ML) has shown significant potential and utility in nuclear engineering (NE) applications 
including M&S, UQ for ML in this context has received relatively less attention. To the authors’ 
knowledge the first investigation into UQ in ML for NE specifically was published in 2005 . 
This article described analytical methods for estimating prediction intervals applied to models 
including artificial neural networks (ANNs) in online monitoring of nuclear power plants. 
Although related work in this topic has been published sporadically since 2005, in recent years 
following the mainstream adoption of ML the NE community has increasingly recognized its 
importance. A recent article [2] highlighting the importance of UQ in ML for NE includes a 
comprehensive review of work done in this topic. The article also shows that this topic has received 
recent attention from national and international institutions such as the US Nuclear Regulatory 
Commission (NRC) and the Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development’s (OECD) 
Nuclear Energy Agency (NEA). 
The complexity of the internal mechanisms of many popular ML models such as ANNs 
prevents systematic analysis from achieving complete and certain characterization of their 
accuracy and limits in a straightforward way. The development of UQ methods has nonetheless 
been pursued to provide estimates of prediction confidence for black-box ML models. In published 
NE studies specifically, methods include Monte Carlo dropout [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], Bayesian 
neural networks [3], [6], [7],[8] and Gaussian processes [4], [7], [9], [10]. A disadvantage of these 
UQ methods is that they require incorporation into the ML model architecture before training and 
optimization. Deep ensembles [6], [7], [11], [12], mean-variance estimation [12], and quantile 
regression [13] have also been successfully applied in NE. These uncertainty aware ML models 
can be coupled to a trained, optimized mean predicting model in a subsequent step. Optimizing 
ML models requires significant manual effort and expertise due to the large design space of ANN 
topologies and training hyperparameters. Consequently, the advantage of post-hoc UQ methods is 
that training and optimization for the mean-predicting component does not need to be repeated. 
Additionally, it has been shown that training ML models to make mean and variance predictions 
simultaneously is more difficult than training a variance head in a subsequent step [14], [15]. The 
benefits of post-hoc UQ methods, however, are not the focus of the cited studies. 
Conformal prediction (CP) is another family of UQ methods that can be coupled to a 
previously trained model in general. Split conformal prediction (SCP) enables prediction 
confidence intervals to be calculated in unseen data samples [16], [17]. Importantly, the prediction 
intervals are guaranteed [18] to cover a specific fraction of unseen samples under the assumption 
of exchangeability. This assumption requires that unseen samples for which predictions and 
confidence intervals are sought are drawn from the same underlying distribution that generated a 
calibration data set. Interest in CP methods is emerging in NE, with recent work including UQ in 
void fraction [8] and swelling [19] predictions. A limitation of CP, however, is that in general it 


cannot guarantee coverage of prediction intervals conditioned on individual samples [20]. Instead, 
the guarantee provided by CP is restricted to marginal coverage, where prediction intervals 
correspond to the specified level of coverage only on average [21]. Mondrian conformal prediction 
(MCP) extends SCP by stratifying the calibration data along a chosen axis, guaranteeing coverage 
in each stratum [21], [22]. The limitation of marginal coverage in these CP methods can often be 
mitigated, however, using normalization to allow prediction intervals to vary in width according 
to an estimate of a prediction’s difficulty [22]. A recent study [23] employs normalized CP in the 
context of operator networks for predicting thermal-hydraulic quantities in nuclear reactors. The 
authors use the standard deviation computed by a dropout-ensemble of operator networks as a 
basis for a normalizer to enable adaptive prediction intervals. The normalizer is related to the 
spread in predictions observed by sampling over multiple inferences, adjusting network weights 
in each inference using a Monte Carlo approach.  
Building on work in [23], here we develop a normalizer for CP with per-sample adaptivity 
enabled by two independently trained neural networks that predict the model (epistemic) and data 
(aleatory) uncertainties. These uncertainty neural networks’ (UNNs’) outputs are combined in 
quadrature to provide a normalization factor for CP. Importantly, this method can be coupled with 
previously trained and optimized ML models. We apply this method to previously trained 
backbone neural networks (BNNs) that predict shielding factors in nuclear cross sections. Training, 
validation and testing data were calculated using Monte Carlo (MC) simulations of a light water 
reactor (LWR) pin cell [24]. The BNNs’ input layer encodes the isotopic composition of uranium 
oxide (UOX) fuel for a LWR using scaled concentrations of 90 nuclides. The BNN includes two 
hidden layers, and an output layer consisting of 2,880 neurons representing shielding factors for 4 
reactions, 8 energy groups and 90 nuclides. The uncertainty neural networks (UNNs) are trained 
using the activations calculated by the second hidden layer of the BNNs as input, along with the 
original ground truth training data. We find that our method produces estimates of both model and 
data uncertainty that are robust across BNN models whose mean relative errors (MREs) span 
approximately 1% to 0.1%. Our normalizer allows the predicted interval widths to be significantly 
narrower, simultaneously reduces the severity of overshoots dramatically, while maintaining the 
same level of coverage.

3. Methods 
3.1. Monte Carlo Simulations 
The training data used in this work were generated using 10,000 OpenMC simulations of 
a 2-D light water reactor pin cell with reflective boundary conditions. Figure 1 shows the geometry 
and dimensions of the pin cell which consists of coolant, fuel, cladding, and a helium gap. The 
composition of the fuel included 90 nuclides and was varied in each simulation. The specific 
compositions were determined by first depleting fresh UOX fuel at enrichments of 1.6% to 19.9% 
a/o from 18 to 210 MWd/kgHM. This was done to establish the maximum and minimum expected 


concentrations of each of the 90 nuclides in diverse LWR fuel types. Latin hypercube sampling 
was then used to select a concentration (in atoms/b-cm) of each of the 90 nuclides, with an 
additional 10% added or subtracted to the maximum and minimum. A minimum concentration of 
zero was replaced with a trace amount of 10ିଶଵ a/o. All simulations were done using 10 inactive 
and 90 active criticality cycles with 10,000 neutrons per cycle. Full details of the data generation 
procedure and OpenMC simulations are provided in [24]. 
 
Figure 1. Pin cell Geometry. Periodic boundary conditions were applied to the enclosing 
perimeter around the coolant. 
Each simulation tallied the integrated flux and the total, absorption, total scattering, and 
elastic scattering reaction rates for all 90 nuclides in the CASMO-8 group structure [26]. The MC 
standard deviations were also recorded for the flux and 4 reactions in each group. While these 
reactions were recorded for the fuel, cladding, helium gap, and water moderator, the neural 
networks in this work were trained using data derived from tallies generated only in the fuel. 
Microscopic cross sections were calculated by dividing the fuel tallies by the flux and nuclide 
concentrations. Tally uncertainties were calculated using standard error propagation and verified 
with the Python uncertainties library [27]. A histogram representing the relative uncertainties in 
all microscopic cross sections in the fuel is shown in Figure 2. The uncertainty was on average 
0.43% of the ground truth value with a minimum value of 0.0837% and a maximum of 2.791%.

4.2 Conformal Prediction 
The outputs of the 𝛿-UNN and 𝜎-UNN can be combined according to Eq. (20) to provide 
a normalization factor ui for Eqs. (9,10): 
𝑢௜= ට൫𝛿መ(𝑥௜)ଶ+ [𝜎௢௠௖(𝑥௜)]ଶ൯ 
(20). 
In this section we use un-normalized, i.e. ui = 1, and unstratified, i.e. Eqs. (1-5) CP as a 
baseline against which our normalization method can be compared. Importantly, although the 
unstratified CP method does not partition the data, results are binned for visualization only.  
Figures 9 and 10 show the coverage as a function of the binned self-shielding strength for 
a target coverage of 99% and 95% respectively. Each data point represents the mean coverage 
level in each equal-frequency bin. In these plots data points are often clustered together 
horizontally, which is an effect of equal-frequency binning. The results show that both un-
normalized and normalized CP consistently over-cover the prediction when the shielding is weak, 
while under-covering in the strong shielding region. An ideal level of coverage would track the 
target coverage exactly, but in unstratified CP the coverage guarantee holds only on average across 
all predictions. Consequently, over-coverage in a subset of the predictions must be balanced by 
under-coverage elsewhere. The results also show, however, that using the normalization of Eq. 
(20) partially restores coverage in the high shielding region. The recovery is more pronounced in 
the results corresponding to BNNs with MRE values of 0.25% and greater.  
Figures 11 and 12 show the interval widths as a function of the binned self-shielding 
strength for a target coverage of 99% and 95% respectively. In the un-normalized CP results, the 
widths are necessarily constant because a single width is chosen to provide the target coverage 
over the entire data. The results also show that using normalization dramatically reduces the 
predicted interval widths for the 99% coverage target. For the 95% target, results are more mixed, 
showing clearer improvement only when the BNNs MRE values are 0.5% or greater.

4.3 Stratified Conformal Prediction: Coverage and Interval Widths 
In this section we show coverage levels and predicted interval widths using un-normalized, 
i.e. ui = 1, and normalized, i.e. Eq. (20), CP where both are combined with stratification according 
to Eqs. (7,8). We choose the two most salient stratification axes that are accessible when no ground 
truth is available to show our results. The first axis we choose is the predicted self-shielding 
strength, which is provided by the BNNs, and is the abscissa in Figs. 9-12. Secondly and 
independently, we stratify the data according to the specific output channel of the BNNs. These 
outputs are the cross-section ratios of 4 reactions, 90 isotopes and 8 energy groups giving a total 
of 2,880 strata. Because the calibration data set consists of 1,500 samples, each stratum contains 
sufficient samples to ensure that the partitioning, Eq. (7), yields meaningful quantiles in a statistical 
sense. 
4.3.1 Stratification by predicted self-shielding strength 
Figures 13 and 14 show the coverage as a function of the binned self-shielding strength for 
a target coverage of 99% and 95% respectively. Because the predictions are stratified and plotted 
along the same axis, the coverage is dramatically more balanced than unstratified CP, Figs. 9,10. 
Although the results show that the target coverage levels are met, this should not be interpreted as 
a success of either method, as marginal coverage is guaranteed by construction. Because the CP 
prediction guarantee is in an average sense, individual data points sitting at coverage levels slightly 
higher or lower than the target are expected. For the same reason, little effective difference in 
coverage between the normalized and un-normalized CP is also expected.  
Figures 13 and 14 also suggest that the coverage of both CP methods trends slightly higher 
than the target, particularly for the higher MRE BNNs. This is also expected as the coverage 
condition, Eq. (8), seeks interval widths that equal or exceed the target coverage level. Figures 15 
and 16 show the mean interval width in each self-shielding strength bin for target coverage levels 
of 99% and 95% respectively. These results show that with few exceptions, the normalization 
approach dramatically reduces the predicted interval widths when self-shielding is significant. This 
is particularly pronounced at a 99% coverage target, Fig. 13, where the normalizer increases 
predicted widths only slightly for BNN MRE levels below 0.5% when self-shielding is weak. In 
these cases the widths are already much narrower than at high self-shielding levels, despite the 
magnitude of the BNN prediction 𝑦ො being larger. At 95% coverage the trend remains although the 
effect is not as pronounced. Results from Figs. 15 and 16 are summarized in Table V.

residuals of BNN-0.1 are larger than the MC uncertainties, which reduces the UNNs’ predictive 
ability. These combined effects are the likely causes of the normalizer’s inability to reduce the 
severity of the largest extreme overshoots.

5. Conclusions 
UNNs were trained to predict model and MC uncertainties using the penultimate layer 
activations of pre-trained BNNs as inputs. The BNNs were trained to predict self-shielding factors 
in microscopic multigroup cross sections with target accuracies from 1% to 0.1% MRE. The model 
and MC uncertainties emitted by the UNNs were combined in quadrature to provide a 
normalization factor for split CP and stratified CP. In the latter case the stratification was done 
along the self-shielding strength axis and the output channel. We compared the balance in coverage 


and the predicted interval widths in unnormalized and normalized CP. In the stratified cases we 
also compared the severity of overshoots with and without the normalizer.  
Results from our reliability analysis and standardized residuals show that 𝛿-UNNs make 
predictions of model error that are well-calibrated in the region where the model uncertainty 
dominates. In the MC uncertainty dominated region, however the standardized residuals indicate 
that the 𝛿-UNNs are unable to reliably predict model error in the predictions of BNNs with low 
MRE values. This is due to the model error being unresolvable when the MC error is large, and 
BNN residuals are smaller than the combined model and MC error. The results also reflect the 
limitation in the models’ predictive ability when ground truth uncertainties are propagated 
assuming reaction rate and flux tally uncertainties are independent. The predicted MC uncertainties, 
while well-calibrated, show a relatively large spread, which is also likely due to inaccurate ground 
truth MC uncertainties in the training data. 
Despite these limitations, CP is dramatically improved overall when the predicted 
uncertainties are combined in quadrature and used as a CP normalizer. The effect of the normalizer 
on unstratified CP improves balance in coverage and reduces interval widths significantly, 
particularly for higher MRE BNNs at 99% target coverage. The improvement is modest at 95% 
target coverage and for the more accurate BNNs. This pattern in reduction in interval widths 
remains consistent in stratified CP, particularly when stratification is along self-shielding strength. 
In CP stratified along the output channel, the normalizer reduces interval widths modestly but 
consistently. In almost all cases, the normalizer reduces the severity of BNN predictions 
overshooting the predicted widths. In contrast to the effect the normalizer has on widths, the 
reduction in overshoot severity is consistently better at low BNN MRE values when CP is stratified 
by output channel. 
This work suggests that the proposed approach to normalization in CP is robust across a 
useful range of ANN based point predictor accuracy levels. Because the method can be combined 
with existing point predicting models, evaluating its robustness in different contexts should not 
require significant effort. Because the normalizer’s benefit was only modest at low BNN MRE 
levels, the focus of follow-up studies will be to investigate ways to improve the normalizer for 
accurate BNNs. A clear path towards this is to re-train the BNNs using accurate ground truth 
uncertainties before training the UNNs. OpenMC simulations, however, will need to be repeated 
to access the correlation factors that will enable correct tally uncertainty propagation. Future 
studies should also prioritize investigating the effect of the normalizer under covariate shift. A 
benefit of CP is that coverage remains guaranteed as long as new data points originate from the 
same distribution as the calibration data. It is unclear whether the normalizer will retain its utility 
if new data points are out-of-distribution with respect to the data on which the UNNs were trained.
