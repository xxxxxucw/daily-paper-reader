## Abstract 

Finding which variables cause which others in multivariate time series, and at what lags, is cen-tral to science and policy, yet existing methods force a choice between flexible confounder adjust-ment, data-driven lag selection, and inference that controls the false discovery rate (FDR). ORACLE-VARX does all three in one pipeline. First, dou-ble/debiased machine learning (DML) removes nonlinear confounder effects from the outcomes and the lagged series. Second, adaptive causal lag estimation (ACLE) picks the lag order at each time step by sequential significance tests, tracking regime changes. Third, entry-wise z-tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction select di-rected edges at a target FDR. We prove that in each rolling window, the debiased coefficients are asymptotically normal around a window-averaged target, so their z-tests are asymptotically valid. On a synthetic benchmark with time-varying struc-ture and nonlinear confounding, ORACLE-VARX (LightGBM) tracks the true lag order best (RMSE 

0.96 vs 1.1–1.5), has edge FDR 0.047 , close to PCMCI ( 0.045 ) and below VAR ( 0.129 ) and VAR-LiNGAM ( 0.187 ), and forecasts better than all three. On nine U.S. sector ETFs with macroeco-nomic confounders, it yields interpretable causal graphs whose lag order rises in high-volatility regimes.

Such lag-annotated graphs arise across domains: lead–lag relation-ships among financial assets signal information flow and systemic risk (Lo and MacKinlay, 1990; Billio et al., 2012); climate teleconnections link weather patterns across distant regions (Wallace and Gutzler, 1981); and neural connectiv-ity studies seek directed interactions at millisecond resolu-tion (Brovelli et al., 2004; Seth et al., 2015). In each setting, recovering the causal lag structure is the first step toward reliable prediction, intervention, and policy evaluation. Time series pose challenges that generic causal discov-ery methods do not address. Autocorrelation inflates the effective number of tests, complicating inference. The true lag order may be unknown and time-varying. More-over, exogenous confounders often enter through nonlin-ear channels. Classical Granger causality and VAR meth-ods (Granger, 1969; Sims, 1980; Lütkepohl, 2005) assume a single, fixed lag order and either ignore confounders or in-clude them linearly. Temporal structure-learning methods— PCMCI (Runge et al., 2019), DYNOTEARS (Pamfil et al., 2020), CD-NOTS (Sadeghi et al., 2025), VAR-LiNGAM (Hyvärinen et al., 2010)—each address some of these issues, but none handles all of (a) nonlinear confound-ing, (b) adaptive lag order selection, and (c) FDR-controlled edge discovery with entry-wise confidence intervals. Our contributions are: 1. We introduce DML orthogonalization for VAR co-efficients , applying double/debiased machine learn-ing (Chernozhukov et al., 2018) to residualize both outcomes and treatments against nonlinear confounder effects via flexible first-stage learners and time-series cross-fitting, yielding √T -consistent and asymptoti-cally normal coefficient estimates (Section 3.2). 2. We propose adaptive causal lag estimation (ACLE) ,a two-stage procedure that combines sequential signifi-cance testing across a grid of significance levels with rolling validation-based tuning to select the lag order 

ˆpt at each time step, tracking regime changes in the underlying causal structure without requiring p to be fixed a priori (Section 3.3). 3. We perform FDR-controlled edge discovery via entry-wise z-tests on the debiased coefficients, corrected by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, yielding di-rected causal graphs with false discovery rate control at level q.4. We establish formal guarantees , proving local asymp-totic normality of the debiased coefficients in each rolling window, around a window-averaged target and with valid standard errors (Theorem 1), extending the DML framework of Chernozhukov et al. On nine U.S. sector ETFs with macroeconomic confounders, the ACLE adaptive lag procedure alone achieves an annualized Sharpe ratio of approximately 1.3 on open-to-close returns, a setting where broad market indices earn near-zero returns (Haghani et al., 2024), and the re-sulting causal graphs yield interpretable lead–lag struc-ture whose adaptive lag order rises in high-volatility regimes (Sections 5.1–5.2). 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Granger causality and VAR models. Classical Granger causality (Granger, 1969) tests whether past values of one series improve the prediction of another beyond the target’s own history, and remains the most widely used framework for analyzing lead–lag effects in multivariate time series. Sims (1980) popularized vector autoregressive (VAR) mod-els as a flexible, data-driven tool for macroeconomic analy-sis, and Lütkepohl (2005) codified the statistical theory for estimation, testing, and lag selection—mostly under linear dynamics and a fixed, globally chosen lag order. Sparse VAR extensions handle high-dimensional settings: Basu and Michailidis (2015) analyzed ℓ1-penalized estimation with non-asymptotic guarantees under stability conditions, and Davis et al. (2016) fit sparse VARs in two stages via par-tial spectral coherence. Uematsu and Yamagata (2025) test each lag coefficient of a large VAR with debiased-LASSO statistics and control the FDR across edges, but in a linear, stationary VAR of known order. All of these methods as-sume that confounders enter linearly or are absent, and none adapts the lag order over time. 

Causal discovery for time series. PCMCI (Runge et al., 2019) combines PC-style parent selection with conditional independence tests (optionally nonlinear) that handle auto-correlation, but it neither adapts the active lags over time nor gives lag coefficients with confidence intervals (Ta-ble 2 compares it and VAR-LiNGAM with ours). VAR-LiNGAM (Hyvärinen et al., 2010) uses non-Gaussianity to identify contemporaneous and lagged structure, but assumes linear effects and no hidden confounders. TiMINo (Peters et al., 2013) establishes identifiability under independent noise and nonlinear relationships, but its algorithm rules out feedback loops between series. DYNOTEARS (Pamfil et al., 2020) learns structure by continuous optimization but gives no inference on individual edges. CD-NOTS (Sadeghi et al., 2025) handles nonstationarity by detecting mechanism changes, but assumes every hidden confounder is a smooth Tan, Zanoria, Chen, Lyu, Cucuringu 

function of time. Recent ML-based Granger and conditional independence tests for time series (Angelis et al., 2025; Wieck-Sosa et al., 2025) adjust for confounders flexibly, and Shi and Shah (2026) also search over the lag depth, one pair of series at a time. These tests detect links but give no lag coefficients with confidence intervals. 

Double/debiased machine learning. Robinson (1988) showed that in a partially linear model, nonparametric nui-sance fits (regressions on the confounders) followed by OLS give √T -consistent estimates of the linear part. Cher-nozhukov et al. (2018) generalized this into DML: Neyman orthogonal scores and cross-fitting allow ML first stages that converge fast enough, while keeping asymptotically normal inference. Recent works extend DML to dependent data (Ciganovic et al., 2026; Yu et al., 2026). For high-dimensional VARs, Hecq et al. (2023) and Adamek et al. (2023) give LASSO-based inference with a fixed lag order. Closest to our work, Ballinari and Wehrli (2024) prove DML consistency for impulse response functions under α-mixing, showing that blocked cross-fitting preserves asymptotic va-lidity (see Remark 1). We apply their cross-fitting theory to VAR coefficient matrices and add ACLE and BH-FDR control. Yu et al. (2026) allow vector outcomes and treat-ments; with the lags as treatments and instruments, their estimator reduces to our single-window one. They assume time-invariant nuisance functions; Theorem 1 covers drift-ing ones for our window average. ORACLE-VARX is, to our knowledge, the first to combine nonparametric DML debiasing, adaptive lag discovery, and FDR-controlled edge selection, with a confidence interval per lag coefficient.

Lag selection. ACLE is a heuristic; we do not prove that it selects the true lag. Theorem 1 explains why it tracks the lag order: if Ak = 0 on the window for all lags above some 

p∗, their z-statistics stay of order one, while every lag block with Ak(u0)̸ = 0 has an entry whose |z| grows like √Tw.With a fixed Stage 1 level α, a zero lag is still added with probability bounded away from zero, so α must shrink with 

Tw; if it shrinks slowly enough that its cutoff grows more slowly than √Tw, true lags are still detected. ACLE does not fix α: Stage 2 picks it from a grid by validation, letting the data set the level. A formal consistency result covering the BH step and this data-driven α is left to future work. 

From coefficients to causal edges. Theorem 1 gives valid 

p-values for each entry of ¯Θ (of Ak(u0) if Tw = o(T 2/3)); BH’s FDR guarantee also needs independent or positively dependent (PRDS) p-values (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995; Benjamini and Yekutieli, 2001). Under causal suf-ficiency, meaning every common cause of the series is ob-served in Wt, a zero entry of Ak is equivalent to Granger non-causality at lag k (Proposition A.13, given a non-degeneracy condition). A hidden variable breaks this, be-cause its effect is absorbed into the lag coefficients (Fig-ure 6): a hidden common cause of two series creates a spuri-ous edge, and a hidden mediator makes an indirect link look direct. The coefficients then estimate the best linear lead– lag predictor from the observed variables, not direct causal effects, and their p-values lose the guarantee of Theorem 1. This happens for VAR and ACLE-VAR, which ignore the confounders in our benchmark, and for the ETFs, where many common causes are unobserved (Section 5.2). 

## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate the full method hierarchy from Table 1 on two complementary benchmarks: a synthetic time-varying system with known ground truth (Section 5.1), and a fi-nancial application to ETF markets with macroeconomic confounders (Section 5.2). The synthetic benchmark is our primary experiment, providing controlled evaluation of lag recovery, coefficient estimation, and false positive control. The financial application demonstrates that the framework discovers interpretable causal structure in real data.

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced ORACLE-VARX, a framework for causal lag discovery in multivariate time series whose confounders act nonlinearly and whose lag structure drifts over time. It combines (i) double machine learning, which removes the confounders’ nonlinear effects before the VAR coefficients are estimated, (ii) ACLE, which picks the lag order in each rolling window by sequential significance testing tuned on validation data, and (iii) Benjamini–Hochberg edge selec-tion. We proved that in each rolling window the debiased coefficients are asymptotically normal around a window-averaged target, with valid standard errors, even though the coefficients drift; under causal sufficiency, a zero coefficient means Granger non-causality. We validated it on synthetic data with known ground truth and on nine U.S. sector ETFs. 

## 7 FUTURE WORK 

Three directions stand out. First, a formal consistency re-sult for ACLE, covering the BH step and the validation-chosen level (Section 4). Second, weakening causal suffi-ciency: hidden confounders turn lag coefficients into pre-dictive rather than causal quantities (Figure 6), so richer, high-dimensional confounder sets, or tests that flag hidden confounding, would let more of the discovered edges be read causally. Third, extending the model to a structural VAR (SVAR) with contemporaneous effects (Lütkepohl, 2005) could distinguish direct lagged effects from effects transmitted through same-time links, at the cost of extra identifying assumptions. Remark 2 (Why Causal Sufficiency Matters) . Causal sufficiency is the critical bridge between statistical Granger causality (a statement about prediction) and structural causality (a statement about direct causal mechanisms). Without it, the equivalence in Proposition A.13 can fail. Figure 6 shows the failure (panel b) and a hidden variable that does not cause one (panel c): • Spurious edges (false positives): An unobserved common cause U of both Y (j) and Y (i) induces a statistical association between them. Since U is not in Wt, DML cannot control for it. The estimator attributes U ’s confounding effect to a direct Y (j) → Y (i) link, yielding ˆAk[i, j ]̸ = 0 even when no direct causal relationship exists. • Hidden mediators (indirect edges, not a failure): An unobserved mediator U lies on the sole causal pathway from 

Y (j) to Y (i) (i.e., Y (j) → U → Y (i)), and no direct Y (j) → Y (i) edge exists. The edge is real and Y (j) does Granger-cause Y (i); only reading it as a direct mechanism would be wrong.

## E USE OF AI IN THE THEORY 

The result to prove came from the authors: we needed a locally stationary version of the DML central limit theorem, and expected its proof to follow the DML argument of Chernozhukov et al. (2018), with extra error terms from the time series (the drift of the coefficients inside the window, and dependence handled through martingale differences). The proofs in Appendix A were then produced by a multi-agent setup: Claude models (Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1, by Anthropic) drafted the proofs and checked each other’s drafts adversarially, and Codex (by OpenAI, with the Astra model) gave an independent adversarial review. Opus was instructed to write the proofs so that they spell out every step and skip none, so that a reader can verify each step. The one external theorem used, the martingale central limit theorem, was checked against the printed page of Billingsley (1995).
