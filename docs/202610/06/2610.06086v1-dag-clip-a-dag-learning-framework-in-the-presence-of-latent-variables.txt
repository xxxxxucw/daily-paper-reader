# DAG-CLIP: A DAG Learning Framework in the Presence of Latent Variables 

Siliang Zhang 1, Yunxiao Chen 2, and Irini Moustaki 21School of Statistics, East China Normal University 

> 2

Department of Statistics, London School of Economics and Political Science 

Abstract 

Learning directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) to uncover causal mechanisms has attracted substantial attention in machine learning. While most existing methods focus exclusively on observed variables, many variables of substantive interest are latent constructs defined by statistical measurement models. Such constructs are particularly common in the social and behavioral sciences. In this paper, we propose a general statistical framework for DAG learning when some or all nodes of the graph are latent variables, accommodating non-Gaussian manifest variables such as binary and categorical data in the measurement model. To overcome the computational burden of high-dimensional integration in standard marginal likelihoods, we develop DAG learning via Composite-Likelihood-based screening and Iterative Pruning (DAG-CLIP), a two-step composite-likelihood-based learning algorithm. This algorithm first solves a smooth acyclicity-constrained optimization problem to screen out misspecified DAG structures, and subsequently performs BIC-guided composite-likelihood backward deletion within the Markov equivalence class (MEC) space to identify a sparse DAG. We establish the statistical consistency of the algorithm in recovering the MEC of the true DAG, and demonstrate its effectiveness through extensive simulation studies and a real-world application to large-scale educational survey data. 

Keywords: Latent variables, causal discovery, Markov equivalence class, pairwise likelihood, composite-likelihood, NOTEARS

1.1 Background 

Directed graphical models characterize dependencies among variables through a directed graph. A prominent subclass uses directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), which are well suited to representing causal relationships (see Chapter 2, Pearl, 2009). Consequently, there is great interest in learning DAGs to uncover underlying causal mechanisms. This task, known as causal discovery, has received widespread attention across machine learning, statistics, and data science (see, e.g., Spirtes et al., 2000; Chickering, 2002b; Kalisch and B¨ uhlmann, 2007; Zheng et al., 2018; Yuan et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024). A key limitation of most existing DAG learning algorithms is their strict requirement that all variables of interest (i.e., DAG nodes) must be directly observed. However, in many applications, especially those in social and behavioral research, many variables of substantive interest are not directly observable. Examples include personality traits, cognitive abilities, mental health status, and political attitudes. These constructs are typically formulated as latent variables and defined through a statistical measurement model—a generative model that specifies the relationship between the latent factors and their manifest indicators. Depending on the data type of the manifest indicators, this measurement model may take different forms, such as a linear factor model (Chapter 7, Bollen, 1989), including its underlying-variable formulation for binary and ordinal indicators (Muth´ en, 1984), or an item response theory (IRT) model (Chen et al., 2025). In recent years, several methods have been proposed for DAG learning in which the nodes are continuous latent variables. Most of them learn the measurement model from data by first identifying groups of manifest variables that each measure a single latent variable and then learning the structure among the identified latent variables (Silva et al., 2006; Kummerfeld and Ramsey, 2016; Cai et al., 2019; Xie et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2022), or by score-based search (Ng et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2026). These approaches mainly consider continuous manifest variables, although recent rank-based methods also accommodate discrete variables (Dong et al., 2025). In these approaches, the measurement structure between latent variables and their observed indicators is learned from data rather than specified in advance. Methods that take the measurement model as given either allow a single indicator per latent variable (Luo et al., 2021; Castelletti, 2024) or, as in the MIMBuild algorithm (Spirtes et al., 2000; Silva et al., 2006), assume a linear factor model with continuous manifest variables. Our focus is on a prespecified (confirmatory) measurement structure that allows each latent variable to be measured by multiple categorical or mixed-type indicators, a common pattern in social and behavioral research. Moreover, the computational cost is high when the measurement model is non-Gaussian, as the likelihood involves an integral over the latent variables that 2has no closed form. We refer the readers to Section 1.3 for further discussion. 

1.2 Contributions 

This paper addresses the gaps mentioned above in existing DAG learning algorithms when latent variables are present. The contribution is fourfold: 1. We propose a general modeling framework for DAG learning, when some or all nodes of the graph are latent variables. Specifically, we assume that the latent variables follow a multivariate normal distribution with a covariance matrix induced by a DAG. Meanwhile, we impose minimal restrictions on the measurement model, thereby allowing virtually any specification, subject to standard smoothness and identifiability requirements for the unknown model parameters. 2. We develop a computationally efficient two-step algorithm with statistical guarantees to learn the latent DAG. The first step screens the space of DAGs using a sparsity-inducing penalty and returns a DAG compatible with the true latent distribution with probability tending to one. The second step removes the spurious edges by a backward search over Markov equivalence classes (MECs). The two steps rely on four technical tools. First, we employ a pairwise composite likelihood (Lindsay, 1988; Varin et al., 2011; Katsikatsou et al., 2012; Katsikatsou and Moustaki, 2016) as part of the objective function in both steps. A challenge brought by the extended measurement model is that the likelihood function involves an integral over all latent variables, which admits a closed-form solution only for the Gaussian linear factor model. When the number of latent variables is moderately large, evaluating the likelihood function repeatedly during graph search becomes computationally prohibitive. The pairwise likelihood addresses this challenge. In particular, each term of the pairwise likelihood involves an integral of dimension at most two, regardless of the number of latent variables. It therefore avoids the high-dimensional integration required by the marginal likelihood. Second, the screening step imposes sparsity through a truncated ℓ1 penalty, which is a continuous surrogate for the ℓ0 penalty. At the global solution of the penalized problem, this penalty yields consistent selection of the sparse structure (Shen et al., 2012; Yuan et al., 2019), a property that the ℓ1 penalty does not guarantee (van de Geer and B¨ uhlmann, 2013). The penalized problem is nonconvex and is solved by difference-of-convex programming (Le Thi and Pham Dinh, 2018) through a sequence of weighted ℓ1 problems. Third, the smooth acyclicity constraint of NOTEARS (Zheng et al., 2018), originally developed for fully observed variables, turns the combinatorial search over DAGs into a continuous optimization problem in which all candidate edges are updated jointly. Fourth, a composite likelihood Bayesian information criterion 3(CLBIC; Gao and Song, 2010) constructed from the same pairwise likelihood guides the pruning step. Based on these tools, we name the proposed algorithm DAG-CLIP (DAG learning via Composite-Likelihood-based screening and Iterative Pruning). Both screening and pruning are important for effective DAG learning. While it is possible to learn a DAG using only the pruning step by starting from a fully connected DAG, as in Dong et al. (2026), this approach has two major drawbacks. First, the computational burden of iterative pruning can be prohibitive because a dense DAG generates many search candidates per iteration. Second, the expanded search space increases the statistical error introduced by multiple comparisons when using the CLBIC. We can also learn a DAG by running only the screening step. In fact, this approach can be shown to consistently recover the MEC of the true sparse DAG. However, this guarantee requires a sufficiently accurate approximation to the global maximum of a highly nonconvex objective, which may be difficult to obtain in practice. Our simulations in Section 5 show that screening alone often retains spurious edges, and they are only eliminated in the pruning steps. The pruning step permits consistent selection under a weaker numerical accuracy condition on the screening solution. This learning strategy of integrating these two steps is modular, meaning it can be seamlessly combined with other suitable objective functions for DAG learning, such as the marginal likelihood under Gaussian DAG models. 3. We show that the proposed algorithm can consistently recover the MEC of the true DAG under a numerical accuracy condition on the screening solution that is weaker than the condition used to establish consistency of screening alone. This result aligns with standard statistical consistency results for DAG learning from observational data without latent variables (e.g., Chickering, 2002b; Kalisch and B¨ uhlmann, 2007; Nandy et al., 2018). That is, without additional assumptions or experimental interventions, the underlying DAG is identifiable only up to its MEC. 4. Finally, we empirically evaluate the proposed method through extensive simulations and an application to large-scale educational survey data. Our simulations compare DAG-CLIP with its screening step alone and with two-step methods that first fit the measurement model and then apply the PC algorithm (Spirtes et al., 2000) or greedy equivalence search (Chickering, 2002b) to estimated factor scores or latent correlations, demonstrating that the proposed approach substantially outperforms these competitors across diverse settings. Furthermore, we apply the method to the Australian Grade 8 public-use data from the 2023 Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) (von Davier et al., 2024) to learn a DAG involving 14 latent variables and 2 observed variables, where the latent constructs are measured 4by 69 manifest indicators comprising both binary and ordinal types. The resulting structure yields highly interpretable insights. In addition, with fewer than half of the possible adjacencies, it predicts held-out responses nearly as well as the saturated model. 

1.3 Related Literature 

This work connects to several key areas in machine learning and statistics. We organize the review around three topics, namely DAG learning of observed variables, DAG learning in the presence of latent variables, and composite likelihood. When all relevant variables are directly observed, DAG learning methods typically focus on recovering the DAG up to its MEC. They may be classified into three broad groups. First, constraint-based methods decide adjacencies and orientations through hypothesis tests of conditional independence. A representative method is the PC algorithm (Spirtes et al., 2000; Kalisch and B¨ uhlmann, 2007), while other methods have also been developed in this direction, such as conservative PC (Ramsey et al., 2006), rank PC for nonparanormal data (Harris and Drton, 2013), and the order-independent PC-stable (Colombo and Maathuis, 2014). Second, score-based methods select a graph by optimizing a model-selection criterion over graphs or equivalence classes. Examples include greedy equivalence search (Chickering, 2002b), which builds on the classical selection consistency of the BIC (Haughton, 1988), and penalized likelihood formulations with the ℓ0 penalty or its nonconvex surrogates (van de Geer and B¨ uhlmann, 2013; Aragam and Zhou, 2015; Yuan et al., 2019). In particular, Yuan et al. (2019) reconstruct a Gaussian DAG by a constrained likelihood with truncated ℓ1 constraints. Because these optimization problems are nonconvex, obtaining the solutions required by the statistical guarantees can be difficult in practice. Finally, continuous-optimization methods express acyclicity through smooth constraints or penalties and update all candidate edges together through gradient-based optimization (Zheng et al., 2018; Yu et al., 2019; Ng et al., 2020; Bello et al., 2022). These methods commonly use an ℓ1-type penalty to impose sparsity. The proposed method can be seen as a hybrid of methods from the last two groups. Its screening step uses continuous optimization with a truncated ℓ1 penalty, and its pruning step uses a score-based search. Methods have also been developed to distinguish the true DAG from the rest of its MEC by incorporating additional assumptions or prior information. Examples include linear models with non-Gaussian errors (Shimizu et al., 2006; Zhao et al., 2022), Gaussian models with equal error variances (Peters and B¨ uhlmann, 2014; Chen et al., 2019), parametric ordinal models (Ni and Mallick, 2022; Ni et al., 2025), and methods that use interventional data or instrumental variables (Ellis and Wong, 2008; Peng et al., 2020; Li et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Taeb et al., 2024). 5As mentioned earlier, DAG learning in the presence of latent variables has received increasing attention in recent years. Methods can be divided into two groups based on the role of the latent variables. In the first group, the latent variables are unobserved confounders, and the graph nodes remain the observed variables. Spirtes et al. (2000); Colombo et al. (2012) proposed the FCI algorithm and its variants, which learn an ancestral graph over the observed variables in the presence of unobserved confounders, and Chen et al. (2021) developed an exact score-based method of this type using integer programming. In the second group, the latent variables are treated as nodes in the DAG, and methods differ on whether the measurement structure, i.e., the relationships between the latent variables and the observed indicators, is learned from data or specified in advance. When the measurement structure is learned, the assignment of observed variables to latent variables is estimated together with the graph. Most methods first identify groups of observed variables that each measure a single latent variable by testing constraints on the observed covariance matrix, and then learn the structure among the latent variables (Silva et al., 2006; Kummerfeld and Ramsey, 2016; Cai et al., 2019; Xie et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2022). Score-based methods have also been developed for this problem (Ng et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2026). In particular, Dong et al. (2026) propose a latent variable greedy equivalence search that starts from a fully connected graph and removes edges by a backward search. Dong et al. (2025) develop a mixed data permutation-based rank test (MPRT) for covariance-rank constraints with discretized measurements and demonstrate its use in latent causal discovery. In these methods, latent variables are defined by the estimated grouping of indicators rather than by substantive theory, so their interpretation can sometimes be unclear. When the measurement model is specified in advance, the method learns only the structure among the latent variables. This is the setting of the proposed method. In the simplest case, each latent variable is measured by a single observed variable, as in latent Gaussian DAG models for ordinal and mixed-type data (Luo et al., 2021; Castelletti, 2024). These models are not flexible enough to include latent variables each measured by multiple indicators. These methods involve optimization based on marginal likelihood functions that involve high-dimensional integrals and tackle the optimization using either Monte Carlo Expectation-Maximization or full Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithms, which are computationally intensive. When each latent variable is measured by several indicators with a given measurement structure, the MIMBuild algorithm (Spirtes et al., 2000; Silva et al., 2006) learns the DAG of the latent variables either by applying the PC algorithm to conditional independence tests derived from rank constraints on the covariance matrix of the indicators, or by greedy equivalence search under a Gaussian likelihood fitted by an EM algorithm. It is developed for linear models with continuous indicators and does not accommodate categorical responses. DAG learning with discrete latent variables has also 6been studied, as in Gu and Xu (2019), Liu et al. (2022), Lee and Gu (2024), and Zhang et al. (2026). Finally, we review the literature on composite likelihood, which appears in the objective function of the proposed method. Composite likelihood is an inference function formed by multiplying a collection of component likelihoods (Lindsay, 1988; Varin et al., 2011). In this work, we employ a variant known as pairwise likelihood, constructed from bivariate margins. This approach has been widely used to estimate high-dimensional latent variable models due to its computational advantages over the standard marginal likelihood (Katsikatsou et al., 2012; Katsikatsou and Moustaki, 2016; Alfonzetti et al., 2025a,b). However, to the best of our knowledge, no existing work uses the pairwise likelihood or other composite likelihood to score and select DAG structures. Model selection under a composite likelihood can be performed using CLBIC (Gao and Song, 2010), a criterion whose penalty function uses an estimated model degree of freedom—rather than simply the number of unknown parameters—to quantify model complexity. The proposed algorithm leverages this criterion during the iterative pruning step. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the modeling framework and discuss the identifiability of the DAG. Section 3 presents the technical tools and the DAG-CLIP algorithm. Section 4 establishes the consistency of DAG-CLIP. Sections 5 and 6 present simulation studies and an application to the TIMSS 2023 data. Section 7 concludes with a discussion of the limitations and future directions. Proofs of the theoretical results, computational details, and additional details of the simulation study and the application are provided in the supplementary material, which is available from the authors upon request.

# 3 Proposed Method 

The proposed DAG-CLIP has two steps. The first step performs a screening in the space of DAGs with sufficient sparsity and returns a DAG and its MEC. As shown in Section 4, with high probability, this DAG induces Φ ∗ but may contain spurious edges. The second step removes spurious edges by searching backwards over the space of MECs. These two steps rely on four technical tools, including (1) a pairwise likelihood that is involved in the objective functions in both steps, (2) a truncated ℓ1 penalty, which is used in the first step to impose sparsity on the search space, (3) a smooth acyclicity constraint, which is incorporated into the objective function in the first step, and (4) a composite likelihood Bayesian information criterion that guides the pruning step. We introduce these tools in Section 3.1 and then describe the two steps in Sections 3.2 and 3.3, respectively.

# 5 Simulation Studies 

This section examines the finite-sample performance of the proposed DAG-CLIP. The study evaluates latent CPDAG recovery from correlated ordinal manifest variables across three graph families, two latent-dimension sizes, and two sample sizes. We compare DAG-CLIP with its screening step alone (DAG-CLIP (0) ), without pruning and three two-step baselines (screening and pruning). 

5.1 Design and Evaluation 

Each simulation draws the true DAG from one of the three graph families mentioned below with m latent variables. (1) The random family places m edges on uniformly sampled node pairs oriented by a random topological order. (2) The star family directs ⌈(m − 1) /2⌉

18 edges into a randomly chosen hub. (3) The tree family arranges the m nodes in a balanced out-tree with every edge directed away from the root. These three families cover sparse random structures, a concentrated hub, and a deep hierarchy, respectively, and they stress adjacency recovery and orientation to different degrees. The nonzero coefficients of B are drawn independently and uniformly from [ −2.0, −0.5] ∪ [0 .5, 2.0]. The latent variables are then rescaled to unit variance as in Section 2, which does not change the graph or its CPDAG. Each latent variable is measured by three ordinal manifest variables, each with five categories, following the graded response model of Example 1, with item-specific loadings and thresholds that yield equal category probabilities. The simulation design fully crosses 

m ∈ { 20 , 40 }, (hence p ∈ { 60 , 120 }), with n ∈ { 2000 , 4000 }, resulting in four design cells with 100 replications each. We apply all five methods to the same generated data within each replication. Within each replication, the screening step is evaluated across truncation levels τ ∈ { 0.02 , 0.05 , 0.10 }, using six starting values - five random and one from a Gaussian fit to the estimated latent correlation matrix. We use the screened DAG whose CPDAG has the smallest CLBIC for the pruning step. Specifically, we compare five methods. (1) DAG-CLIP is the proposed method with the settings described above. (2) DAG-CLIP (0) is the screening step of DAG-CLIP alone, included to isolate the contribution of the pruning step. The remaining three methods are two-step procedures: they first fit the graded response model by pairwise maximum likelihood, under the same assignment of manifest to latent variables and with the latent correlation matrix left unrestricted, and then apply a structure learning method for the observed variables. (3) Factor-score GES (FS-GES) obtains regression factor scores and replaces each underlying response by its conditional mean given the observed category under the fitted parameters, and then applies greedy equivalence search (Chickering, 2002b) with the Gaussian BIC to the resulting n×m matrix of scores. (4) Latent-correlation GES (LC-GES) applies the same search procedure directly to the estimated latent correlation matrix, treating it as the sample covariance matrix of a Gaussian sample of size n. (5) Factor-score PC (FS-PC) applies the PC algorithm (Spirtes et al., 2000) to the factor scores, using Fisher-z tests at level 0 .01. Each test checks whether two factor scores are independent after adjusting for up to four other factor scores. Each baseline treats an estimated summary of the measurement model as observed data. Further details of the simulation design and the baseline methods are given in the supplementary material. We evaluate the five methods above, including the proposed method, using five common accuracy measures from causal structure learning. All five measures compare the estimated and true CPDAGs. # 6 Application to TIMSS 2023 

6.1 Data and Graph Nodes 

The Trends in International Mathematics and Science Study (TIMSS) is an international assessment administered by the International Association for the Evaluation of Educational Achievement (IEA) that aims to measure how well students in Year 4 and Year 8 have mastered the knowledge they are expected to learn as part of the mathematics and science curriculum. The TIMSS questionnaire also collects information on various aspects of education, such as home resources, attitudes, and classroom experiences (von Davier et al., 2024). Overall, the availability of constructs and background variables in TIMSS provides many opportunities for education research. We focus on fourteen latent constructs measured by the TIMSS student questionnaire and two observed achievement scores. Broadly, these variables cover mathematics- and science-specific achievement; attitudes and motivation; classroom and instructional environment; school climate and social experiences; home resources and student background context; and digital literacy. Contrary to testing specific education hypotheses, our analysis, which is enabled by our proposed framework, is exploratory and focuses on two research questions. The first asks which relationships among the sixteen graph nodes are represented by adjacencies in the fitted graph (in other words, which edges exist). The second asks which of their directions are shared by all DAGs in the selected MEC (for which directions there is compelling evidence). These are the adjacencies and the directed edges of the CPDAG introduced in Section 2. We answer these two questions by implementing the proposed methodology in building the CPDAG. Because language translations, item parameters, curricula and sampling designs differ across education systems, we analyze data from one country, specifically Australia, Grade 8 public-use data of TIMSS 2023 (IEA, 2024; Fishbein et al., 2025; von Davier et al., 2024). The analysis consists of 7,997 students from 276 schools who responded to the context questionnaire. Table 3 lists the 16 graph nodes. Fourteen latent constructs are measured by 69 questionnaire items (each construct is measured by a small block of binary or ordered categorical items). Specifically, four constructs describe general resources and experiences, and two parallel groups of five constructs describe the mathematics and the science learning 23 context. The remaining two nodes are mathematics and science achievement. For the achievement constructs, TIMSS reports achievement through five plausible values per student (von Davier et al., 2024). For each student, we average the five plausible values into one score, standardize it and enter it as an observed node as in Example 4. Regarding the remaining constructs, the item blocks follow the published TIMSS scale assignments, so each item measures a single construct as assumed in Section 2. To keep the number of item pairs manageable, we fit a one-factor probit model to each block and retain the five items with the largest loadings. Home Educational Resources has only four items, and we retain all of them. Every latent construct therefore has at least three indicators and satisfies the identifiability conditions discussed in Sections 2 and 3.1. The supplementary material describes the item blocks and their coding. For model fitting and testing, we randomly split students into a design sample (20%), a fitting sample (60%), and a test sample (20%). We use the design sample to merge sparse categories, select items from each item block through the one-factor model as described above, and choose one anchor item per construct (set the loading of one item in each block of items to one to fix the scale of the latent variables). Item response categories are coded so that a higher response category indicates a higher position on the corresponding construct. The analysis includes three binary items, 66 ordered polytomous items with 3 to 5 response categories, and two achievement scores. Since all item indicators within a block of items measure the same construct and are positively correlated, all loadings are positive. We use the fitting sample to estimate the model and select the graph with DAG-CLIP, and the test sample only for out-of-sample evaluation. The analysis uses complete cases, as the retained items have nonresponse rates between one and five per cent. We treat the resources and experience constructs that capture students’ home and personal resources as well as broader school experiences as background conditions for subject-specific learning contexts and achievement. We therefore exclude arrows from achievement and learning-context constructs into this background group, while leaving within-group directions unrestricted. The target is a MEC consistent with this restriction. No other directions are constrained in the search. The selected orientations are discussed in Section 6.3.

In this work, we addressed the challenge of learning DAGs with latent constructs measured by non-Gaussian manifest variables, which are common in social and behavioral research. To avoid the heavy computational burden of traditional marginal likelihoods, we developed DAG-CLIP, a novel two-step composite-likelihood-based framework. The algorithm replaces the marginal likelihood with a pairwise likelihood, thereby avoiding high-dimensional in-tegrals. It first uses a smooth acyclicity-constrained optimization approach to screen out misspecified DAG structures, and then applies an information-criterion-guided backward-deletion procedure within the Markov equivalence class space to yield a sparse, refined causal graph. We established the theoretical consistency of DAG-CLIP in recovering the true Markov equivalence class, while extensive simulations and an application to large-scale educational survey data confirmed its practical effectiveness and scalability in handling complex measurement models. The work can be extended in several directions. First, the current paper assumes unidimensional measurement models, i.e., each manifest variable is conditionally independent of the other latent variables given the single latent variable it measures. We make this assumption for ease of exposition, not as a technical requirement. In fact, as shown in Katsikatsou et al. (2012) and Katsikatsou and Moustaki (2016), the pairwise likelihood function applies to complex multidimensional measurement designs, and the DAG-CLIP method can be extended to such settings. Second, the NOTEARS acyclicity constraint can be replaced by alternatives, such as the log-determinant constraint proposed in Bello et al. (2022). These alternatives preserve asymptotic consistency under the same regularity and numerical accuracy conditions, but may yield different finite-sample performance. Third, prior knowledge may sometimes be available about causal directions among a subset of variables. DAG-CLIP can easily incorporate such knowledge as constraints in both the screening and iterative pruning steps. Finally, the method can be extended to exploratory settings where the relationship between manifest and latent variables is unknown and must be learned from data (e.g., Silva et al., 2006; Xie et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2022; Dong et al., 2026). In this case, one may combine recent representation learning methods (Rohe and Zeng, 2023; Cui et al., 2026) and the proposed DAG-CLIP method to simultaneously define the latent variables via manifest variables and learn their causal relationships. 28 References
