## ABSTRACT 

Tabular foundation models (TFMs) outperform established machine learning models on tabular benchmarks through in-context learning. Building on this success, interest is growing in apply-ing them to data streams, where data arrive continuously and evolve over time. On a stream, a TFM adapts by updating its context rather than its parameters, so its accuracy and cost depend on which examples it keeps and how often it rebuilds its context. We therefore present a systematic study of TFMs on data streams, covering memory management, computational cost, and stream-specific challenges such as concept drift and delayed labels. We find that TFMs achieve the highest predictive performance, and that simply retaining the most recent examples is as effective as existing memory management techniques. They also recover faster than streaming learners after drift and keep the highest accuracy under label delay. This accuracy, however, comes at a high serving cost, since a nearly unchanged context is re-encoded at every prediction. These results point to architectural efficiency as the way forward for in-context stream learning. 

## 1 Introduction 

Data streams arise across a wide range of tabular applications, from fraud and intrusion detection to sensor monitoring and clinical decision support (Dal Pozzolo et al., 2014; Shyaa et al., 2024; Arora et al., 2024). Unlike batch learning, stream learning must predict and adapt continuously under bounded memory and time, while the data-generating distribution changes (Gama et al., 2014; Bifet et al., 2018). Numerous stream learners have been proposed, from incrementally grown trees and drift-triggered ensembles (Domingos & Hulten, 2000; Gomes et al., 2017, 2019; Bifet et al., 2010b) to online AutoML systems that adapt the whole pipeline (Celik et al., 2023; Kulbach et al., 2022; Verma et al., 2024), each adapting by updating model parameters as data arrives. Tabular foundation models (TFMs) offer a different route, predicting from labelled examples held as context in a single forward pass without updating parameters (M¨ uller et al., 2022; Hollmann et al., 2025), so adaptation to drift is handled entirely by updating the context memory. Recent approaches such as DualFIFO (Lourenc ¸o et al., 2026) and CURE (Lee et al., 2026) exploit this property and report large gains over streaming ensembles under a prequential protocol (Gama et al., 2013). However, their evaluations are narrow in the streams and costs they cover, leaving no controlled account of how backbone, memory, label delay, and drift jointly determine predictive performance and serving cost. To close this gap, in this paper we systematically study TFMs on data streams. Our contributions are as follows: • We compare TFM backbones against streaming learners and streaming AutoML systems under the same protocol on real and synthetic streams with different types of drift, and find that TFMs are the most accurate but also the most costly learners (Figure 1). • We conduct a stream-specific analysis of memory composition, memory size, label delay, context refresh rate, and query batching, and find that backbone quality and memory size dominate predictive performance, while the memory management policy matters mainly under delayed labels. arXiv:2610.05352v1 [cs.LG] 4 Oct 2026 Rethinking Tabular Foundation Models On Data Streams A P REPRINT

## 2 Background 

Data streams. A data stream is an unbounded sequence of examples (xt, y t) arriving one at a time, where xt ∈ X is a feature vector and yt ∈ Y its class label. Each example is drawn from a distribution pt that may change over time, and such a change is a concept drift , which may be abrupt, gradual, incremental, or recurring (Gama et al., 2014; Lu et al., 2019). A stream classifier must use bounded memory and time per example, be ready to predict at any time, and adapt when pt changes (Bifet et al., 2018). It is evaluated prequentially (test-then-train), in which each example first tests the current model and only then becomes available for learning (Gama et al., 2013), and in practice its label may arrive only after a delay of d examples (Grzenda et al., 2020). Most stream learners meet these requirements by updating the model as data arrives. Hoeffding trees grow incrementally from sufficient statistics (Domingos & Hulten, 2000; Bifet & Gavald` a, 2009), drift detectors such as ADWIN trigger a response when the distribution shifts (Bifet & Gavald` a, 2007), and ensembles such as Adaptive Random Forest, Streaming Random Patches, and Leveraging Bagging replace degraded members (Gomes et al., 2017, 2019; Bifet et al., 2010b). SAM-kNN instead retains past examples in short-and long-term memories (Losing et al., 2016), and is the classical learner closest to context-based adaptation. Online AutoML extends adaptation to the whole pipeline, re-running a search on detected drift (Celik et al., 2023), evolving a population of pipelines (Kulbach et al., 2022), or searching around the current best configuration (Verma et al., 2024). 

Tabular foundation models. A tabular foundation model (TFM) is a transformer pretrained once on a large number of synthetic tables (Hollmann et al., 2023; Google Research, 2026). At prediction time, a context Dctx = {(xi, y i)}ni=1 

of labelled rows and a query x∗ pass through a single forward pass, ˆp(y | x∗, D ctx ) = fθ (x∗, D ctx ), and on small and medium tables TFMs now match or exceed tuned gradient-boosted trees (Erickson et al., 2025). TabPFN lets every context row attend to every other row directly (Hollmann et al., 2025), while TabICL first embeds each row from its columns and then lets rows attend to one another (Qu et al., 2026), and TabFM alternates attention over columns and rows before attending over compressed row vectors (Google Research, 2026), so in all cases encoding the context costs time quadratic in n. Drift-Resilient TabPFN (Helli et al., 2024) is the one backbone pretrained for shift, on temporally shifting priors with a known time covariate. Context construction has been studied for fixed tables, either by retrieving rows per query (Thomas et al., 2024) or by compressing and pruning the context (Feuer et al., 2024), but all assume a fixed pool of rows drawn from a single distribution. 

Streaming TFMs. In a stream, the context becomes a memory Mt of at most B labelled rows that must be main-tained as rows keep arriving, trading recent rows that allow fast adaptation against older rows that preserve earlier concepts (Lourenc ¸o et al., 2026). The closest prior works (Appendix E) are DualFIFO (Lourenc ¸o et al., 2026), which splits the memory into a short-term FIFO buffer and a class-balanced long-term store, and CURE (Lee et al., 2026), which evicts redundant rows to keep the memory informative. Their evaluations leave several gaps open. First, neither compares performance across drift types, since DualFIFO is evaluated only on real-world streams, where drift type is unknown, and CURE includes a single synthetic stream, with abrupt drift. Second, neither places serving cost beside that of classical learners. Because Mt and Mt+1 differ by a single row, every step re-encodes almost the same set of rows, yet DualFIFO times ten instances per forward pass and CURE compares its per-step runtime only with Dual-FIFO. Third, both assume that each label arrives immediately, although labels in deployed streams often arrive late. ## 3 Experimental setup 

Streams. The evaluation covers real and synthetic classification streams (Table 1). Several real streams are distributed by the USP data stream repository (Souza et al., 2020b). Only datasets whose row order reflects the order in which observations arrived are included, since an arbitrary file order does not represent a true stream. The synthetic streams use data generators with abrupt, gradual, incremental, and recurring drift, the shapes drift takes over time (Lu et al., 2019). As each drift type is paired with a different generator, they serve only to describe the stream conditions and not to compare them directly. 

Methods. The comparison covers dedicated streaming learners, streaming AutoML, and in-context TFMs. Classical streaming learners include Hoeffding Adaptive Tree (Bifet & Gavald` a, 2009), Adaptive Random Forest (Gomes et al., 2017), Streaming Random Patches (Gomes et al., 2019), Leveraging Bagging (Bifet et al., 2010b), and SAM-kNN (Losing et al., 2016). Streaming AutoML methods include ASML (Verma et al., 2024), EvoAutoML (Kulbach et al., 2022), and OAML (Celik et al., 2023). In-context TFMs include CURE (Lee et al., 2026), DualFIFO (Lourenc ¸o et al., 2026), and FIFO-based variants of TabICL v2 (Qu et al., 2026), TabPFN v1 (Hollmann et al., 2023), Drift-Resilient TabPFN (Helli et al., 2024), and TabFM (Google Research, 2026), where TabPFN v1 is also the backbone of DualFIFO and Drift-Resilient TabPFN. These TFMs span different backbone designs, pretraining strategies, and model scales, from models with tens of millions of parameters to a billion-scale backbone, allowing us to examine architecture, scale, and drift-oriented pretraining. No-Change and Majority-Class serve as simple reference baselines. Following established evaluation practice (Erickson et al., 2025; Lourenc ¸o et al., 2026), all methods use the settings and implementations described in their original work, with streaming learners and streaming AutoML methods running on CPU and TFMs on GPU. 

Evaluation protocol and metrics. All methods follow the same prequential protocol, where each arriving instance is first predicted and becomes available for learning only after a label delay of d instances (Gama et al., 2013; Grzenda et al., 2020). Memory-based methods retain at most B labelled examples, and unless varied explicitly, all runs have no label delay d and share the same budget B across memory-based methods. We report accuracy together with balanced accuracy, which weights classes equally, and the chance-corrected statistics Cohen’s κ, which adjusts for agreement expected by chance (Cohen, 1960), and temporal κt, which adjusts for the accuracy of the No-Change baseline (Bifet et al., 2015). Latency is reported in milliseconds per instance, measured as wall-clock serving time on each method’s own host, and memory growth per instance. To support reproducibility, we release the full experimental pipeline used in this study, including the code, run config-urations for every experiment, and the raw result files behind all reported tables and figures. 1

In-context methods occupy the most accurate and expensive end of the comparison, with the additional predictive gains requiring more than an order of magnitude higher latency than the cheapest ensemble, even though the TFMs run on GPUs. Context refresh rate and query batching. Deployment levers can spread the cost of encoding the memory over more than one prediction, one across time and one across queries. Refreshing the context every K instances while still predicting on every instance saves only a small share of latency at the first interval with little loss in predictive performance, and further reductions in update frequency buy almost no additional saving while degrading predictive performance, most sharply on the smart-meter stream (Table 4). Serving q queries against a shared context instead amortises the encoding across simultaneous predictions, and per-instance serving time falls substantially on every backbone while predictive performance remains similar on the recurring-drift stream and declines more noticeably on the smart-meter stream at larger batches (Table 5). Neither lever removes the underlying cost, since a refresh only stores the new memory while every prediction call re-encodes it. Fixed and per-query cost. To characterise the batching gains, we approximate per-call serving time by t(q) = A+qγ ,where A is the fixed overhead and γ the incremental cost per query, fitted to a separate sweep on noaa . The fixed term dominates single-instance serving time on every backbone (Table 6). It combines context encoding with preprocessing, data transfer and execution overhead, but separate measurements bound the non-encoding part. Dispatch does not depend on the memory budget, and preprocessing and transfer grow only linearly in it, yet the TabICL and TabPFN calls grow severalfold between the smallest and the default budget (Table 3), so most of the default-budget call is context encoding. Storing a new memory, which includes preprocessing, also takes only a small fraction of a call. The fixed term is therefore largely amortisable, although the fit does not measure the exact encoding share or the achievable cache speed-up. Together with the refresh-rate plateau, this motivates reusing context representations across predictions (Figure 7).

## 5 Conclusions and future work 

We presented a systematic study of TFMs for data streams. TFMs achieve the strongest predictive performance in our evaluation, recover faster than streaming learners after drift, and remain the most accurate under label delay, but serve more than an order of magnitude slower than the cheapest streaming ensemble. Backbone choice and memory size contribute more than the memory management policy, which paid off only under delayed labels and only on one stream. In practice, FIFO with a strong backbone and a tuned memory size is a strong default, and query batching lowers cost where latency allows. The main computational limitation is repeated context encoding at every prediction. Our compact causal models show that reusing encoded context substantially reduces serving time and memory while staying close in accuracy wherever the checkpoint transfers. However, this result is limited to the compact models we pretrain, and whether existing TFMs support similar reuse remains open. Overall, efficient streaming TFMs will require architectural changes rather than more elaborate memory management. 

Limitations. Our study covers stream classification only, so the findings may not carry over to regression or other tabular tasks. Our experiments are constrained by the context limits and serving costs of the evaluated TFMs, so sub-stantially larger memories remain unexplored. In addition, context-based adaptation depends on the labelled examples available in memory, which limits adaptation under delayed feedback and changing concepts. The caching exper-iments are restricted to compact models and fixed attention windows, and therefore do not establish that the same efficiency gains will transfer directly to larger pretrained TFMs. Finally, unlabelled observations arriving during label delay are not used. 

Future work. A key direction is to develop TFMs that encode arriving examples once and reuse their representations as the stream evolves. Approaches based on local attention (Beltagy et al., 2020), recurrent state reuse (Dai et al., 2019), and key-value caching (Xiao et al., 2024) provide useful foundations. Future models could also incorporate pretraining on drifting sequences to better balance recent information, recurring concepts, and delayed feedback. Scaling such architectures while retaining the predictive strength of current TFMs remains an important challenge.

A.1 Real streams 

• electricity : Half-hourly records from the New South Wales electricity market between 1996 and 1998, with eight features covering price, demand and interstate transfer. The label is whether the price moved up or down relative to a moving average of the preceding day. Consecutive labels are strongly autocorrelated, so repeating the last label already scores well. • noaa : Fifty years of daily weather measurements from one NOAA station, with eight features such as temperature, pressure and wind speed, and a binary label for whether rain was observed. Seasonal and climatic variation change the inputs over time, although no drift points are annotated. • meter : Electricity consumption recorded by smart meters in Los Alamos, New Mexico, sampled every fifteen minutes. Each row is one household-day, so the ninety-six features are the readings of that day in order, and the ten classes identify the household. • rialto : Ten buildings beside the Rialto bridge in Venice, photographed by a fixed webcam over twenty consec-utive days and encoded as a normalised twenty-seven-dimensional colour histogram. The label is which building the image shows. Changes in illumination, weather, and time of day alter the input distribution while the building identities remain fixed. • posture no8 : Position readings from four tags worn by five people performing a scripted set of activities, giving three features, the tag’s coordinates, and an activity label. The original release has eleven activities. Following CURE (Lee et al., 2026), we drop the rarest and keep ten, because TabPFN v1 accepts at most ten classes. 

A.2 Synthetic streams 

All four are built through CapyMOA (Gomes et al., 2024), which wraps the MOA implementations (Bifet et al., 2010a), and none of them appears in the pretraining prior of any model we train. • agr a , abrupt: The AGRAWAL generator describes loan applicants through nine attributes such as salary, age and loan amount, and labels them by one of ten fixed classification functions. We switch that function three times, from 1 to 4 to 7 to 10 , at instances 7,500 , 15 ,000 and 22 ,500 of a 30 ,000 -instance stream, so the concept changes instantly at three known points while the inputs keep their distribution. • sea g , gradual: The SEA generator labels three numeric attributes by thresholding the sum of the first two. We move from function 1 to function 3 over a transition window of 10 ,000 instances centred at the midpoint of a 

100 ,000 -instance stream, so during the transition each instance is drawn from the old or the new concept with a probability that shifts smoothly, and there is no single point at which the concept changes. • rbf m , incremental: RandomRBF places fifty class centroids in a ten-dimensional space and draws instances around them, with five classes. Every centroid drifts continuously at a speed of 0.0001 per instance, producing continuous movement of the decision boundary over 100 ,000 instances without discrete drift points. • tree r , recurring: RandomTree labels ten attributes, five numeric and five nominal, by a randomly generated decision tree. We build three such trees and cycle through them with abrupt transitions every 5,000 instances, four visits to each concept, giving 60 ,000 instances in total.

C.5 Decision boundaries on a synthetic stream 

To make the difference between in-context and instance-based learning visible, we generate a two-dimensional stream with irrelevant uniform features and label noise, whose sine-shaped concept switches abruptly to a rotation of itself, recurs, and then drifts gradually to another rotation. A kNN classifier, TabPFN v1, TabICL v2 and TabFM are fitted on the identical FIFO memory and evaluated on a grid over the two informative features (Figure 11). Because all four see the same examples, every difference comes from how they read them. The irrelevant features corrupt the distances of kNN, while the TFMs ignore them and follow the true boundary in the order of their overall accuracy. Right after each drift all four fail alike, since the memory still holds the previous concept, so under FIFO the speed of adaptation is set by memory turnover and the quality of the recovered boundary by the backbone. Memory size exposes the resulting trade-off. A larger memory sharpens the steady-state boundary of TabICL and TabFM but still holds the old concept long after a drift (Figure 12), and recovery time grows nearly in proportion to memory size (Table 11). Choosing the size per window with hindsight is worth several points, and for TabICL and TabFM a selector over all sizes captures part of this headroom, whereas switching between only the smallest and largest memory does not (Table 12). These results come from one stream and one seed, so they indicate headroom for memory management rather than a method. D.3 Pre-training small models 

We pre-train compact nanoTabPFN (Pfefferle et al., 2025) 6 and nanoTabICL (Soda Team, Inria, 2026) 7 models to test whether restricted context interactions enable more efficient streaming inference. Bidirectional attention allows changes in memory to affect all retained representations, whereas causal attention restricts each row to earlier obser-vations and can support cached reuse when the remaining architecture is compatible. Sliding attention further limits each row to a window of W observations, giving O(W ) attention cost per step at fixed model size. Cached serving and re-encoding are not equivalent because cached rows may retain information from observations that have since been evicted, while re-encoding uses only the current memory. Within each architecture family, the attention mask is varied while the remaining training configuration is fixed, and all models are pretrained from scratch on synthetic drifting sequences that exclude the evaluation streams.

## E Prior work 

This appendix describes the methods evaluated in this study, with the limitation of each that is most relevant here. 

Memory management for TFMs. DualFIFO (Lourenc ¸o et al., 2026) keeps a short-term FIFO buffer of recent rows and a long-term store, and when the store is full it drops the oldest row of the most represented class, so that the memory stays class-balanced. It is evaluated only on real-world streams, where the drift type is unknown, and its cost is reported with ten instances predicted per forward pass, so neither the effect of drift type nor single-instance serving cost is measured. CURE (Lee et al., 2026) splits the budget into a short bank of recent rows and a long bank. Rows leaving the short bank enter the long bank only if their prediction entropy was high, and the long bank evicts a row that has a close same-class neighbour. Its evaluation includes one stream with a known drift type, and its runtime is compared only with DualFIFO, so its behaviour across drift types and its cost relative to classical learners remain open. 

TFM backbones. TabPFN v1 (Hollmann et al., 2023) is pretrained on synthetic tables and lets every context row attend to every other row. It is limited to small contexts and at most ten classes, and it is the backbone shared by DualFIFO and Drift-Resilient TabPFN. Drift-Resilient TabPFN (Helli et al., 2024) pretrains the same architecture on a prior whose causal model changes over time and receives a time index as input. It is designed and evaluated for batch data split into time domains rather than for streams, so applying it to a stream requires choosing how arrival order maps to domains. TabICL v2 (Qu et al., 2026) first embeds each row from its columns and then lets rows attend to one another, which scales to larger contexts than TabPFN v1. Its column stage attends over all rows, so the context cannot be encoded incrementally. TabFM (Google Research, 2026) alternates attention over columns and rows, compresses each row, and then attends over the compressed rows. It is released without a technical report and accepts at most ten classes. 

Stream learners. Hoeffding Adaptive Tree (Bifet & Gavald` a, 2009) grows a decision tree from running statistics and replaces subtrees when an ADWIN detector signals change. As a single tree, it adapts slowly after a drift. Adaptive Random Forest (Gomes et al., 2017) and Streaming Random Patches (Gomes et al., 2019) train ensembles of Hoeffding trees on resampled instances and random feature subsets, and swap in background trees when a detector fires. Both need many instances before their trees split, which gives them a slow cold start. Leveraging Bagging (Bifet et al., 2010b) increases the resampling weight and resets its worst member on detected change, so each reset rebuilds a member from scratch. SAM-kNN (Losing et al., 2016) keeps a short-term memory sized by recent error and a long-term memory of consistent older examples, and predicts by nearest neighbours. It adapts through stored examples like a TFM, but its distances degrade with irrelevant features. 

Online AutoML. OAML (Celik et al., 2023) re-runs a pipeline search on a buffer when drift is detected, so it depends on the detector and adds search cost at every trigger. EvoAutoML (Kulbach et al., 2022) evolves a population of pipelines and replaces the weakest by mutation, with a cost that grows with the population. ASML (Verma et al., 2024) searches around the current best pipeline, balancing exploration and exploitation. Like the other AutoML systems, its accuracy depends on search running alongside prediction. 

Evaluation under delay and compute limits. Prequential evaluation assumes that each label arrives before the next prediction (Gama et al., 2013). Delayed-label protocols relax this assumption (Grzenda et al., 2020; Souza et al., 2018), and work on online continual learning and test-time adaptation shows that a method slower than the arrival rate must skip or delay updates (Ghunaim et al., 2023; Alfarra et al., 2023; Prabhu et al., 2023). Existing TFM stream methods assume immediate labels and report cost separately from accuracy, which our label-delay and cost studies address. 

Reusing encoded context. Language models avoid re-encoding their past through segment-level recurrence (Dai et al., 2019), local attention (Beltagy et al., 2020) and key-value caching (Xiao et al., 2024). These rely on causal attention over the sequence, which current TFM backbones lack, and our cached-serving proof of concept adds it to a compact TFM. 26 Rethinking Tabular Foundation Models On Data Streams A P REPRINT
