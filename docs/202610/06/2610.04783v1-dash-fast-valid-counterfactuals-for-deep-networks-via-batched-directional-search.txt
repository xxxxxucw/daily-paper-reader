ABSTRACT 

Counterfactual explanations are most useful when they can be generated with low latency, remain close to the factual input, and satisfy input-domain, categorical, and actionability constraints. Achieving these objectives simultaneously is challenging for deep neural networks. Heuristic methods are often fast but may return invalid counterfactuals, whereas exact methods can certify global proximity but may not finish within practical time limits. We introduce DASH, a batched heuristic search method for finding close, valid, and actionable counterfactuals for deep neural networks under ℓ1, ℓ2, and ℓ∞ objectives. DASH uses directional Lipschitz bounds and local affine models to generate anchors, then ranks and expands promising regions with batched network evaluations. We compare DASH against nine prior heuristic methods and time-limited exact mixed-integer baselines on four tabular datasets, with network depths from 2 to 32, and evaluate scalability on PBMC3k. Across 9,000 tabular query-norm cases, DASH returns a valid counterfactual within 5% of the best heuristic-observed valid distance in 94 .6% of cases, with a median CPU search runtime of 0.061 s. PGD-bisect, the baseline with the highest pooled within-5% coverage, meets this criterion in 41 .1% of cases, with a median runtime of 0.298 s. These results show that the proposed search maintains high valid proximity across norms while keeping its search runtime practical. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Neural networks are increasingly used as predictive models that inform decisions, in settings such as mortgage default assessment from financial transactions (Kvamme et al., 2018), clinical-risk predictions from electronic health records (Rajkomar et al., 2018), and process fault diagnosis from operating measurements (Wu & Zhao, 2018). In such applications, a prediction can prompt a further question: what sufficiently small change to the input would make the model predict a different, desired outcome? For example, a borrower may seek changes to financial attributes that would enable them to cross a credit approval threshold, while an engineer may seek nearby operating conditions associated with a desired process state. Counterfactual explanations (CFEs) formalize this query by finding an alternative input that produces a desired target prediction while minimizing its deviation from the original or factual input (Wachter et al., 2018; Karimi et al., 2020). Let f denote a fixed predictor, x0

a factual input, Ytar the target-output set, and A(x0) the set of actionable modifications. CFEs can be mathematically formulated as an optimization problem

We call a point target-valid when it satisfies the target constraint f (x) ∈ Y tar , actionable when x ∈ A (x0), and a valid counterfactual 1arXiv:2610.04783v1 [cs.LG] 3 Oct 2026 when both conditions hold. The actionable set may impose feature bounds (e.g., employment duration), immutable coordinates (e.g., location), permitted directions of change (e.g., age), and categorical assignments (e.g., employment type) (Ustun et al., 2019). CFE methods can be measured on four evaluation criteria: likelihood of producing valid counterfactuals ( validity ), time it takes to produce a counterfactual ( runtime ), proximity to the factual ( distance ), and whether it provides a certificate of proximity or global optimality ( optimality certificate ). Existing CFE methods make different compromises among these criteria. Penalty-based heuristic methods like Wachter (Wachter et al., 2018), S-CFE (Sadiku et al., 2025), and DiCE (Mothilal et al., 2020) replace the target constraint with a differentiable prediction-loss term and optimize it jointly with an input distance penalty. Consequently, their termination condition does not guarantee validity or distance optimality of the proposed counterfactual. Other heuristics use discrete or sample-based search (Brughmans et al., 2024). However, they neither enforce actionability constraints nor give an optimality guarantee. Exact approaches instead encode the predictor neural network, target constraint, and the actionable set as a satisfiability or mixed-integer optimization problem (Karimi et al., 2020; Mohammadi et al., 2021). ReLU networks permit exact mixed-integer linear encoding due to their piecewise affine nature, whereas other non-linear activations require either mixed-integer non-linear encoding or mixed-integer linear approximation. Finding a minimum-distance counterfactual even for ReLU networks is NP-hard (S¨ alzer & Lange, 2021; Weng et al., 2018). Consequently, solvers can spend substantial computation proving optimality even after a valid counterfactual has been found, limiting their applicability to large networks under practical runtime budgets. These approaches leave a gap between heuristic methods, whose returned outputs may violate target or actionability conditions, and exact methods, which may not complete within a practical time limit. To address target-validity, actionability and runtime, we propose DASH, a heuristic search method that balances all three axes for ℓ1, ℓ2, and ℓ∞ norms. DASH uses local sensitivity information and linearization to estimate the decision boundary to achieve high validity, a strict enforcement of actionability constraints at each iteration, and batching the network evaluations to reduce computational overhead. We evaluate this approach against prior heuristic methods, and exact, time-limited, mixed-integer baselines on four tabular datasets and the PBMC3k single-cell RNA sequencing dataset. Pooled across all 9,000 tabular query-norm cases, DASH finds a valid counterfactual within 5% of the best heuristic-observed valid distance ∼ 2.3 times more often than PGD-bisect, the baseline with the highest pooled within-5% coverage, with a ∼ 5 times faster median CPU search runtime. Whereas prior scalability experiments (Mohammadi et al., 2021) considered architectures containing at most approximately 18 ,000 parameters, our PBMC3k evaluation includes a network with more than 100 ,000 parameters. As a heuristic, DASH does not certify non-existence or global optimality. Nevertheless, on the 3,448 tabular query-norm cases for which a time-limited exact solver certified the optimum, DASH returned a counterfactual within 1% of that optimum in 82 .7% of cases. We measure distance and validity of the found counterfactual, and runtime of the method, under ℓ1, ℓ2, and ℓ∞ objectives, and additionally evaluate a GPU implementation on PBMC3k. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Adversarial Methods Related to CFEs are minimum-norm adversarial boundary-crossing prob-lems (Pawelczyk et al., 2022). Adversarial attack methods solve this through margin penalties or barriers (Carlini & Wagner, 2017; Finlay et al., 2019), adaptive norm constraints (Pintor et al., 2021) or local affine boundary models (Moosavi-Dezfooli et al., 2016; Croce & Hein, 2020). Our PGD-bisect baseline combines projected gradient descent (Madry et al., 2018) with bisection. Applying these search ideas to mixed-feature counterfactuals requires explicit treatment of actionable con-straints like immutable features, categorical assignments, and permitted directions of change (Ustun et al., 2019). 

Heuristic CFE methods. For differentiable predictors such as neural networks, several CFE methods have used first-order optimization. Wachter et al. (2018) convert the target constraint into a differentiable quadratic loss, and jointly optimize it with a normalized distance function, using gradient descent algorithms. DiCE (Mothilal et al., 2020) and S-CFE (Sadiku et al., 2025) add a diversity term and a non-smooth ℓp (0 ≤ p ≤ 1) sparsity penalty term respectively. These objectives guide the search, but minimizing a penalty does not by itself certify that the returned point 2satisfies every target and actionability constraint. Other heuristic CFE methods rely on sampling. NICE (Brughmans et al., 2024) identifies the nearest training instance with the desired class and successively replaces coordinates of the factual input with the corresponding values from the chosen training instance. Growing Spheres (Laugel et al., 2018) samples increasingly distant hyperspherical shells to locate a point with the desired prediction, whereas the Greedy method of Schut et al. (2021) uses Jacobian-guided feature updates. The limitation of these heuristic methods is that they often fail to find valid counterfactuals. 

Exact counterfactual methods. Certifying a nearest counterfactual requires finding a valid coun-terfactual and ruling out all closer actionable points. One family of exact methods uses satisfiability modulo theories (SMT). ReLUplex (Katz et al., 2017) develops an SMT-based verification method for ReLU networks, while MACE (Karimi et al., 2020) applies SMT to counterfactual explanations using repeated queries to bound the optimal distance. An alternative is mixed-integer programming (MIP), where ReLU networks can be encoded exactly with mixed-integer linear constraints (Fischetti & Jo, 2018; Tjeng et al., 2019). Mohammadi et al. (2021) use this approach to compute nearest counterfactuals with guarantees. Bound tightening, presolve, and stronger ReLU formulations can improve the tractability of the MIP formulations (Fischetti & Jo, 2018; Grimstad & Andersson, 2019; Tjeng et al., 2019; Tsay et al., 2021; Huchette et al., 2026). Despite these advances, exact certification can remain computationally expensive for larger networks.

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Datasets and predictors. Our primary testbed is actionable tabular counterfactual search, where continuous and categorical features coexist. We evaluate HELOC, Give Me Some Credit (GMC), Adult Income, and COMPAS (Pawelczyk et al., 2021) using fully connected ReLU networks of width 64 and depths 2, 4, 8, 16 , 32 . We also evaluate PBMC3k (10x Genomics, 2017) to test the same search on a wider network. MNIST is used to test a no-actionable dataset, whose results are reported in Appendix C.1. For each of three training seeds, we select 50 correctly classified held-out factual inputs and seek the opposite class with verification margin μ = 10 −4. Each method receives the same factual inputs under each objective norm. This yields 9,000 tabular and 450 PBMC3k queries per method. Discussion. The results distinguish finding a target-valid point from finding a close counterfactual that satisfies the specified actionability constraints. Across the tabular tasks that contain both continuous and categorical variables, DASH combines high valid-within-5% coverage with short search times under all three norms. Its proximity is also supported on the subset where an exact solver certifies the optimum. These findings support boundary-guided, actionable search as a practical approach to tabular counterfactual generation, especially when categorical variables coexist with continuous variables. For continuous only datasets, like PBMC3k, DASH outperforms all methods on ℓ1 and remains competitive otherwise. 

Limitations and future work. DASH does not certify global optimality or non-existence: a finite beam and runtime budget can cause it to miss an existing counterfactual. Its search also relies on local gradient and boundary estimates, and the current implementation is limited to ReLU networks and the actionability constraints considered here. Future work could combine DASH’s incumbent with certified lower bounds and extend the search to causal, user-specific, or more general linear constraints. On some continuous-only settings and norms, adversarial attack methods outperform the current configuration; adapting the balance between one-directional and gradient-based anchors may improve performance in those settings. 9AI U SE STATEMENT

Table 4 reports the implemented baseline settings. Methods receive the same coordinate bounds, except FAB, which is clipped only to the feature range, and DeepFool, which is unconstrained; neither receives the actionability constraints. Methods differ in whether they enforce one-hot assignments during search. Consequently, every returned output is evaluated using the common target-validity and actionability checks before inclusion in validity or proximity metrics. Wachter and DiCE project categorical groups before evaluating output validity. NICE copies one-hot groups jointly from its target-side training instance. PGD-bisect and FMN apply categorical projection before final evaluation. Under ℓ2, FMN returns no counterfactual on any of the 750 Adult queries; these queries count as failures. Growing Spheres and Greedy use coordinate bounds but do not consistently repair categorical assignments during their search. The mixed-integer formulations encode the applicable bounds and categorical constraints directly. MIP methods are evaluated only on tabular predictors of depths 2, 4, and 8.

FAB reimplementation. We use a repository reimplementation of FAB (Croce & Hein, 2020), not the authors’ released code. At each iteration, it forms a local affine decision hyperplane and computes box-constrained projections under the requested ℓ1, ℓ2, or ℓ∞ norm. The ℓ1 projection uses a continuous-knapsack solution, while the scalar parameters for the ℓ2 and ℓ∞ projections are found by bisection. It then combines projections from the current point and factual, applies an overshoot and backward correction, and performs a final boundary search. We use at most 100 iterations, 

αmax = 0 .1, overshoot η = 1 .05 , backward-step factor β = 0 .9, and 30 final boundary-search steps. The implementation enforces box bounds but does not repair categorical or other tabular actionability constraints during its search; all outputs undergo the common final validity checks. 

Norm-adapted PGD-bisect. Our PGD-bisect baseline combines a projected-gradient search at a fixed radius with bisection over that radius, following the projected-gradient attack of Madry et al. (2018). For each requested p ∈ { 1, 2, ∞} , an inner restart searches the intersection Sp(ϵ) = {x :

∥x − x0∥p ≤ ϵ, ℓ ≤ x ≤ u}, where ℓ and u are feature-bound vectors. Its ascent direction is the signed gradient for p = ∞, the Euclidean-normalized gradient for p = 2 , and a signed coordinate of largest gradient magnitude for p = 1 . Each iteration proposes a step of length 2.5ϵ/ 100 in the corresponding norm, then takes the Euclidean projection onto Sp(ϵ). For p = ∞, this projection is coordinate-wise clipping. For p = 1 , we use the clipped soft-threshold form of the exact ball-box projection given by Croce & Hein (2021); a scalar multiplier is found by bisection so that the projected perturbation has ℓ1 norm at most ϵ. Norm-adapted Growing Spheres. The original method searches Euclidean shells and then reduces unnecessary feature changes (Laugel et al., 2018). We retain those two phases while replacing the shell by {x : a ≤ ∥ x−x0∥p ≤ b} for the requested p ∈ { 1, 2, ∞} . Gaussian vectors normalized in ℓ2

generate Euclidean directions, independently signed exponential variables normalized in ℓ1 generate cross-polytope directions, and uniform-cube vectors normalized in ℓ∞ generate cube directions. These constructions follow the probabilistic geometry of ℓp balls (Barthe et al., 2005). Conditional on a shell, the radius is sampled with Rd uniform on [ad, b d], where d is the input dimension. We clip sampled points to the feature bounds, choose the target-valid point with smallest distance in the requested norm, and apply the same feature-reversion pass as in the Euclidean implementation. Clipping can move points inward and therefore changes the ideal uniform shell distribution near the domain boundary. The ℓ1 and ℓ∞ versions are our norm adaptations, not variants supplied by the original authors. All three use 1,000 samples per shell, shell width 0.2, and at most 1,000 shells; outputs receive the same target and actionability checks as every other method.
