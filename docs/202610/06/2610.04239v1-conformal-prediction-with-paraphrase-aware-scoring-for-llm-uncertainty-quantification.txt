## Abstract 

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) for large language models (LLMs) aims to provide reliable measures of predictive confidence, yet current methods are often unstable under meaning-preserving perturbations. Semantically equivalent paraphrases can induce substantial variability in predictive confidence, even for methods with for-mal guarantees, such as conformal prediction. To address this issue, we propose a paraphrase-aware UQ framework robust to semantic rewordings. Our approach trains a lightweight proxy model on LLM hidden states and aggregates its predic-tions across paraphrases to construct label-wise nonconformity scores. Under score exchangeability, conformal calibration retains marginal coverage. This guarantee can also hold under test-only rewording, provided that the paraphrase pipeline satisfies an additional distributional alignment condition. We evaluate three settings (normal, fully reworded, and semi-reworded) which apply rewording to neither dataset, both calibration and test datasets, or only the test dataset, respectively. Across seven multiple-choice QA benchmarks and multiple model families, our method produces compact prediction sets with empirical coverage generally near the nominal target, even in the semi-reworded setting. Ablation studies show that the learned proxy accounts for most of the reduction in set size, while paraphrase-augmented training and inference-time aggregation improve stability under reword-ing. Code is available at https://github.com/Raina-Xin/PA_Score .

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in high-stakes domains such as education and medicine [4, 24]. In these settings, reliable uncertainty quantification (UQ) is indispensable, as decision-makers must assess when to trust model predictions. However, UQ in LLMs is often unstable: predictive confidence can be poorly calibrated and highly sensitive to superficial variations in input phrasing [35]. Such instability undermines the reliability of uncertainty estimates and limits their practical usefulness. Another fundamental limitation is that many existing UQ methods lack formal guarantees. Most approaches rely on heuristic confidence scores [35, 27, 40, 3, 18], making it difficult to determine whether their outputs can be trusted, especially in high-stakes scenarios. This motivates the use of methods with theoretical guarantees. Conformal prediction (CP) provides such a framework, constructing prediction sets with finite-sample coverage under mild assumptions [44, 34]. In CP, a nonconformity score measures how atypical a candidate output is for a given input and determines whether it is included in the prediction set.

While CP ensures statistical validity, its practical reliability depends critically on the stability of the underlying uncertainty scores. Ideally, semantically equivalent inputs should yield comparable uncertainty estimates. For example, the questions “What is the capital of France?” and “Which city serves as France’s capital?” should lead to similar prediction sets. In practice, however, even such paraphrases can induce substantial variability in uncertainty estimates, leading to unstable or inflated prediction sets. This simple example reveals a gap between theoretical guarantees and semantic robustness. This challenge is particularly pronounced in the LLM setting, where variability arises from semantic-preserving perturbations such as paraphrasing, rather than classical distributional shifts. Addressing such instability requires methods that can achieve both statistical validity and semantic robustness. 

Contributions. To address this challenge, we introduce a paraphrase-aware scoring mechanism within the conformal prediction framework that reduces sensitivity to semantic rewordings. Rather than relying on a single input, our approach constructs uncertainty estimates by aggregating pre-dictions across a set of paraphrases, thereby capturing variability induced by rewording. To enable consistent aggregation, we employ a lightweight proxy model to produce calibrated and comparable outputs across paraphrases. The resulting nonconformity scores can be integrated with standard CP, retaining its coverage guarantees when the required exchangeability conditions hold. Focusing on the nonconformity score provides a convenient mechanism for incorporating problem-specific structure. Importantly, modifying the score leaves the underlying conformal guarantees unchanged, making it a simple and modular approach to improving robustness. We demonstrate that our method applies to both split CP and quasi-conditional CP (QCCP) [13]. The learned proxy accounts for most of the end-to-end set-size reduction, while paraphrase-augmented training and test-time aggregation contribute to wording stability. Theoretically, we show that our scores preserve score-level exchangeability under paraphrased inputs, and more generally under transformations satisfying a distributional closedness condition (see Asm. 5.1). Empirically, we conduct large-scale experiments on five general QA benchmarks and two medical QA benchmarks, showing end-to-end prediction-set reductions of up to 2–4×

against raw-probability baselines, with empirical coverage generally near the nominal target. We also conducted controlled ablations to separate the proxy, training-view, and inference-aggregation contributions. 

## 2 Related Work 

Conformal prediction provides finite-sample marginal coverage when calibration and test examples are exchangeable [44, 34]. Its applications to language models include conformal language modeling, multiple-choice question answering, and API-based inference [30, 22, 37]. Learned nonconformity 2scores and quasi-conditional methods further adapt prediction sets to task structure [10, 13]. Our focus is the additional question of how label-wise scores and prediction sets change when an input is reworded without changing its intended meaning. Perturb-and-aggregate uncertainty estimation is established in SPUQ [12], while other work studies semantic uncertainty and prompt sensitivity [21, 29]. SPUQ’s original instance-level uncertainty value is distinct from the per-option score required to form conformal prediction sets. Prior work establishes perturbation-based uncertainty estimation and conformal coverage, but leaves open how to design label-wise nonconformity scores that are stable under meaning-preserving rewording. This matters in practice because semantically equivalent queries can yield different prediction sets even when marginal coverage is maintained. Our work addresses this nonconformity score design problem through a unified treatment of methodology, evaluation, and validity analysis, extending the focus from instance-level uncertainty to the stability of conformal decisions. For detailed related work, please refer to Appendix A. 

## 3 Preliminaries 

Problem Setup. We study conformal prediction for classification when inputs may be reworded without changing their meaning or correct labels. Our goal is to construct small prediction sets that contain the correct label at a prescribed coverage level. We investigate how the choice of nonconformity score affects this coverage-efficiency tradeoff under paraphrasing, considering both matched calibration and test distributions and test-only rewording shifts. Coverage under the latter setting requires additional assumptions, which we formalize in Section 5. Formally, we study supervised prediction with input x ∈ X (e.g., a natural language question) and a finite label space Y (e.g., multiple-choice answers). Within this framework, two widely used approaches are split conformal prediction (split CP) and quasi-conditional conformal prediction (QCCP).

To address this issue, we introduce paraphrase-aware (PA) nonconformity scores, which explicitly incorporate semantic variability by aggregating predictions across paraphrases. These scores can be integrated with either split CP or QCCP, yielding robust prediction sets to reworded inputs while retaining statistical coverage guarantees. 

Overview of Score Construction. At a high level (Figure 2), our approach constructs uncertainty scores by aggregating predictions across paraphrases, thereby capturing variability induced by semantic rewordings. It proceeds as follows. For each input question x, we generate a set of paraphrases B(x). For each paraphrased input x′ ∈ B (x), we extract its semantic embedding E(x′)

(e.g., the corresponding LLM hidden state), resulting in a collection of embeddings {E(x′) : x′ ∈B(x)}. We then train a lightweight proxy classifier to map these embeddings to calibrated predictive probabilities. Based on these probabilities, we construct paraphrase-aware nonconformity scores, denoted by S(x, y ), which aggregate information across the paraphrase set B(x). The resulting scores are subsequently calibrated via split CP or QCCP to produce the final prediction set. Due to space constraints, the full procedure is summarized in Algorithm 1 in the appendix. Additional details on paraphrase quality and runtime are provided in Appendices E.5 and E.6. 

Motivation for a Proxy Classifier. Raw LLM probabilities are often overconfident and poorly calibrated, 3 as they are trained for next-token prediction rather than uncertainty quantification [27, 40]. To address this limitation, we introduce a lightweight proxy classifier Pθ (y | E(x)) that produces more reliable predictive probabilities for downstream decision making. The proxy takes the last-layer hidden state of the LLM as input and outputs a probability distribution over the label space Y. In our implementation, the proxy is a shallow two-layer MLP trained on LLM embeddings {E(Xi)} with labels {Yi}, using a combination of task loss (cross-entropy) and a calibration-aware loss (soft-binned ECE; 20). This design improves calibration while remaining computationally efficient. The main variant requires hidden-state access and labeled proxy-training data. The blackbox API extension and full online generation cost are reported in Appendices G.1 and E.6. 

Remark 4.1. Since the PA score depends on embeddings E(·), one may be concerned about sensitivity to representation choices. In Appendix F.4, we show that the PA score remains robust across a range of embedding variants, including different ways of summarizing token-level representations (e.g., pooling) and selecting model layers.

Choice of the Class Function ϕ. We now describe how we construct the class function ϕ (intro-duced in (2)), which captures semantic similarity among input questions. At a high level, ϕ groups semantically similar inputs so that downstream procedures operate on coherent groups rather than a single global structure. Since most benchmarks lack explicit type annotations (e.g., commonsense vs. factual), we construct these groups automatically. Specifically, we embed each question–context pair using a pretrained SBERT encoder ( all-MiniLM-L6-v2 ) [31], fit K-means on the calibration embed-dings only, and assign each test input to its nearest centroid; the resulting cluster assignment defines 

ϕ(x). Formally, this partitions X into N clusters {G j }Nj=1 , and we set ϕ(x) = PNj=1 1{x ∈ G j } · ϕj ,where ϕj denotes the representative vector of cluster j. The number of clusters K can be fixed or selected via the silhouette score on calibration embeddings. This construction yields semantically coherent groupings and enables conditioning on latent question structure without requiring manual annotations. For more implementation details, see Appendix D.4. 

## 5 Theoretical Analysis 

When integrated into standard CP frameworks, our PA scores inherit the original theoretical guaran-tees under exchangeability, since these guarantees are agnostic to the specific choice of nonconformity score. However, such guarantees rely on the exchangeability of the resulting scores, which may be vi-olated under distributional shifts. We show that, in certain structured settings, our PA construction can restore exchangeability at the score level, even when it does not hold for the original calibration–test pairs. We formalize this intuition below.

Implications for Paraphrasing. Meaning-preserving paraphrases express the same underlying content through different wordings. Distributional closedness formalizes the stronger requirement that this rewording leaves the distribution of generated paraphrases unchanged. Under this view, our PA scores leverage multiple independent samples drawn from T (· | x) to construct a robust statistic for each input. Under Asm. 5.1, this invariance extends to the joint distribution of these samples. Lemma 5.1 then establishes exchangeability of the resulting scores under the stated score conditions. Consequently, our scores retain the corresponding marginal coverage guarantees within CP and quasi-conditional guarantees within QCCP under its joint feature–score assumptions, including when test inputs undergo rewording. A formal statement together with proofs is given in Appendix C. 

Remark 5.1. The analysis also quantifies departures from exact distributional closedness. Under the assumptions in Appendix C.3, a total-variation bound controls the change in coverage induced by rewording. Appendix E.4 complements this result with an empirical diagnostic of score-distribution shift and its effect on coverage. 

## 6 Empirical Performance 

Datasets. We evaluate our framework on seven benchmark datasets. Five are general multiple-choice question answering (MCQA) datasets from the LLM-Uncertainty-Benchmark [53]: MMLU, CosmosQA, HellaSwag, HaluDial, and HaluSum, covering question answering ( QA ), reading com-prehension ( RC ), commonsense inference ( CI ), dialogue response selection ( DRS ), and document summarization ( DS ). We further include two medical MCQA datasets, MedMCQA [28] and MedQA 

[19], to evaluate robustness in specialized domains. For each dataset, we sample 10,000 questions. Following the LLM-Uncertainty-Benchmark protocol [53], we augment the response space |Y| of all datasets to six options by adding I don’t know ” and None of the above .” Together, these datasets span diverse reasoning tasks and domains, enabling a comprehensive evaluation of both generalization and robustness.

In the fully reworded setting, we apply the same paraphrasing operator T (introduced above) to all inputs across training, calibration, and test sets, and compute all scores on reworded questions. This tests how the LLM paraphrased input can influence the stability of different nonconformity scores. All CP scores are computed on these LLM-reworded questions. In the semi-reworded setting, training and calibration use original inputs, while test inputs are reworded by an LLM. This represents deployment in which users or an upstream system rephrase inputs after calibration, creating a structured distribution shift. Normal and Fully reworded retain matched calibration and test distributions, so standard conformal validity applies under the corresponding exchangeability assumptions. Semi-reworded tests whether coverage and efficiency persist across the rewording shift, with theoretical validity governed by the additional conditions stated in our analysis. Additional details are provided in Appendix E.3.

Proxy Model Improves Accuracy and Calibration. In our work, the proxy model Pθ is a lightweight two-layer MLP trained on top of frozen hidden states from the LLM. We find that 

Pθ produces more accurate and better-calibrated distributions than raw LLM logits. As shown in Figure 3, the proxy consistently outperforms logits across benchmarks on all calibration metrics. For example, accuracy improves significantly ( DS 0.597 → 0.944 , DRS 0.510 → 0.742 ), while both Brier score and NLL decrease ( RC Brier 0.075 → 0.039 , NLL 2.513 → 0.487 ). Here, Brier measures the mean squared error between predicted probabilities and true one-hot labels, while NLL penalizes assigning low probability to the correct answer; both capture calibration quality. This improvement arises because raw logits are optimized for next-token prediction rather than calibrated posteriors, often leading to miscalibration and poor class separation. In contrast, the proxy leverages hidden states, which encode richer task-relevant signals, and is trained with a calibration-aware loss. Where Do the Gains Come From? We ask how much of PA’s improvement comes from learning a proxy and what paraphrases add during training and inference. Table 6 first compares raw probabilities with proxies trained on either probability vectors or hidden states. ## 8 Conclusion 

In this work, we introduced a framework for paraphrase-robust conformal prediction through paraphrase-aware nonconformity scores applied to both split CP and quasi-conditional CP. Un-der the stated exchangeability conditions, our method inherits conformal coverage guarantees while producing compact prediction sets. The learned proxy drives most of the compactness gain, while paraphrase training and inference-time aggregation improve wording stability. Experiments on five general QA and two medical QA benchmarks demonstrate robustness under rewording and strong generalization across model families. These findings motivate further study of robust nonconfor-mity score design across tasks and modalities, with preliminary claim-verification results and a vision–language extension discussed in Appendices G.3 and G.4. 10 Acknowledgments and Disclosure of Funding

## A Detailed Related Work 

Heuristic and Calibration-Based Uncertainty for LLMs. LLM uncertainty is often estimated from token-level signals such as entropy, logits, or ranks, followed by calibration. Early work studies calibration in deep models and NLP tasks [27, 40, 36], and extends to prompt- and generation-level schemes for long-form outputs [3, 18]. More recent approaches replace raw probabilities with representation-based surrogates, including semantic entropy probes [21], relevance-aware confidence [8], perturbation-based measures [12], multi-agent diversity signals [45], and semantic-density 16 metrics [29]. Complementary directions include abstention mechanisms [25], multicalibration of confidence scores [7], post-hoc calibration from generated text [41], and parameter-efficient Bayesian or ensemble-style methods for fine-tuned LLMs [2, 47]. While perturbation-based methods such as SPUQ explicitly aggregate across input variations, these methods address instance-level uncertainty. We study label-wise scores and prediction-set stability within CP, whose coverage guarantee additionally requires exchangeability. 

Conformal Prediction for LLMs. Conformal prediction (CP) provides distribution-free prediction sets with finite-sample coverage under exchangeability [44, 34]. Recent work adapts CP to language modeling in several directions, including conformal language modeling [30, 30], API-only inference without logit access [37], and methods tailored to multiple-choice settings [22, 52]. CP has also been used for output alignment and certification [14], as well as benchmarking LLMs using uncertainty-aware metrics [53]. Extensions to generation tasks include ConU , which applies split CP to sets of sampled responses [48], and SConU , which studies exchangeability violations and approximates con-ditional guarantees [50]. Other approaches combine CP with re-asking strategies to improve accuracy and efficiency [42], while selective answering with risk control has been explored via conformal abstention [38, 49]. In contrast to these works, our approach focuses on semantic invariance : we develop paraphrase-aware scores that capture stability under meaning-preserving transformations, and integrate them with both split conformal prediction and quasi-conditional calibration on semantic embeddings, going beyond standard split CP. 

Toward Conditional Guarantees and Paraphrase Robustness. Exact conditional coverage is unattainable in finite samples without distributional assumptions [43, 11], motivating relaxations such as quasi-conditional guarantees via augmented quantile regression [13]. Recent work extends CP to leverage feature-conditional structure in LLM settings [5]. In parallel, another line of work studies 

semantic uncertainty , introducing embedding-based metrics and perturbation procedures [12, 21, 17]. Related approaches examine prompt sensitivity and meaning-preserving perturbations [29, 6], but do not provide conformal guarantees. Our method bridges these directions by (i) quantifying predictive stability across paraphrases using semantic embeddings and a proxy model, and (ii) calibrating these scores within the conformal prediction framework, yielding prediction sets that are both semantically robust and statistically valid.

D.1 Dataset Details 

We used five general and two medical MCQA datasets to show that our paraphrase-aware score can be broadly used and can adapt to high-stakes scenarios where calibration is important. The five general datasets test a wide range of LLM capabilities and have been used in previous CP benchmarking papers [53, 42]. We use the five general datasets processed by Ye et al. [53]. Each of the datasets contains 10,000 questions. 1. QA, MMLU [15]: MMLU is a dataset designed to test the general knowledge and question-answering abilities of LLMs. Question topics range from sociology and high school geography to electrical engineering and abstract algebra. 2. RC, CosmosQA [16]: CosmosQA focuses on gauging an LLM’s reading comprehension abilities. The LLM is given a short paragraph and is then asked to answer a follow-up question based on commonsense reasoning. 3. CI, HellaSwag [54]: HellaSwag evaluates if LLMs can use commonsense inference to construct a realistic and meaningful continuation of a given scenario. HellaSwag is deliberately designed so that LLMs struggle with questions that humans could normally answer with high confidence. 4. DRS, HaluEval [23]: HaluEval consists of hallucinated LLM responses to user queries. A subset of HaluEval contains queries that relate to dialogue response selection : the LLM must be able to choose a logical response for a conversation. We refer to this part of HaluEval as HaluDial. 5. DS, HaluEval [23]: The HaluEval dataset also has hallucinated document summaries . In an MCQA setting, the LLM must determine which summary in the answer choices is most relevant to a provided document. We refer to this part of HaluEval as HaluSum. 6. MedMCQA [28]: MedMCQA is a large-scale multiple-choice medical QA dataset comprising over 194,000 entrance-exam–style questions spanning 2400 healthcare topics and 21 medical subjects. We select a subset of 10,000 single-answer questions from MedMCQA (i.e. where exactly one option is marked correct) for our experiments. 7. MedQA [19]: MedQA is a medical exam QA dataset derived from professional medical board exams (e.g. USMLE), providing each question paired with candidate answer options and cor-responding references. In our work, we use only the US-part of MedQA, and further restrict to 10,000 multiple-choice items that have exactly one correct answer. Following the LLM-Uncertainty-Benchmark protocol [53], every question across all seven datasets is presented with a uniform label space of six options |Y| = 6 : the original answer choices (typically four for MMLU, CosmosQA, HellaSwag, and MedMCQA, and four to five for MedQA) are augmented with two additional options, “ I don’t know ” and “ None of the above ”, and HaluDial/HaluSum are likewise cast into this six-way format. This is why all reported set sizes are bounded by 6 rather than by the number of native answer choices.

SPUQ-adapt. and TRON-adapt. are per-option score controls rather than the original SPUQ and TRON algorithms. For raw option probabilities py (x), let ¯py (x) = |B (x)|−1 P

The probability-based TRON control is exactly LAC. D.4 Experimental Implementation Details Paraphrased Generation for Calculating PA Score. Paraphrases were generated by prompting 

Qwen3-8B [51] with the following query: “Rephrase the following question in your own words (preserving its meaning): Original question: {question} \n Rephrased question:". Additional details, such as any context or the answer choices, were not included with the original question. A total of 6 paraphrases were generated per question. Paraphrases that were equivalent to the original question or were previously generated were not included in the final set. The temperature of Qwen3-8B [51] was set to 1.1 to encourage diverse responses. 

LLM Embeddings. We next obtained the LLM embeddings for each question and paraphrase. These LLM embeddings are used to train the proxy model and serve as the LLM’s representation of the query. We treated the paraphrases as new samples in the dataset and assigned them the same answer choices and correct label as their parent question. For each sample, the input prompt included the question and the list of answer choices without the correct label. The LLM embedding was extracted from the final hidden layer; this layer simultaneously encodes the LLM’s understanding of the question and its predicted answer. The LLM logits were also retrieved by finding the raw score corresponding to each answer represented as a token (e.g. ‘A’, ‘B’, etc.). We then applied softmax to the logits to obtain a probability distribution over the answer choices. For each dataset except the DS dataset, the LLM was provided with 2 example questions and their correct labels. Due to the long context needed for the questions in the DS dataset, only 1 example was provided to the LLM in this case. We followed this procedure to get the embeddings and logits of the instruction-tuned versions of Qwen3-8B [51], Llama-3.1-8B [9], and Phi-3-small-8k [1]. 

Proxy Model. The proxy model calibrates the LLM’s probability distribution of the answer choices across the paraphrases. We used a 2-layer MLP with ReLU activation as our proxy model. The input to the proxy model is an LLM’s embedding for a question or paraphrase, and the output is a vector with dimension |Y| . The loss function used to train the proxy model is Ltotal = LCE + λece × LECE ,where LCE is the standard cross-entropy loss for multiclass classification and LECE is the soft-binned expected calibration error loss [20]. λECE is a hyperparameter that can be tuned to increase or decrease the relative importance of calibration in different contexts. Out of the 4,000 questions in the training set, 600 questions were used as the validation set. Since each question has 6 paraphrases, the effective training and validation set sizes are 23,800 and 4,200, respectively. We conducted grid search on the following hyperparameters: learning rate, weight decay, λECE , hidden dimension, and 23 the batch size. The hyperparameters used for each dataset and each model are provided in Appendix D.5. 

Score Calculation. For our baselines, we only use logits from the original question. The LAC score [33] is defined as sLAC (x, y ) = 1 − f (x)y , where f (x)y is the softmax probability of label y for a question x. The APS score [32] is sAPS (x, y ) = P     

> y′∈Y :f(x)y′≥f(x)y

f (x)y′ , i.e., the cumulative probability of labels ranked at least as high as y. To find the score of a question x using the PA method, the LLM embeddings of its paraphrases (represented by the set B(x)) are inputted into the trained proxy model. Then, the score for answer choice y of question x is given by Smean (x, y ),

Sweighted (x, y ), or Sworst (x, y ) (see Section 4). Note that only the scores of the 6 paraphrases are used in the PA formulas. The LLM’s embedding of the original question does not contribute to that question’s final PA score. This is in contrast to LAC and APS, which only rely on the logits of the original question and do not factor in the paraphrases. 

Split Conformal Prediction. Split CP [44] uses a separate calibration dataset to calculate a global score threshold that determines the prediction sets for the test set. For each calibration example, we use the nonconformity score from the LLM’s softmax probabilities or proxy model’s probability distribution. These nonconformity scores are collected and the (1 − α) quantile is estimated with a finite-sample correction qα = Quantile( {si}, ⌈(n + 1)(1 − α)⌉/n ). At test time, each example’s prediction set is formed by including all labels whose score is below qα, with a fallback to the most probable label if the set is empty. This procedure ensures that the empirical coverage approaches the nominal 1 − α guarantee. 

Quasi-Conditional Conformal Prediction. In contrast to split CP, QCCP [13] calculates class-specific thresholds for pre-defined classes. The feature function ϕ is defined to assign a class to a question based on its features. In the case that ϕ is a constant function, then QCCP is equivalent to split CP; we use this ϕ function ( ϕ = intercept) to obtain all split CP results. In our QCCP analysis, we focus on a ϕ function that takes in the embedding of each question generated by all-MiniLM-L6-V2 

[31]. K-means clustering is used to separate the questions into clusters, with each cluster acting as a class, and a different score threshold is selected for each cluster. For a given test question, the ϕ

function is either a one-hot encoding of its assigned cluster or a vector of its embedding’s distances to the three closest cluster centroids (dist3) (which can be used to calculate the threshold). The coverage rates reported using QCCP are calculated marginally with the exception of Figure 4 and Figure 9, which displays class-wise coverages. We used the conditionalconformal package [13] to implement QCCP.

E.2 Finite-Sample Behavior of Class-Conditional Coverage 

When applying our PA scores within QCCP, we obtain population-level guarantees of the form 

1 − α ≤ Pr( Y ∈ bC(X) | X ∈ G) ≤ 1 − α + slack ,

for each cluster G, where the slack term decreases with the effective sample size within the cluster (see Lemma B.2). It is important to note that this guarantee holds at the population level, rather than for finite-sample empirical estimates. In practice, when we estimate coverage within each cluster using a finite number of samples, the observed coverage rate will generally fluctuate around the target level 1 − α = 0 .90 .These deviations arise from two sources: (i) sampling variability due to finite data, and (ii) the presence of a nonzero slack term, which depends on the size and heterogeneity of each cluster. As a result, empirical cluster-wise coverage is not expected to match 0.90 exactly, even when the theoretical guarantee holds. Therefore, moderate deviations from 0.90 in our experiments should not be interpreted as a violation of coverage guarantees, but rather as a natural consequence of finite-sample estimation under the QCCP framework. To illustrate this phenomenon, we conduct three additional analyses in the semi-reworded setting, varying the number of clusters K ∈ { 5, 7, 10 , 20 }.

E.3 Robustness under the Semi-Reworded Setting 

Recall that the two versions of each dataset in our evaluation are defined as Dorig = {(Xi, Y i)}Ni=1 and 

Dreworded = {(T (Xi), Y i)}Ni=1 , where T represents the rewording operation. In the semi-reworded setting, we test a realistic semantic shift scenario where the calibration set is drawn from Dorig 

(human-written inputs), but the test set is drawn from Dreworded (LLM-reworded inputs). Under this shift, the exchangeability assumption required for standard conformal prediction is violated for baselines like LAC and APS, as the distribution of their nonconformity scores (which depend on raw logits) changes between calibration and testing. 27 However, we hypothesize that our PA score remains robust to this shift. Our PA score is calculated by aggregating predictions over the set of paraphrases. We denote this paraphrasing generation as T ∗ to distinguish between the rewording used to create a distinct dataset ( T ) and the paraphrasing used to calculate the PA score ( T ∗). Consequently, for a calibration point X, the score S(X, Y ) depends on T ∗(X). For a test point T (X), the score depends on the composition T ∗(T (X)) . Invoking Assumption 5.1 (distributional closedness), we posit that T ∗(T (X)) d

≈ T ∗(X). Under the exact alignment and score conditions stated in our analysis, calibration and test scores retain the required exchangeability. Under approximate alignment, only the corresponding coverage-deviation bound applies. We do not establish these conditions for an arbitrary external rewriter and internal generator. Please refer to Table 15 which reports the observed clean-to-rewrite change at a fixed cutoff. 

Cluster Φ training in the semi-reworded setting. For QCCP, the conditioning feature Φ( X)

must remain exchangeable between calibration and test paired with the score s(X, Y ). In the semi-reworded setting, calibration uses X but test inputs are T (X). To preserve (Φ , s ) exchangeability for QCCP under this shift, we therefore train and apply the SBERT cluster Φ on a fixed paraphrase 

T †(X) (a single LM rewording, identical for calibration and test pieces), rather than on the raw original or raw rewritten text. The PA / LAC / APS / TRON / SPUQ scores themselves are computed exactly as in the main text; only the input to the cluster encoder is changed. We further evaluate our hypothesis in the semi-reworded setting using Llama-3.1-8B as both the dataset-level rewriter ( T ) and the internal paraphrase generator ( T ∗). Under split CP, PA achieves 89.97% coverage on CosmosQA and 90.27% on HellaSwag—both tightly aligned with the nominal 90%—while producing the smallest prediction sets (SS ≈ 1.41–2.03). Under QCCP, PA again reaches coverage closest to 90% and maintains compact sets, substantially outperforming both LAC and APS. Compared with Table 4, these results highlight that the quality of the paraphrase generator T ∗ is crucial: with a stronger model such as Llama-3.1-8B , empirical coverage improves for the evaluated generator. E.5 Semantic Consistency of Generated Paraphrases 

To verify that the generated paraphrases approximately preserve the semantics of the original ques-tions, we conduct a quantitative paraphrase quality evaluation across CosmosQA (RC), HellaSwag (CI), and MedMCQA (medical MCQA). For each dataset, we use the same paraphrase generation pipelines as in the main experiments and evaluate all paraphrases that are actually used for conformal prediction. 

Paraphrase sets and generators. Recall that for each original question x we use three paraphrase-related sets: (i) T ∗(x), the set of paraphrases used for PA score computation in the normal setting; (ii) T (x), the fully reworded questions used in the fully reworded setting; and (iii) T ∗(T (x)) , the paraphrases of fully reworded questions used for PA score computation in the fully reworded setting. We instantiate these sets using two independent LLM paraphrase generators: Llama-3.1-8B and 

Qwen3-8B . All quality metrics are reported separately for each generator and each scenario. 

Metrics. Given an original question x and a paraphrase p, we compute three standard similarity metrics: 1. SBERT cosine similarity. We use the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 SentenceTransformer to embed both 

x and p, normalize the embeddings, and compute 

cos( x, p ) = ⟨SBERT( x), SBERT( p)⟩.

This serves as a semantic similarity measure that is robust to minor lexical changes. 2. BLEU. We compute sentence-level BLEU scores using sacrebleu with effective order enabled, treating the original question as the reference and the paraphrase as the hypothesis. 3. ROUGE-L. Across CosmosQA and MedMCQA, we observe moderate to high SBERT cosine similarity (typically in the range 0.61 –0.87 , with Qwen3-8B paraphrases consistently above 0.81 ), indicating that the paraphrases remain close in semantic space to the original questions while allowing stylistic variation. To evaluate whether the PA nonconformity score depends on a particular LLM representation, we conduct a controlled sensitivity analysis on the Qwen3-8B model using the CosmosQA benchmark. In the main paper, PA uses the last-layer hidden state at the final token position. Here, we consider three alternative representations that are commonly used in transformer analysis: • Mean pooling over tokens. We average hidden states across all sequence positions (masking padding tokens). This yields a sequence-level representation that does not rely on a specific token location. • Intermediate-layer hidden states. Instead of the last layer, we extract representations from a configurable layer index. In the experiment below, we uses the second-to-last layer). We keep the extraction location fixed (final token), so only the layer depth varies. • Attention-weighted pooling. We use attention weights from the last layer to produce a weighted average over token embeddings. Specifically, we take the last token’s attention distribution, average over attention heads, and use this vector as weights over the hidden states of all tokens. 

Results. Tables 24 and 25 report coverage rate (CR) and set size (SS) under split CP. Across all pooling and layer choices, PA-based nonconformity scores remain extremely stable. This demon-strates that PA does not depend on a specific pooling strategy or layer depth, and that the semantic structure encoded throughout the transformer stack is sufficient for proxy training.

This suggests that our aggregation strategy effectively smooths out the noise induced by brittle paraphrases, yielding uncertainty estimates that better reflect the model’s underlying semantic knowledge rather than its sensitivity to surface-level variations. G.3 Extension to Factuality Verification Tasks 

While our primary framework addresses tasks with finite label spaces (i.e., |Y| < ∞), it naturally extends to critical sub-problems within open-ended generation, specifically factuality verification .Although the space of generated text is theoretically infinite, modern evaluation protocols typically decompose long-form generations into discrete atomic claims, each evaluated independently against a knowledge source [26, 5]. This decomposition effectively reduces the verification problem to a binary classification task. Formally, let an open-ended generation be parsed into a set of atomic claims {c1, c 2, . . . , c k}. We treat each claim ci as an input x ∈ X , where the label space is restricted to Y = {True , False }.Our paraphrase-robust framework is particularly well-suited for this setting due to the sensitivity of LLM-based evaluators to surface-level phrasing. To apply our method: 1. Paraphrase Generation: For a given claim x, we generate a set of semantic paraphrases B(x).2. Proxy Scoring: We utilize our lightweight proxy model Pθ (y | E(x)) to predict the truthfulness of the claim based on the embeddings of these paraphrases. 3. Robust Uncertainty: We calculate PA scores across B(x).By integrating these scores into the conformal prediction framework, we ensure that the resulting uncertainty estimates, as well as the subsequent decision to flag a claim as hallucinated, are robust to lexical diversity. This guarantees that a claim is not penalized merely due to the specific syntax chosen by the LLM, but rather evaluated based on its semantic validity. 38 Preliminary experiment on claim-verification. To provide concrete evidence beyond MCQA, we conducted a preliminary claim-verification experiment on FEVER [39], a standard benchmark for factuality verification. We evaluated the same three settings used in the main paper— clean ,

semi-reworded , and fully-reworded —and report coverage and average set size at α = 0 .1. As in the main text, coverage closer to 0.9 is better, while smaller set size is preferred. Table 30 and Table 31 show that the proposed PA score transfers meaningfully to this open-ended verification setting under Qwen3-8B . On FEVER, the backbone is sharp enough that all non-APS methods return essentially singleton prediction sets, so the main distinction is whether coverage stays closest to the nominal target under shift. PA remains the most stable method across settings, especially in the fully reworded case, where it reaches 90 .5% under Split CP and 90 .3% under QCCP, while LAC/TRON/SPUQ dip slightly below 90% . APS remains overly conservative, with prediction sets close to the full label set. These results suggest that the benefit of paraphrase-aware scoring is not specific to MCQA, but can also improve robustness in claim-level factuality verification, which serves as a practically relevant building block for open-ended generation evaluation. G.4 Extension to Image Modality 

Although our experiments focus on text-based tasks, the proposed framework is not limited to textual inputs and can, in principle, be extended to vision–language settings such as visual question answering (VQA). The key requirement of our method is access to a stable hidden representation for each input variant, which is naturally provided by modern vision–language models (VLMs). Concretely, given an image–question pair, we treat the visual input as part of the conditioning context and apply the same paraphrase-aware uncertainty pipeline at the representation level. In lieu of 39 textual paraphrases, visual perturbations can be used to induce semantic-preserving variants of the input image. For example, this can be realized by adding Gaussian noise to the image, producing multiple visually indistinguishable variants that preserve the underlying semantic content while altering surface-level pixel statistics. Each perturbed image is processed by a frozen VLM encoder, and we extract the final-layer hidden representation corresponding to the answer token, mirroring the text-only pipeline. Paraphrase-aware (variant-aware) nonconformity scores are then computed across these image variants and integrated into the same conformal prediction framework without modification. 

## H Limitations and Broader Impacts 

Limitations. The strongest variant requires hidden states and labeled proxy-training data. Online paraphrase generation is a material cost: the B200 six-view measurements are 22.7–83.4 times slower than raw single-input inference (Appendix E.6). The blackbox extension shows applicability, but still not in parity with the white-box model (Appendix G.1). Arbitrary test-only rewriting is still outside the coverage guarantee. Our framework relies on the quality of the paraphrase generator: if generated paraphrases exhibit semantic drift or limited diversity, the effectiveness of the paraphrase-aware score may degrade. Although we observe stable behavior across several paraphrase generators, domain-specific settings may require stronger or more specialized paraphrasing models. More broadly, our work focuses on semantic-preserving perturbations induced by paraphrasing and does not explicitly address other forms of distribution shift, such as topic drift, temporal changes in factual knowledge, or adversarial perturbations. In addition, our experiments are primarily conducted on English multiple-choice QA benchmarks. Extending paraphrase-aware conformal prediction to multilingual settings and free-form generation tasks remains an important direction for future work. 

Broader impacts. Our method improves the stability and reliability of LLM uncertainty estimates under semantic rewording, which may be particularly helpful for users whose phrasing differs from typical training distributions, such as non-native speakers or users of regional dialects. At the same time, prediction sets produced by conformal methods should not be interpreted as guarantees of correctness in high-stakes applications, but rather as uncertainty-aware decision support. 40
