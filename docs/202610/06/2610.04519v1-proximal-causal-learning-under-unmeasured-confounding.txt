# Abstract 

Estimating treatment effects from observational data typically relies on the No Unmeasured Confounding Assumption (NUCA), which rarely holds in practice. Proximal causal learning (PCL) addresses unmeasured confounding via proxy variables, yet existing methods require the proxy variables to be pre-specified. Thus, we propose PCL-U, a framework that learns proxy variables directly from observed covariates. PCL-U uses neural encoders to decompose covariates into treatment-inducing, outcome-inducing, and shared proxies, guided by minimax mutual information objectives, and obtains causal estimates through a practical moment-based risk function. Experiments on benchmarks show that PCL-U matches or outperforms existing baselines. Besides, there are two types of synthetic datasets with varying dimensions and confounding strengths that illustrate that our method maintains stable estimation accuracy. 

# 1 Introduction 

Causal inference is widely used to support decision-making in medicine, economics, and public policy (Ru-bin, 1974; Imbens and Rubin, 2015; Athey and Imbens, 2019). A central goal is to estimate treatment effects from data. While randomized controlled trials (RCTs) identify such effects by design, they are often unethical, prohibitively expensive, or infeasible (Bottou et al., 2013), motivating the use of observational data (Pearl, 2009). Observational studies, however, suffer from two challenges: each unit reveals only one potential outcome (Imbens and Rubin, 2015), and confounders induce distributional imbalance between treated and control groups, causing selection bias (Rosenbaum and Rubin, 1983). The problem becomes severe when some confounders are unmeasured: such latent variables af-fect both treatment and outcome but are absent from the data, violating NUCA and yielding biased estimates (Miao et al., 2018). Recent representation-learning methods (Shalit et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2022; You et al., 2025) address observed confounding by learning balanced representations, but

these methods require the NUCA (Rosenbaum and Rubin, 1983). These methods cannot verify that the learned representation captures all confounding (Wu et al., 2022), NUCA is untestable and often unrealistic in practice. When the NUCA is violated, Proximal causal learn-ing (PCL) (Miao et al., 2018; Tchetgen et al., 2020) offers an alternative by exploiting proxy variables— treatment-inducing proxies Z, outcome-inducing prox-ies W , and shared covariates X—that carry information about the unmeasured confounder U (Figure 1). How-ever, existing PCL methods assume valid proxies are known a priori (Xu et al., 2021; Mastouri et al., 2021; Kompa et al., 2022; Shao et al., 2025) or prespecified using domain knowledge. We therefore propose Proximal Causal Learning under Unmeasured confounding ( PCL-U ), which au-tomatically learns proxy variables from observed co-variates C and estimates treatment effects under un-measured confounding. PCL-U maps covariates C

through three encoders to learn proxy variables ˆZ, ˆX,ˆW , guided by surrogate objectives that encourage the designated proximal roles, followed by single-stage neu-ral estimation of potential outcomes using the NMMR-V formulation (Kompa et al., 2022). Our contributions are: 

• To our knowledge, this is the first work to integrate proxy learning into representation learning with observed covariates for treatment effect estimation. 

• On the IHDP benchmark, our method obtains the lowest PEHE and ϵAT E in both settings, reducing PEHE over the best baseline by 23.8% within-sample and 18.6% out-of-sample. 

• Two types of synthetic datasets are used to evalu-ate the limitations and robustness of our method in varying confounding scenarios, including dimen-sion and strength.

These methods still require valid proxies to be given in advance. Compared with representation-learning methods, PCL-U targets treatment effect estimation the proxi-mal causal framework which can handle unmeasured confounding. Compared with existing proximal meth-ods, it does not require proxy variables to be given in advance. Instead, PCL-U learns proxies directly from observed covariates and combines proxy learning with neural outcome bridge estimation in an end-to-end framework. 

# 3 Preliminaries 

## 3.1 Problem Setup 

For each unit i, we observe a binary treatment Ai ∈{0, 1}, an outcome Yi, and a pretreatment covariate vector Ci from a sample of n units. The observations 

{(Yi, A i, Ci)}ni=1 are assumed i.i.d. from a common dis-tribution P . Let Y (a) denote the potential outcome under treatment a ∈ { 0, 1} (Rubin, 1974). Our tar-get is to estimate the average treatment effect (ATE), 

τ = E{Y (1) − Y (0) }. Under the No Unmeasured Con-founding Assumption (NUCA), all confounders are assumed observed, which is rarely guaranteed in prac-tice. To identify τ without this assumption, we rely on treatment-inducing confounding proxies Z, outcome-inducing confounding proxies W and shared proxies X,which satisfy: 

Assumption 1 (Consistency and positivity) . The ob-served outcome satisfies Y = Y (A) a.s., and for each 

a ∈ { 0, 1}, 0 < P (A = a | U, X ) < 1 a.s. 

Assumption 2 (Latent exchangeability and condi-tional independence) . For each a ∈ { 0, 1}, the treat-ment assignment is conditionally ignorable given (U, X ),namely Y (a) ⊥ A | U, X , and the proxies satisfy 

Y ⊥ Z | A, U, X and W ⊥ (A, Z ) | U, X .

Assumption 3 (Completeness) . For any square-integrable function g and each a ∈ { 0, 1}, E{g(U ) |

Z, A = a, X } = 0 ⇒ g(U ) = 0 and E{g(W ) | Z, A =

a, X } = 0 ⇒ g(W ) = 0 almost surely. 

Assumption 1 states the causal consistency condition and the overlap requirement, ensuring that each unit has a positive probability of receiving either treatment level (Imbens and Rubin, 2015). Assumption 2 replaces the standard No Unmeasured Confounding Assumption with a proximal structure. The latent exchangeability condition Y (a) ⊥ A | U, X states that, conditioning on both the unobserved confounder U and the shared co-variates X, treated and control units are exchangeable. The exclusion restriction Y ⊥ Z | A, U, X requires that Z affects the outcome only through its influence on treatment, while W ⊥ (A, Z ) | U, X requires that 

W is excluded from the treatment mechanism given (U, X ). Assumption 3 imposes completeness condi-tions that accommodate both categorical and contin-uous confounders. We note that the completeness rules out conditional independence of U and Z given (A, X ). Completeness requires sufficient proxy infor-mation about U . # 4 Method 

We propose PCL-U (Proximal Causal Learning under Unmeasured confounding), an end-to-end framework that (i) learns proxy representations ( ˆZ, ˆX, ˆW ) from the observed covariate vector C, and (ii) estimates bridge functions Eq. (1) from the learned proxy rep-resentations. Throughout this section, Z, X, and W

denote the theoretical proximal variables in the iden-tification analysis, whereas ˆZ, ˆX, and ˆW denote the learned proxy representations derived from C. We now describe the designated roles of the learned represen-tations, motivated by the theoretical relationships in Assumption 2: 

• Treatment-inducing proxy ˆZ. The designated role of the treatment proxy is to retain information statistically associated with the treatment assignment 

A while providing no additional information about 

Y conditional on the treatment A, the unmeasured confounder U , and the shared proxy ˆX.

• Outcome-inducing proxy ˆW . The designated role of the outcome proxy is to retain information statistically associated with Y while being independent of A conditional on the unmeasured confounder U and the shared proxy ˆX.

• Shared proxy ˆX. The shared proxy ˆX captures confounding information relevant to both A and Y .Decomposition-based methods (You et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2022; Cheng et al., 2022) require independent representations. However, treatment effects can still be estimated under the proximal causal framework, even when residual undecomposed information U re-mains among the learned proxy variables, provided that the proximal identifying assumptions hold and an out-come bridge exists. Inspired by the mutual-information-based framework of Xu et al. (2022) and Cheng et al. (2022), we use minimax mutual-information-based rele-vance and exclusion surrogates to encourage the learned representations to exhibit the designated proxy roles; details are presented in Section 4.1. Because U is unobserved, these conditions cannot be verified from the mutual information objectives alone. Establishing sufficient conditions under which the proposed MI sur-rogates recover valid proximal representations remains a direction for future theoretical work. We further design two experiments to evaluate the feasibility of our method under different dimensions and strengths of U (see Section 5.4.2). 

## 4.1 Framework 

Fig. 2 illustrates the two components of PCL-U. Proxy learning uses three encoder networks Φ Z , Φ X , and ΦW , trained with mutual-information-based surrogate objectives, to map the observed covariates C to learned proxy variables ˆZ = Φ Z (C), ˆX = Φ X (C), and ˆW =ΦW (C). The vectors VZ , VX , and VW summarize the effective input weights of the encoders and are used to compute the regularization loss LO . Thus, Φ Z , Φ X ,and Φ W denote the encoder mappings, whereas ˆZ, ˆX,and ˆW denote their outputs used in the subsequent bridge estimation. Bridge estimation includes two outcome bridge networks, ( h1( ˆW , ˆX) and h0( ˆW , ˆX)), and a kernel function k( ˆZ, A, ˆX)( ˆZ′, A ′, ˆX′), which are combined to compute the bridge estimation loss Lh.The learned proxies are then supplied to the outcome bridges to estimate treatment effects. 3Figure 2: Overview of the PCL-U architecture.

## 5.2 Datasets 

We use IHDP (Hill, 2011), Jobs (LaLonde, 1986; Smith and Todd, 2005), and Twins (Almond et al., 2005) because they are established benchmarks that facilitate comparison with prior treatment-effect esti-mation methods. Their different application domains and evaluation protocols assess the general applicability of PCL-U. 

Synthetic datasets. Our central claim is that PCL-U can estimate causal effects in the presence of unmea-sured confounding. # 6 Conclusion 

We propose PCL-U, a framework that automatically learns proxy variables from observed covariates and incorporates them into neural bridge function estima-tors to estimate treatment effects in the presence of the unmeasured confounder U . Experiments on the IHDP, Jobs, and Twins datasets show that PCL-U matches or outperforms baselines based on the No Unmeasured Confounding Assumption. We further design and eval-uate synthetic datasets with varying dimensions and strengths of U , demonstrating the limitation imposed by the du > min( dz , d w) condition and showing that PCL-U remains effective as the confounding strength in-creases and achieves competitive empirical performance relative to NMMR without requiring prespecified prox-ies. Because the validity of learned proxies cannot be assessed directly from observed data, establishing the-oretical guarantees for their identifiability remains an open problem. # B Dataset Details 

IHDP (Hill, 2011). The IHDP dataset is a semi-synthetic benchmark based on the Infant Health and Development Program. It retains real covariates while synthesizing potential outcomes under a non-random treatment assignment. The dataset contains 747 units (139 treated and 608 control units) with 25 covariates. We employ a 63/27/10 train/validation/test split and replicate the experiment 100 times (Li and Yao, 2022). 

Jobs (LaLonde, 1986; Smith and Todd, 2005). The Jobs dataset studies the effect of job training on employment status by combining a randomized experiment with an observational comparison group from the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID). The randomized component from the National Supported Work program includes 722 units (425 control and 297 treated) with 17 covariates, while the PSID comparison group includes 2,490 control units, yielding 3,212 units in total. We employ a 56/24/20 train/validation/test split and replicate the experiment 10 times. 

Twins (Almond et al., 2005). The Twins dataset is derived from U.S. twin births recorded between 1989 and 1991, restricted to twins weighing less than 2 kg with complete covariate records. Each sample contains 30 covariates describing the parents, pregnancy, and birth. We set treatment A = 1 for the heavier twin and A = 0 for the lighter twin, with one-year mortality as the outcome. The final dataset contains 11,400 twin pairs. To simulate selection bias, we follow prior work (Louizos et al., 2017; Yoon et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2020) and retain one twin from each pair as the observed unit, with selection probability ai | xi ∼ Bern( σ(w⊤xi + n)), where 

w ∼ U (( −0.1, 0.1) 30 ×1) and n ∼ N (0 , 0.1). We employ a 56/24/20 train/validation/test split and replicate the experiment 10 times.

# F Implementation Details 

All experiments in Python on a device equipped with an Intel Xeon w7-3455 CPU, an NVIDIA GeForce GT 710 GPU, and 256 GB of memory. We implement PCL-U in TensorFlow and jointly optimize all components using Adam for 2,000 iterations with a mini-batch size of 128. The learning rate is initialized at 1 × 10 −3 and multiplied by 0 .97 every 100 iterations. The objective combines the MI-based proxy loss Lproxy , the deep orthogonality regularizer LO , the outcome bridge loss Lh, and ℓ2 regularization on the bridge-network parameters, with weights 

α, β, γ, and 3 × 10 −7, respectively. Each of the Z, X, and W representation networks has three hidden layers of width dim in and uses ELU activations. Its output is normalized by dividing each sample’s representation by its ℓ2 norm, with numerical stabilization ( normalization=’divide’ ). The bridge network has three ReLU hidden layers of width 80 and a linear output layer. We use zero-mean Gaussian weight initialization with scale 0.1 and disable dropout by setting both keep probabilities to 1. Table 9 reports the dataset-specific settings. Here, scale is the RBF kernel length scale, h split indicates whether treatment-specific bridge networks are used, batch norm indicates whether batch normalization is enabled, and outcome specifies whether the outcome is continuous or binary. For fixed network sizes and proxy dimensions, a mini-batch optimization update with b samples requires 

O(b2) time and auxiliary memory due to the pairwise kernel computation in Lh. Compared with NMMR using prespecified proxies, PCL-U incurs additional computation and parameter storage for the proxy encoders and variational networks.
