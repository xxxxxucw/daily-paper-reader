## Abstract 

Hardware-aware neural architecture search (NAS) is dominated by evaluation cost: every architecture must be trained before its reward is known. Conformal-prediction filters cut this cost by pruning candidates whose predicted-reward upper bound misses a threshold, with a distribution-free guarantee that at most a fraction δ are wrongly discarded. That guarantee assumes exchangeability between calibration and test candidates, which the surrounding reinforcement-learning (RL) loop violates: the policy’s proposals improve as search proceeds and, in layer-by-layer construction, shift within every episode. We replace one-shot quantile estimation with online feedback control (Adaptive Conformal Inference, with tuning-free, locally-adaptive, and group-conditional variants), restoring steerable coverage: dialing the target delivers it, monotonically and reproducibly, for arbitrary sequences. Across three neural-network architecture families and both single-step and sequential search (three seeds), it tracks every requested level to within ∼10 −3 while pruning 25–50% of evaluations at no measured accuracy cost, whereas static calibration loses control of its coverage and a Gaussian-process baseline stays conservative regardless of the request. Finally, used as an acquisition function on one constrained testbed, the same optimistic bound beats random search, a gain that fixed optimism already carries and online calibration sharpens. The source code is available at https://github.com/Vicomtech/rl-hw-nas .

Keywords: neural architecture search, hardware-aware NAS, conformal prediction, adaptive conformal inference, distribution shift, reinforcement learning, edge AI 

## 1 Introduction 

Deploying deep neural networks on resource-constrained edge devices (microcontrollers with a few hundred kilobytes of SRAM, embedded accelerators with hard real-time latency budgets) has become a central requirement of practical machine learning. On-device inference avoids the latency, cost, energy and privacy penalties of round-tripping data to the cloud, but it confronts the designer with a multi-objective problem that hand design handles poorly: accuracy must be traded against memory footprint, inference latency and quantization precision, simultaneously and per device. The design spaces are combinatorial (depth, width, kernel sizes, connectivity and per-layer bit-widths multiply into millions of configurations), and the accuracy of a configuration can only be observed by training it. Hardware-aware

neural architecture search (HW-NAS) automates this exploration by optimizing a scalar reward that combines task accuracy with hardware penalties, and has repeatedly produced edge models that dominate hand-designed baselines (Tan et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2019; Cai et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2020). The search strategies behind these results fall into a few families. Reinforcement-learning controllers learn a proposal distribution over architectures from observed rewards (Zoph and Le, 2017; Tan et al., 2019); evolutionary methods mutate populations of strong candidates (Real et al., 2019); differentiable methods relax the discrete space and optimize architecture parameters by gradient descent (Liu et al., 2018); supernet approaches such as Once-for-All train one weight-sharing network from which sub-networks are extracted without retraining (Cai et al., 2020); and Bayesian optimization (BO) builds a probabilistic surrogate of the reward and proposes candidates by expected utility (White et al., 2021). Whatever the proposal mechanism, a common bottleneck dominates the wall-clock cost: candidate evaluation . Each proposed architecture must be trained (even if only partially, on a subset of data) before its reward is known, and a realistic search consumes hundreds to thousands of such evaluations. A large fraction of them are wasted on candidates that were never competitive, which is why evaluation-avoidance mechanisms (reward predictors, early-stopping proxies, weight sharing) are as important to practical NAS as the search strategy itself. More fundamentally, on moderate spaces, well-tuned random search remains a remarkably strong baseline that learned controllers often fail to beat (Li and Talwalkar, 2020), so the marginal value of a NAS method lies less in the cleverness of its proposer than in how efficiently and safely it spends the evaluation budget. MARCO (Fayyazi et al., 2026) made this explicit for edge HW-NAS: a multi-agent reinforcement-learning search over MCU-scale CNN spaces in which a conformal-prediction filter prunes unpromising candidates before training. A cheap surrogate predicts each candidate’s reward; a split-conformal offset (Vovk et al., 2022; Papadopoulos et al., 2002) widens that prediction into an upper bound that is guaranteed (with probability at least 1 − δ) not to fall below the true reward; candidates whose bound misses an acceptance threshold are discarded without training. The construction is attractive precisely because the guarantee is distribution-free: it requires no assumption about the surrogate’s error distribution. It requires something else instead: exchangeability between the calibration candidates and the candidates the filter subsequently judges. That assumption is violated by the search itself. A reinforcement-learning policy improves; the distribution of proposed architectures later in the search is not the warm-up distribution the filter was calibrated on, and it keeps moving for as long as the policy learns. In sequential, layer-by-layer formulations of the search the violation is compounded within every episode, as the filter is asked to judge partial networks that grow from shallow stubs to deep architectures in a handful of steps. Under this drift the static conformal guarantee silently degrades: the realized coverage of the bound detaches from the requested 1 − δ, in a direction and magnitude that depend on where calibration happened to occur. The practical consequences are the two failure modes a pruning filter can have: wasted evaluations (over-coverage: the bound is looser than requested, so junk is trained anyway) and false discards (under-coverage: genuinely strong candidates are pruned unseen, an error that, unlike a wasted evaluation, can cost the best architecture in the space). MARCO acknowledges the issue and mitigates it by periodically recalibrating the filter; as we show, this reduces the variance of the failure but

not the failure itself, and re-estimating a single, stream-wide offset, however often, cannot correct the depth-dependent miscoverage a within-episode shift produces, because it never conditions on where in the episode the candidate sits. This paper replaces the static filter with an adaptive one, and then pushes the resulting machinery beyond filtering altogether. Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI, Gibbs and Cand` es, 2021) does not estimate a quantile once but controls the coverage error by online feedback, updating the conformal offset after every evaluation. Its guarantee is of a different kind: long-run coverage converges to the requested 1 − δ for arbitrary candidate sequences (no exchangeability, no stationarity), which is precisely the form of guarantee that survives inside a reinforcement-learning loop. On top of this foundation we remove ACI’s single hyperparameter by expert aggregation (following Zaffran et al., 2022), obtain per-candidate interval widths through normalized conformal scores, and obtain group-conditional coverage (coverage that holds at every layer depth of the sequential search, not merely on stream average) through a Mondrian construction. Finally, we observe that a calibrated optimistic bound is exactly what an exploration strategy needs: the same machinery that gates evaluations can steer them. Used greedily, it yields a distribution-free analogue of the Gaussian-process upper confidence bound (GP-UCB) (Srinivas et al., 2010), whose optimism is continuously verified against realized outcomes rather than derived from model assumptions; used with lookahead, it plans over the layer-by-layer construction tree with AlphaZero-style search (Silver et al., 2018), whose leaves are valued by the calibrated bound instead of training runs. Our contributions are as follows: 1. Adaptive conformal filtering under policy drift. We replace the static split-conformal filter with ACI, which keeps coverage calibrated and steerable under the policy drift that leaves static conformal filtering not merely biased but out of control .Across three architecture families and datasets (LeNet/MNIST, ResNet/CIFAR-10, MobileNet/CIFAR-100), in both single-step and sequential search, the adaptive filter tracks every requested coverage level 1 − δ to within ∼10 −3 with across-seed deviation two orders of magnitude below the baselines, while the periodically-recalibrated static filter over- and under-covers uncontrollably, cannot be steered by δ, and on the one landscape with an isolated optimum falsely discards the best architecture. The filter needs no step-size tuning in its aggregated form on these streams and prunes 25–50% of evaluations at no measured cost in solution quality. 2. Conditional conformal extensions for structured search. A normalized filter yields per-candidate interval widths (the prerequisite for any conformal acquisition, since a global offset cannot rank exploration targets), and a Mondrian filter yields per-depth coverage in the sequential search, where we show marginal calibration conceals a systematic shallow-over/deep-under coverage imbalance that per-group adaptation corrects wherever the search visits a depth often enough to calibrate it. 3. From pruning to search: conformal acquisition. We turn the calibrated bound into the search’s proposal mechanism, greedy acquisition over sampled pools, an optimism-shaped sequential controller, and a conformal-optimistic Monte-Carlo tree search whose simulated completions are valued by the bound so that real training

is spent only on committed candidates. On one constrained testbed where most architectures are infeasible (one family, three seeds), greedy optimistic acquisition reaches higher best-reward than random search at every budget up to 1,000 evaluations (and is the most reproducible searcher we test) while an attribution ablation locates the mechanism in the optimism: fixed optimism carries most of the gain, its online calibration adds a final edge and a ∼6× variance reduction over pure exploitation, and tree search buys only an early-budget head start. Shaping the controller’s own reward toward the objective, by contrast, does not reliably beat the unshaped controller and can destabilize it. 4. A controller-agnostic framework and an honest controller analysis. All filters and proposers sit behind common interfaces over interchangeable model families and hardware cost models, so every claim is tested across architectures, datasets and both search formulations, the single-step bandit and the sequential Markov decision process (MDP). Within this framework we also report a negative result that sharpens the paper’s thesis: the reinforcement-learning controller itself, faithful to MARCO, does not outperform random search on these spaces, it demonstrably learns, but it optimizes the expected reward while search rewards the argmax. The value added by our method is therefore located where the evidence places it: not in the learned controller, but in the calibrated bound, which, as a filter, gives drift-robust control of the evaluation budget behind any proposal mechanism, and, as an acquisition function, is itself a proposer that beats random search where the controller does not. We further contrast the conformal bounds with the natural Bayesian-optimization alternative throughout: a Gaussian-process upper confidence bound at the same nominal level is confidently miscalibrated under search-induced drift (its realized coverage is pinned near 0.99 regardless of the requested level, and it cannot be steered), which turns the standard “why not BO?” objection into one of our clearest experimental illustrations of why distribution-free, feedback-controlled uncertainty is the right tool inside a learning search loop. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work in hardware-aware NAS, conformal prediction and Bayesian optimization for architecture search. Section 3 details the methodology (the search problem, the controllers, the family of conformal filters and the conformal acquisition) together with the coverage guarantees each construction carries under drift. Section 4 reports the experiments: the headline calibration and steerability results, solution quality and false discards, the generality studies across families, datasets and search formulations, the controller comparison, and the directed search. Section 5 concludes with limitations and future directions. 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Hardware-aware neural architecture search 

Hardware-aware NAS has grown into a broad subfield, surveyed and taxonomized by search space, search strategy and hardware cost-estimation approach (Benmeziane et al., 2021). Architecture search began as an accuracy-only problem solved with reinforcement learning 

> 4Coverage You Can Steer

at extravagant cost (Zoph and Le, 2017; Baker et al., 2017), and was quickly reoriented toward deployment constraints. MnasNet (Tan et al., 2019) folded measured mobile latency into the reward; ProxylessNAS (Cai et al., 2019) and FBNet (Wu et al., 2019) made latency differentiable through per-operator lookup tables; Once-for-All (Cai et al., 2020) decoupled training from search by extracting sub-networks from a single weight-sharing supernet; MCUNet (Lin et al., 2020) pushed the setting to microcontrollers, co-designing the architecture with the inference engine under kilobyte-scale memory budgets. Differentiable search over relaxed spaces (Liu et al., 2018) offers a third route, though its stability and its coupling to a specific supernet limit portability across cost models. Evolutionary NAS provides a fourth route, favored for treating resource budgets as explicit constraints, with recent frameworks making the constraint handling itself adaptive: Xie et al. (2026), for example, ramps a Params/FLOPs penalty across evolutionary generations and embeds structured filter pruning as a local operator. Our work operates in the MCU-scale regime of MCUNet and MARCO (small CNN spaces with per-layer quantization choices, evaluated against SRAM and latency budgets), but the contribution is orthogonal to search-space or cost-model design: we target the statistical machinery that decides which candidates are worth evaluating, and it applies unchanged across the families and oracles we test. 

2.2 Reducing the evaluation cost 

Since candidate training dominates NAS wall-clock time, a large literature attacks evaluation cost directly. Partial-training proxies truncate epochs and data; weight sharing amortizes training across candidates (Pham et al., 2018; Cai et al., 2020); learned performance predictors rank candidates from architecture features (White et al., 2021; Wen et al., 2020); zero-cost proxies score untrained networks from single minibatches (Mellor et al., 2021; Abdelfattah et al., 2021). A complementary family allocates evaluation resource adaptively rather than predicting outcomes: multi-fidelity bandits such as Hyperband cast configuration evaluation as pure-exploration bandit allocation, pouring successively more epochs or data into surviving candidates and early-stopping the rest (Li et al., 2017), the same research program that furnishes our random-search baseline (Li and Talwalkar, 2020). That line is orthogonal to ours: it decides how much to spend on a candidate, whereas our filter decides, with a calibrated error statement, whether to spend at all, and the two compose. What these methods share is that their predictions carry no calibrated error statement : a predictor used greedily will confidently mis-rank in regions it has not seen, and the cost of that failure (discarding the best architecture unseen) is invisible in average-error metrics. Our filter wraps exactly such a predictor (a small multilayer perceptron, MLP) in a conformal envelope, converting point predictions into bounds with a controlled false-discard rate; the guarantee, not the predictor, is the contribution, and better predictors tighten the resulting bounds without changing the argument. 

2.3 Reinforcement-learning controllers, and how strong random search is 

RL controllers for NAS span single-agent policy gradient (Zoph and Le, 2017), weight sharing (Pham et al., 2018), and Q-learning over layer sequences (Baker et al., 2017). MARCO (Fayyazi et al., 2026), our departure point, structures the controller as cooperating agents under centralized training / decentralized execution (CTDE; cf. MADDPG, Lowe et al., 

> 5Brandimarte, Aranjuelo, Nieto and Otaegui

2017; MAPPO, Yu et al., 2022): a hardware-configuration agent selects macro-architecture knobs and a quantization agent selects per-layer bit-widths, trained with proximal policy optimization (PPO) on a hardware-aware reward, with candidates built layer by layer in a sequential MDP. We reproduce this controller faithfully (including the per-layer formulation and per-step credit assignment) as the substrate for our filtering study. A parallel evaluation literature, however, has repeatedly found that well-tuned random search matches or beats learned NAS controllers on moderate spaces (Li and Talwalkar, 2020; Yang et al., 2020). Our results reproduce this finding for the MARCO controller and diagnose its mechanism (the policy optimizes expected reward while search rewards the argmax); rather than contradicting our approach, this finding relocates the value of NAS machinery to where our contribution sits (the calibrated, controller-agnostic management of the evaluation budget) and motivates the argmax-aligned acquisition search of our final contribution. 

2.4 Bayesian optimization and optimism-based search 

Bayesian optimization is the canonical sample-efficient search: a probabilistic surrogate (typically a Gaussian process, GP) plus an acquisition rule, with GP-UCB supplying optimism in the face of uncertainty and regret guarantees under correct model specification (Srinivas et al., 2010). BO has been adapted to architecture spaces through tailored kernels (Kandasamy et al., 2018) and neural predictors with ensemble uncertainty (White et al., 2021). The known weakness is that the uncertainty is model-based : coverage of GP credible intervals is an artifact of kernel and noise assumptions, and miscalibration under distribution shift is well documented. Closest to our acquisition track, Stanton et al. (2023) combine BO with conformal prediction sets to protect acquisition decisions against feedback covariate shift , the train/test distribution shift a design algorithm induces by choosing where to query (Fannjiang et al., 2022). Our setting differs in the drift source (an improving RL policy and within-episode depth shift, not only the feedback shift induced by acquisition itself), in the online mechanism (ACI’s feedback control rather than weighted split-conformal recalibration), and in the search space (discrete layer-by-layer construction, where the bound also drives tree search). We include GP-UCB at matched nominal levels as a baseline throughout, and find it confidently miscalibrated on the search stream (realized coverage pinned near 0.99 whatever level is requested), which empirically substantiates the case for distribution-free, feedback-controlled uncertainty inside a learning loop. 

2.5 Tree search for architecture construction 

Monte-Carlo tree search (MCTS) has been applied to NAS by AlphaX (Wang et al., 2020), which guides MCTS with a learned value predictor, and LaNAS (Wang et al., 2021), which learns to partition the space to focus sampling. These works establish that the layer-by-layer construction process is a natural planning tree; they evaluate leaves with surrogate predictors but attach no calibrated statement to the values that guide exploration, and they spend real evaluations by fixed schedules. Our conformal-optimistic MCTS keeps the AlphaZero-style structure (Silver et al., 2018), with priors from the controller and values backed up through PUCT (predictor-weighted upper confidence bounds for trees, the AlphaZero selection rule), but values every simulated completion with a calibrated optimistic bound whose coverage is controlled online under drift, and spends real training runs only on committed candidates,

with the bound’s online updates closing the loop between planning optimism and realized outcomes. 

2.6 Conformal prediction on non-exchangeable data 

Conformal prediction converts any predictor into set- or interval-valued predictions with finite-sample marginal coverage, assuming only exchangeability (Vovk et al., 2022; Shafer and Vovk, 2008); the split (inductive) construction makes it cheap enough to deploy around learned models (Papadopoulos et al., 2002; Lei et al., 2018). Locally-adaptive scores normalize by a difficulty estimate to obtain per-input interval widths (Papadopoulos et al., 2008), Mondrian (group-conditional) constructions give per-group validity at the cost of per-group data (Vovk et al., 2022; Bostr¨ om and Johansson, 2020), and recent work characterizes the interpolation between marginal and fully conditional guarantees (Gibbs et al., 2025). The frontier relevant to us is coverage without exchangeability: reweighting handles known covariate shift (Tibshirani et al., 2019), robust variants bound the coverage gap under bounded drift (Barber et al., 2023), and Adaptive Conformal Inference (Gibbs and Cand` es, 2021) abandons distributional assumptions altogether, controlling long-run coverage on arbitrary sequences by online updates. An active line of follow-ups builds on ACI: removing its step size by expert aggregation (Zaffran et al., 2022), strengthening its regret and coverage dynamics (Bhatnagar et al., 2023; Gibbs and Cand` es, 2024; Angelopoulos et al., 2023), and analyzing decaying-step-size variants that trade drift-tracking for convergence (Angelopoulos et al., 2024), a trade our constant-step-size choice deliberately refuses. Applications of conformal methods inside sequential decision loops are emerging: safe autonomous decisions (Lekeufack et al., 2024), online prediction under semi-bandit feedback (Ge et al., 2025), and conformal BO (Stanton et al., 2023). The semi-bandit setting shares a censored-feedback structure with our own filter, which observes rewards only for the candidates it accepts. Architecture search, however, has seen conformal prediction only in MARCO’s static form, where it filters candidates under an exchangeability assumption the surrounding RL loop violates. To our knowledge, this work is the first to bring online conformal inference into a NAS loop (matching the guarantee to the drift the search actually produces), the first to measure and correct its depth-conditional coverage in sequential construction, and the first to use the resulting calibrated bound as the search’s acquisition function. 

## 3 Methodology 

We address hardware-aware neural architecture search on edge devices, where the dominant cost is candidate evaluation: every proposed architecture must be (partially) trained before its reward can be observed. Our method couples a multi-agent reinforcement-learning search controller, in the style of MARCO (Fayyazi et al., 2026), with a conformal-prediction early-filter that prunes unpromising candidates before training. Our central departure from MARCO is the filter itself: static split-conformal calibration assumes exchangeability between calibration and test candidates, an assumption that reinforcement-learning policy improvement violates by construction. We replace it with adaptive conformal inference, which maintains a distribution-free coverage guarantee on the non-stationary candidate stream, and we extend it with tuning-free, locally-adaptive and group-conditional variants.

3.1.3 Evaluation proxy and quantization 

Evaluating A(a) by full training is infeasible inside a search loop, so we use the standard partial-training proxy: each candidate is trained for E epochs on a fixed, seed-determined random fraction ρ of the training set (identical for all candidates, so the proxy ranks them on equal footing), and A(a) is its top-1 accuracy on the held-out evaluation split. The default setting is ( E, ρ ) = (5 , 0.1). For search-quality comparisons we additionally employ a stronger proxy ( E, ρ ) = (15 , 0.3): as Section 4 shows, the weak-proxy reward landscape of the ResNet-like family collapses to an isolated optimum typically found by chance during warm-up, which cannot separate search methods; the stronger proxy restores a climbable landscape. Coverage results are reported on the proxy each experiment ran on, calibration measures bound-versus-outcome tracking and is therefore largely proxy-agnostic. Bit-width choices are made accuracy-relevant through fake post-training quantization (PTQ, Jacob et al., 2018): after proxy training, each block’s weights are quantized to its chosen bi before scoring, so low bit-widths reduce cost and potentially accuracy, rather than receiving cost-model credit for free.

3.3 Conformal evaluation filtering 

3.3.1 The filtering loop 

Candidate evaluation is the search bottleneck, so we prune before training. All filters share a common interface with three parts. First, they are calibrated on a warm-up set. Second, they expose a per-candidate reward upper bound U (x), computed from the candidate’s feature encoding x = x(a) (defined together with the surrogate below). Third, after each real evaluation they receive the candidate’s features x and its measured reward r = R(a)(Eq. (1)) as feedback. What each filter does with that feedback (a per-step offset update, a periodic recalibration, or no change after warm-up) is specified per filter below. The filtered search loop proceeds as follows: 1. Warm-up. The first W candidates are always evaluated (the filter is inactive), forming the initial calibration set. 2. Filtering. Thereafter, a proposed candidate is evaluated if and only if U (x) ≥ τ ,where τ is the user-chosen acceptance threshold on the reward; otherwise it is pruned 

(never trained) and the policy receives a fixed rejection reward rrej = −1. 3. Feedback. Every real evaluation ( x, r ) is fed back to the filter (updating it to the filter-specific degree above). The rule is one-sided by design: the filter must guarantee that pruning rarely discards a candidate whose true reward would have reached τ , i.e. that r ≤ U (x) holds with probability at least 1 − δ, where δ ∈ (0 , 1) is the user-specified tolerated failure rate of the bound. Empirical coverage is measured on the post-warm-up evaluated stream as the fraction of candidates with r ≤ U (x); the pruned fraction is the evaluation saving. In the sequential MDP the filter gates each step’s partial-network evaluation, and coverage is additionally reported conditional on layer depth , where the within-episode distribution shift is strongest. For uncensored measurement of depth-conditional coverage we use a no-prune audit mode in which every candidate is evaluated (pruning disabled) while the filter otherwise runs exactly as in a live search, so coverage is observable at depths that a realistic τ would otherwise censor. 

3.3.2 Reward surrogate 

The surrogate g(x) is a three-layer multilayer perceptron (hidden width 64, ReLU), trained by mean-squared error, matching MARCO’s surrogate. Its input is the family-specific

feature encoding x(a): each categorical knob is one-hot encoded and the per-block bit-widths become one binary flag per block, zero-padded to the family’s maximum depth, giving a fixed dimension d per family (e.g. d = 23 for the ResNet-like family); the sequential space encodes per-layer slots for all possible layers plus cumulative-context features. Whenever a filter is (re)calibrated, the available data is split: the surrogate is fit on the first half and nonconformity scores are computed on the held-out second half, following split (inductive) conformal prediction (Papadopoulos et al., 2002). The split is essential for validity, residuals computed on the surrogate’s own training data understate its generalization error.

3.5 Evaluation protocol 

• Coverage. The fraction of post-warm-up evaluated candidates with r ≤ U (x); reported marginally and, in the sequential MDP, conditional on layer depth (measured in the no-prune audit mode of Sec. 3.3.1 to avoid censoring). 

• Calibration error and steerability. |coverage − (1 − δ)|, swept over 

δ ∈ {0.05 , 0.1, 0.2, 0.3}. A filter is steerable if its realized coverage moves monotonically with the requested level, the operational property a practitioner needs from the δ dial. 

• Evaluation saving. The pruned fraction of proposed candidates (filtered search), or the best reward reached at matched evaluation budgets (directed search). 

• Solution quality. The best accuracy/reward discovered, compared across filters at fixed seed: a miscalibrated bound harms solution quality only by falsely discarding 

top candidates, which this comparison exposes directly. 

• Reproducibility. Across-seed standard deviation of coverage (three seeds for the calibration sweeps; eight for the depth-conditional audits, whose per-depth estimates are far noisier because each depth group holds only a fraction of the stream). Default settings are summarized in Table 1.

4.3 Solution quality: pruning is free, miscalibration is not 

Across every δ, seed and family, the adaptive and agaci arms discover the same best architecture as the unfiltered baseline, pruning 25–50% of evaluations costs no measured solution quality. The same held for every filter except one : on the ResNet-like family, the faithful MARCO baseline ( split-recal ) consistently returned a strictly worse optimum (best accuracy 0.481 vs 0.505 for every other arm, across seeds and δ). The per-trial logs identify the mechanism directly: its recalibration-churned, miscalibrated bound falsely discarded the best architecture (pruned it, never trained it) on every seed. The cross-family pattern shows when this failure actually costs anything, and the answer is the shape of the landscape. It hurts the final result only where the optimum is isolated :on the ResNet-like family’s single sharp peak, split-recal ’s best accuracy falls 0 .024 below every other arm. Where instead a cluster of near-optimal architectures exists, as in the LeNet-like family’s flat ≈0.98 plateau (penalty ≈0.002) and the MobileNet-like family’s upper cluster (penalty 0), a false discard still leaves an equally good neighbour, so the final accuracy is unchanged. Calibration is thus cheap insurance: it costs almost nothing in solution quality, and its value shows up precisely on the landscapes where the search cannot afford to lose a single needle.

Shaping the controller’s own reward does not help. A separate question is whether the same optimism can be injected into the RL controller itself rather than used as an external proposer: the optimism-shaped controller (Eq. (14)) shapes the per-step reward toward improvement-over-incumbent plus an optimism bonus. Comparing the shaped controller against its unshaped counterpart at matched episodes (eight seeds, per-step currency, so the comparison is within this pair, not against the arms above), shaping does not reliably win: on the outlier-robust median the unshaped controller is marginally ahead on reward (0 .67 vs 0 .63) and the shaped one marginally ahead on discovered accuracy (0 .72 vs 0 .70), both within seed noise. ## 5 Conclusion 

Conformally-filtered architecture search rests on a promise: candidates are pruned without training only under a controlled probability of discarding a good one. We showed that in the setting where this idea is deployed (a reinforcement-learning search loop) the static split-conformal construction that makes the promise cannot keep it, because the loop’s own policy improvement destroys the exchangeability the guarantee is built on. Moreover, we observed that the failure is not merely a bias but a loss of control : realized coverage carries a systematic tilt (unresponsive to the requested risk level, under-covering at tight targets, over-covering at loose ones) and lands unpredictably across seeds, so no fixed correction recovers it. Periodic recalibration (a faithful version of the prior work taken as baseline) reduces the variance of this failure without restoring control, and on the one benchmark landscape whose optimum is isolated it falsely discards the best architecture on every seed. 

> 27 Brandimarte, Aranjuelo, Nieto and Otaegui

Replacing quantile estimation with online feedback control repairs this at negligible cost. Adaptive conformal inference tracks every requested coverage level to within ∼10 −3,monotonically and reproducibly (seed deviation ∼0.002, one to two orders of magnitude below the baselines), across three architecture families and datasets, in both the single-step and the sequential per-layer formulations of the search, while pruning 25–50% of candidate evaluations at no measured cost in solution quality. On these streams the result did not depend on the step-size choice: aggregating ACI experts over a fixed grid matches the fixed-γ

filter to within 0.004 with nothing chosen by hand. The comparison with a Gaussian-process upper confidence bound at the same nominal level sharpens the point of principle: model-based uncertainty on this non-exchangeable stream is confidently miscalibrated (pinned near 0.99 coverage whatever level is requested), whereas distribution-free feedback control delivers the level asked for. Inside learning loops, uncertainty that is verified against outcomes is better than uncertainty that is assumed from a model. Two further results delimit what calibration can and cannot buy. In the sequential search, marginal coverage (the guarantee ACI formally carries) conceals a systematic imbalance across construction depth whose direction is family-dependent (deep layers under-cover on two families, shallow-mid layers on the third); per-depth Mondrian adaptation restores group-conditional coverage exactly where the search visits a depth often enough to calibrate it (a flat, at-target line across every depth when all groups have data) without knowing in advance where the hardness lies. And the reinforcement-learning controller itself, reproduced faithfully from prior work, does not outperform random search at matched budget: it demonstrably learns, but it optimizes the expected reward while search rewards the argmax. We report this negative result deliberately: it locates the practical value of this line of work not in the learned controller but in the calibrated bound itself (controller-agnostic management of the evaluation budget that transfers to any proposer) and it motivates our final direction, in which that same bound becomes the proposal mechanism. On one constrained testbed, our proposed greedy optimistic acquisition beats random search at every budget up to 1,000 evaluations and is the most reproducible searcher we test. An attribution ablation traces most of the gain to the optimism itself (fixed optimism already beats random) with online calibration adding a final edge and a marked reduction in seed variance. 

5.1 Limitations Hardware cost model. Our oracle is analytical and monotone but not calibrated to a physical device. The calibration and steerability results are unaffected (the filter’s coverage tracks whatever reward the oracle produces) so the cost model bears only on the absolute hardware interpretation: hardware-aware conclusions concern orderings and budget-binding regimes, not absolute latencies or memory footprints, and the framework’s claimed hardware generality is demonstrated only across parameterizations of one MCU-style model, with validation against measured cost models (GPU, NPU, and a physical edge target) left to future work. 

Proxy fidelity. All rewards derive from partial-training proxies. Calibration claims are unaffected (the bound provably tracks whatever the proxy outputs) but solution-quality claims inherit the proxy’s ranking fidelity, and whether proxy rankings survive full training 

> 28 Coverage You Can Steer

is not tested here. Relatedly, the proxy’s strength shapes what experiments can measure at all: on the weak proxy the ResNet-like family’s landscape degenerates to a needle found by warm-up luck, which is why search-quality comparisons use the stronger proxy and why per-family recalibration of the acceptance threshold τ is a prerequisite, not a tuning nicety. 

Conditional coverage under data-starved or structurally harder groups. Mon-drian adaptation closes the depth-conditional gap only where a depth group has enough data to calibrate it; where a group is starved (the deepest depths on the ResNet-like family whose search rarely reaches them) the guarantee falls back toward the marginal one. More data narrows the gap, whether pooled into coarser depth bins or supplied by a substrate that visits those depths more often (the strong proxy), but does not fully close it. And on the LeNet-like family the terminal construction depth under-covers for every method even when well-sampled (episode-final networks there carry the full cost penalties and concentrate the policy’s drift) while on the MobileNet-like family it calibrates exactly, so structurally harder groups exist but are family-specific rather than a law of depth. Group-conditional validity under such groups, where they occur, remains open. 

Coverage measurement under pruning. In filtered runs, coverage is observable only on accepted candidates, a τ -dependent population. For the adaptive filter this is benign in practice: the τ -sweep (Sec. 4.2) shows its coverage τ -invariant across the operating range (0 .899–0 .901, loosening only at the reward ceiling), and uncensored no-prune audits sidestep the conditioning entirely for both the bandit and sequential experiments: on the full, unpruned bandit stream the calibrated arms still track every requested level to ∼10 −3

(0 .949 /0.899 /0.800 /0.700 on the ResNet-like/CIFAR-10 and LeNet-like/MNIST families). Closing it fully is a censoring problem, not a compute one: pruned candidates are never trained, so their rewards are unobserved by construction, and the accepted-only estimate is censored by the very selection it would measure. Recovering coverage on the full proposed population would require either training the pruned candidates (the no-prune audit, which is not the live filtered condition) or a statistical selection-bias correction, a methodological gap shared with prior work (Fayyazi et al., 2026). 

Scope of the search problem. The empirical base is CNN classification on MCU-scale spaces, chosen for faithful comparison with prior work. Our empirical base is from-scratch architecture search over CNN families: candidates are proposed by varying trainable archi-tecture parameters and scored by partial training. The filter itself assumes only an expensive, reward-producing evaluation, so it should wrap other regimes unchanged—structurally differ-ent spaces (transformer families), different evaluation modalities (e.g. warm-start fine-tuning of pretrained models rather than from-scratch training), detection/segmentation tasks, and real deployment toolchains—but that transfer is asserted by design, not yet by experiment. 

Directed search. The conformal-acquisition results (Sec. 4.6) are the paper’s narrowest evidence base (one family, one constrained testbed, three seeds), so whether the calibration edge and the greedy-over-tree-search advantage generalize across families remains open. The filter contribution is independent of this track. 

Statistical power. Three seeds for the calibration sweeps and eight for the depth audits suffice for the headline effects (10–100 × calibration-error ratios) but leave wide intervals on the sparse deep-depth estimates and the small-budget search comparisons; both are flagged where reported.

5.2 Outlook 

The broader suggestion of this work is that the statistical machinery inside learning-driven search loops should carry guarantees matched to the non-stationarity those loops create. Distribution-free online calibration proved to be a drop-in upgrade for evaluation filtering; the same quantity (a bound whose meaning is continuously verified) is a natural currency for exploration, resource rationing, and stopping rules. Extending calibrated search from pruning to full acquisition, from marginal to conditional validity on structured state spaces, and from proxy rewards to deployed, device-measured objectives are, in our view, the three most consequential next steps.
