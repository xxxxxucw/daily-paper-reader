# Evaluation of Active Feature Acquisition Policies with Tabular Foundation Models 

## Yuta Kobayashi ∗ Divyam Madaan ∗ Shalmali Joshi ∗

Abstract 

Active feature acquisition learns policies that sequentially acquire features to maximize information about a target variable. We study how to learn and evaluate such policies from finite offline data using prior-data fitted networks (PFNs), which are off-the-shelf models that output posterior predictive distri-butions without task-specific training. We show that under the imbalanced coverage of offline data, using total predictive entropy as a reward creates an epistemic bias that penalizes acquiring sparsely observed features. Specifically, this reward conflates epistemic uncertainty (arising from lack of offline data) with 

aleatoric uncertainty (arising from uninformative features). To address this, we target the posterior ex-pected (aleatoric) entropy instead of the total predictive entropy output by a PFN for evaluating feature acquisitions. Empirical evaluations on synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate that our approach consistently reduces value estimation bias and yields credible intervals with strong empirical coverage, which can translate to improved downstream policy selection. 

# 1 Introduction 

In many critical decision-making domains, acquiring complete information is often constrained by monetary cost, operational delays, or physical risks (Trapeznikov et al., 2012; Janisch et al., 2020; Erion et al., 2022). This challenge is particularly prominent in clinical risk prediction (Erion et al., 2022). Active Feature Acquisition (AFA) frames feature acquisition as a sequential decision-making problem that adaptively selects which features to measure next with the goal of maximally reducing uncertainty about a target variable. For example, relying on principles of Bayesian Experimental Design, AFA optimizes feature selection by reducing entropy in classification tasks or variance in regression tasks (Ma et al., 2018; Covert et al., 2023; Chattopadhyay et al., 2023). Relying on the total predictive uncertainty from a PPD estimate to evaluate feature acquisitions conflates the sources of uncertainty that can be reduced through acquisitions (Kobayashi et al., 2025). We prove that under standard information gain objectives, this conflation underestimates feature acquisition reward by inflating uncertainty in regions of low data support. We show that, relative to the aleatoric objective, the error of the total-uncertainty objective is exactly the epistemic uncertainty at the final state, which penalizes policies that acquire sparsely observed features. In contrast, we recover the bias-corrected estimates of information from feature acquisitions by projecting the potential reduction in aleatoric uncertainty, leading to the Bayes-optimal selection criterion. For example, an observational study of Alzheimer’s disease risk may collect measurements X and a binary outcome Y under a standard-of-care protocol that relies mainly on cognitive tests and blood biomarkers, acquiring invasive cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples predominantly in a high-risk subgroup (see Figure 1). We refer to this (potentially unknown) mechanism as the behavior policy, which introduces retrospective missingness into historical observations. A clinical researcher may want to evaluate a candidate feature acquisition criterion (i.e., the target policy) hypothesized to predict disease while reducing the number of patients for whom invasive CSF is required. Under the total-uncertainty objective, however, acquiring CSF may appear less informative than it is because sparse coverage in the historical data inflates the epistemic uncertainty at the resulting states. Building on this insight, we develop a Bayesian estimation procedure to recover the posterior distribution of the expected information gain for target acquisition policies, leveraging positivity and missing-at-random (MAR) assumptions to ensure causal identification of the policy value (von Kleist et al., 2025). We instantiate this estimator using Bayesian predictive inference (BPI), which directly recovers the posterior through a sequence of one-step predictive distributions (Fong et al., 2023; Fortini & Petrone, 2025). This allows tabular foundation models to perform principled uncertainty decomposition, allowing us to directly estimate the aleatoric objective (posterior mean) of our AFA policy value (Nagler & R¨ ugamer, 2025; Melnychuk et al., 2026). We organize the paper as follows: 

• (Section 3 ) We demonstrate that using total predictive uncertainty as a surrogate reward introduces 

epistemic bias . We define this bias as the systematic overestimation of uncertainty—and the consequent underestimation of information gain—for features with low support, caused by retrospective missingness in finite offline data. 

• (Section 4 ) We construct a Bayesian estimator for the target policy’s information gain and instantiate it via Bayesian Predictive Inference (BPI). This bypasses traditional prior specification, seamlessly integrates tabular foundation models, and mitigates epistemic bias while yielding rigorous credible intervals for policy evaluation. 

• (Section 6 ) We empirically validate our approach across synthetic environments and real-world datasets. We show that BPI estimators leveraging specialized PFNs and pretrained foundation models (TabICL) successfully circumvent epistemic bias and provide reliable uncertainty bounds.

# 4 Bayesian Predictive Inference for AFA Value Estimation 

In this section, we present our framework for estimating the AFA policy value. By explicitly estimating the aleatoric component of Equation (2) under the target policy, our approach guarantees a bias-corrected reward estimate, unconfounded by Ue. Section 4.1 introduces Bayesian predictive inference (BPI). This framework derives posterior distributions for quantities of interest directly from a posterior predictive distribution (PPD). Since modern foundation models are typically trained with an autoregressive loss, using data samples as context trained with a next-token prediction autoregressive loss effectively yields a PPD estimator. For instance, TabPFN (M¨ uller et al., 2021) is an off-the-shelf foundation model that can be used as a PPD estimator combined with in-context learning to adapt to new tasks. Section 4.2 details how we compute the posterior over the finite sample estimate of the target AFA functional, ¯G(Pobs ), to estimate the policy value. 

## 4.1 Bayesian Predictive Inference for Estimating Ua

Standard Bayesian estimation requires explicit specification of a prior-likelihood pair, which is often difficult to design in practice. Instead, we investigate BPI, which performs Bayesian estimation using a sequence of one-step-ahead predictive distributions (such as PPDs) defined directly on observable quantities. Under this framework, statistical uncertainty arises strictly from finite sample sizes. With infinite observations, the quantity of interest could be identified precisely. Given n samples, however, the samples unobserved so far (n + 1 → ∞ ) are the sole source of Ue.Let Z ∈ Z be a generic random variable with distribution P(Z), and let F denote the CDF. # 5 Related Work 

Active Feature Acquisition (AFA) formulates feature selection as a sequential decision-making process, where the goal is to learn policies that dynamically select which features to acquire next to optimally predict a target variable. Previous studies have predominantly focused on optimization of task-specific acquisition for a static offline dataset (Shim et al., 2018; Kachuee et al., 2019; Janisch et al., 2019), and we focus on approaches that define the learning objective based on greedy or multi-step information gain (Covert et al., 2023; Gadgil et al., 2023; Ghosh & Lan, 2023; Kobayashi et al., 2025). To estimate the optimal acquisition policy, existing methods typically rely on either generative sampling-based techniques (Ma et al., 2018) or policy networks that directly output the optimal next action (Covert et al., 2023; Guney et al., 2025; Aronsson & Chehreghani, 2026). In contrast, our work focuses strictly on the evaluation of pre-defined acquisition policies under offline data with inherent missingness (von Kleist et al., 2025). Rather than proposing a new optimization method, our goal is to formulate and validate estimators for the policy value driven by tabular foundation models (PFNs). This integration provides a highly practical and scalable inference mechanism for offline data, yielding strong empirical performance without the overhead of tuning bespoke models. 66 Experiments 

Evaluation Metrics. We evaluate our approach on two primary dimensions: the quality of the uncertainty estimates for the policy value, and the accuracy of downstream acquisition policy selection. We define a 

ground-truth reference value either analytically using closed-form expressions in simulations, or empirically using a large oracle dataset without missingness. 

• Bias of point estimate: The error relative to the reference value determines whether our aleatoric entropy estimator successfully mitigates epistemic bias. 

• Coverage assessment: A standard diagnostic for uncertainty quantification is frequentist coverage (Ng et al., 2025), which verifies whether the epistemic uncertainty captured by the posterior reliably bounds the reference estimand. The resulting empirical coverage rate of the credible intervals should be close to 1 − α where we select α = 0 .05. We also report the interval width. 

• Ranking and regret of candidate acquisitions: We measure the policy regret and the relative accu-racy of action selection compared to the reference estimand, reporting pairwise accuracy (comparing the relative ordering accuracy of all candidate pair combinations). We defer additional evaluation of epistemic uncertainty estimates for the conditional Y | ST using various known diagnostics such as posterior contraction (Ng et al., 2025) to Section D.4. 

Baselines. We evaluate six estimators across two distinct categories: 

• Total uncertainty estimators: These variants yield a biased point estimate for the true entropy. (1) 

Total (plug-in) uses a single PFN pass to obtain a PPD estimate Pn after n observations and plugs it directly into the entropy estimation. (2) Total (bootstrap) resamples the context z1: n to refit the PFN 

K times and averages predictions before calculating entropy. 

• Bias-corrected aleatoric estimators: These variants (including both non-BPI and BPI approaches) mitigate epistemic bias and generate credible intervals. (3) Bootstrap performs the same K refits but computes the entropy within each sample before averaging. (4) Marginal is an ablation using the BPI posterior but restricting it to the marginal rather than the joint distribution. (5) Copula implements the BPI posterior using the copula martingale posterior. (6) CLT implements the BPI posterior using the predictive CLT.

# 7 Conclusion 

Summary. In this work, we showed that naive Bayesian approaches using total predictive uncertainty yield biased estimates for feature acquisition. We introduced a novel Bayesian estimation framework that explicitly mitigates this bias, allowing tabular foundation models to be used for AFA policy evaluation. In synthetic settings, we demonstrate that predictive Bayesian estimators yield highly accurate value estimates and downstream improvements in policy ranking. Further, we evaluate on real-world datasets, confirming that our TabICL-based estimators mitigate epistemic bias while providing reliable uncertainty bounds. 

Discussion and future work. (i) We focus on small- to medium-sized datasets where imbalanced coverage naturally yields high epistemic uncertainty, and where current PFN models can reliably produce calibrated PPD estimates; extension to larger scales is left to future work. (ii) Because our primary goal is studying the biasing effect of epistemic uncertainty on policy value estimation, we rely on a standard inverse probability weighting (IPW) estimator. Integrating our Bayesian PFN-based strategy with advanced semiparametric estimators (e.g., the Double Reinforcement Learning estimator from von Kleist et al. (2025); Melnychuk et al. (2026)) that offer stronger asymptotic guarantees, or relaxing current data-generating assumptions to handle flexible missingness mechanisms and temporality, are natural directions for future research. (iii) While our simulations confirm the theoretical advantages of our framework, our real-world results are constrained by the accuracy of the reference values and the current limitations of TabICL in producing ideal PPDs for aleatoric entropy estimation. We expect future improvements in tabular foundation models to directly translate to reduced estimation error in our framework. 10 References

## B.1 The Bayesian Bootstrap 

The Bayesian Bootstrap (Rubin, 1981) provides a nonparametric posterior over the empirical measure. Specifically, for a dataset of size n, we first draw a vector of probability weights from a uniform Dirichlet distribution, w ∼ Dirichlet(1 , . . . , 1) ∈ ∆n−1, and use it to parameterize a categorical distribution over the data indices. Intuitively, this Dirichlet weighting is directly equivalent to forward sampling within the martingale posterior framework. Simulating the unobserved future data conditional on the empirical support follows a predictive P´ olya urn scheme, and the limit converges exactly to this Dirichlet distribution. By sampling N − n indices with replacement from this distribution, we generate a single forward-sampled realization of the empirical measure.

## C.3 ICU lab acquisition under real-world missingness 

Cohorts. We use a public ICU dataset from PhysioNet. For both, each feature is the mean of a variable over a 48 h window, and it is missing if the variable was never measured in that window. 

• PhysioNet/CinC 2019 (Reyna et al., 2019): We use 27 hourly variables plus age and gender, for 29 features. The records cover the whole ICU stay, so one patient gives up to five overlapping windows (24 j, 24 j + 48] h, j = 0 , . . . , 4. A window is used only if the stay covers it. The label is sepsis onset (the first positive SepsisLabel ) within 24 h after the window ends. A window is dropped, along with all later windows of that patient, once onset has occurred by the end of the window. Cohorts are restricted to an acute population: the criteria are temperature >38 or <36 ◦C, heart rate >100 or <60, respiratory rate >20 or <12, systolic BP <90, MAP <65, and SpO 2 <92%.

On the tabular benchmarks, the bias-corrected estimators (bootstrap and BPI variants) do not yield statistically significant improvements in policy selection over the plug-in estimators (Figure D.7). All es-timators attain similar pairwise accuracy and regret within each task, while the level around which they concentrate varies across tasks. Since the estimators share the same predictive entropy and differ only in the epistemic correction, this pattern suggests that the ranking errors are driven by a component common to all estimators, such as approximation error of the underlying predictive model (TabICL), rather than by the epistemic term that the correction targets. On Adult and California , however, the regret is large and the pairwise accuracy falls below 0.5. Because random estimation errors alone would drive pairwise accuracy toward 0.5 rather than below it, this indicates a systematic error. A plausible mechanism is that better policies (as defined by the reference JN ) reach more specific and hence more sparsely covered final states in the subsampled data, where the negative entropy is underestimated most strongly (i.e. shrinks to the prior entropy), so that the estimation error reverses the ranking.
